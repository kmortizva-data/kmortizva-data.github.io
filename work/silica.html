Silica in an iron flotation plant

Can a model call the concentrate grade before the lab does? I spent ten modules finding out that it cannot, and why.

Feed
737,453 rows and 24 sensors from a real iron ore flotation plant, March to September 2017.
Separation
Trees, forests, gradient boosting, a neural network, and lag features. Then, crucially, a persistence baseline to compare them against.
Concentrate
A verdict, 31 written notes and an 89 page volume.

Assay

the model adds +0.009 R² over doing nothing

Persistence, meaning "assume it stays where it is", scores R² 0.632 at one hour. The trained model scores 0.641. Past five hours both go negative. At twelve hours the model is actively worse than the baseline.

Halfway through, this project looked like a success. Adding hourly memory and gradient boosting took R² from 0.04 to 0.64, which is the kind of jump you put on a slide.

Then I measured what the process gives you for free. Silica does not move much in an hour, so simply predicting that the next value equals the last one already scores 0.632. Almost the entire jump was the inertia of the plant, not the skill of the model. That correction stayed in the notes, because catching it is worth more than the number was.

The part I would actually take to an operations meeting came from clustering, not prediction. K means found three operating states, and one of them dominates March and April and then vanishes. The plant changed how it was run, which explains a drift that had been quietly degrading every model since module two. Monthly error climbs 0.44 in June, 0.65 in August, 0.76 in September. The data cannot tell you tomorrow's grade, but it can tell you the plant is not the plant it was.
