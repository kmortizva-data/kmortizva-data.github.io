Gold is not normal

Strong skewness, the log10 transform, and a bimodality that will turn out to be geology.

Gold is not spread like a bell. Its raw histogram is crushed against the left, with a very long tail to the right, and until it is transformed with log10 it does not show its real shape: two populations, and an anomaly threshold at P95 = 1.57 on the log scale, about 37.5 ppb.

This chapter looks at the data before modelling anything: where the sampling happened, how gold is distributed, what an anomaly is and where they fall. And it ends with the decision to transform only what needs transforming.

Where the sampling happened, and where it did not

The samples do not sit on a regular grid. They follow a northwest to southeast alignment, consistent with the regional geological strike, and they crowd where there was interest: a target-oriented campaign, with potentially relevant areas left without a single sample.

That can be measured. Nearest neighbour analysis asks, for every sample, how far the closest other sample is. The mean came out at 0.22 km, where a random spread over the same area would give 0.75 km. And 73% of the samples have their neighbour within 0.22 km.

Clustered sampling is not a flaw in the data, it is a decision by whoever sampled. But it has a direct consequence for everything that follows: the clusters weigh more than the gaps, and the map will know a lot about a few corridors and almost nothing about the rest.

A toy example before the theory

Picture four samples at 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 ppb of gold. On a raw histogram, three of them land in the first centimetre of the axis and the fourth goes off alone to the far end: the big value dictates the scale. Now take the base 10 logarithm: 0, 1, 2 and 3. Every multiply by ten becomes add one. The four samples sit evenly spaced and the histogram can finally be read.

That is all the log10 transform does. It does not change the data: it changes the rule you look at it with.

Raw gold, and gold in logarithms

Raw, the gold distribution shows strong positive skewness: a great many samples with low values and a few very high ones that hide the structure of the mineralised populations. The crushed histogram, the boxplot with an excess of outliers: not directly interpretable.

Transformed, the histogram reveals a clear bimodality: a background population, the local geochemical baseline, and an enriched population attributable to high-grade mineralisation events.

With that bimodal structure and the inflection in the cumulative curve, the 95th percentile was chosen as the robust threshold to separate anomalies: 1.57 in log10, about 37.5 ppb, which coincides with the break between the main distribution and the upper tail.

The anomalies, on the map

The next step projected those anomalies into space with Voronoi polygons: each sample is assigned the area of the map closer to it than to any other sample, and the polygon is coloured by its grade. That shows whether high values appear isolated or grouped.

The high-grade polygons group into continuous domains, and that geometric continuity is a powerful geostatistical indicator that the mineralisation answers to defined structural controls, possibly vein systems or extensive alteration zones, rather than erratic surface dispersion.

Overlaying the geological map, the anomalies confine themselves to basement units (the pink and greenish tones, likely intrusive complexes or metamorphic sequences), while the sedimentary cover in pale greys is nearly empty of anomalies: a sterile overburden. And against the known rare earth deposits, the most enriched samples cluster around deposits 3, 4 and 7.

Beyond gold: the rest of the chemistry

To validate the metallogenic model, the analysis was extended to the whole geochemical suite with a proximal versus distal enrichment comparison, the so-called pathfinder chart. The heavy rare earths, Ho, Lu, Tb and Dy, come out enriched near the deposits with ratios above 2.0. And an interesting detail: the relative enrichment of gold, copper and zinc in the proximal zone is lower than that of the rare earths; gold sits near 1.0 and copper appears depleted.

The univariate sweep of every element showed the expected dichotomy: major oxides such as Al2O3 split into two humps, and the economic elements carry the positive skewness typical of hydrothermal distributions. Yttrium is bimodal even raw. For cerium and lanthanum the high-grade thresholds landed at 199.90 ppm and 155.00 ppm.

Transform only what needs it

The log10 transform was applied, but selectively: where skewness exceeded 1.5. A one-size-fits-all rule would have been a mistake, because transforming a distribution that is already reasonably normal can induce artificial negative skewness. The ones that came in fine stayed in their original units, ppm or percent, to keep their geological meaning.

What stands established

Clustered sampling (0.22 km against 0.75 expected), gold with strong skewness that turns bimodal in log10, an anomaly threshold at P95 = 1.57 log (~37.5 ppb), anomalies forming continuous domains inside basement units, and transformation only where skewness asks for it. The question opening the next chapter is who gold keeps company with: which elements travel with it and which ones avoid it.
