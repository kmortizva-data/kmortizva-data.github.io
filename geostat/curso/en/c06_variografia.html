Chapter 6 · Gaussian · range 4,470 m · nugget 0.105 · anisotropy 045°

The spatial signature

From the experimental variogram (240 m lag) to the model: maximum continuity NE-SW and mixed geometric-zonal anisotropy.

Two samples 240 metres apart resemble each other more than two samples 5 kilometres apart. Measuring exactly how much, over the 425 samples with gold, is what building a variogram means, and this chapter builds it whole: the 240 m lag, the check against the global variance of 1.81, the model that describes it best (Gaussian: nugget 0.105, sill 0.486, range 4,470 m) and the direction in which gold resembles itself the most, 045°, towards the northeast.

In this chapter: what a variogram measures, how the lag was chosen, the regional trend that the 1.81 line gives away, the duel between three models, the project's own curve drawn from its own numbers, and an anisotropy that turned out to be of two kinds at once.

What a variogram measures

Take every pair of samples separated by a distance h, give or take. For each pair, subtract the two gold values (in log10), square the difference and average over all the pairs; half of that average is the semivariance, γ(h). If γ is small, samples at that distance resemble each other; if it is large, they no longer do. Repeat for many distances and you have a curve, γ against h: the experimental variogram. It is the deposit's spatial signature, and three words describe it:

  • Nugget: the value of γ just as it lifts off from h = 0. Two samples at almost the same spot are not identical, because of sampling and assay error and because of variability at a scale finer than the sampling. That initial step is the nugget.
  • Sill: the value at which γ stops growing. Beyond it, distance carries no more information: two samples that far apart are, for practical purposes, independent.
  • Range: the distance at which the sill is reached. It is a sample's radius of influence, and the number kriging will use to decide whom to listen to and whom to ignore.

The lag: 240 metres

The experimental variogram groups the pairs into distance bins, and the width of that bin, the lag, is the first knob. Three were tried: 120, 240 and 480 m. At 120 m, each bin of the regional variogram averages some 1,481 pairs and the curve fills with noise. At 480 m it averages 5,722 and smooths so much that the rise over the first few kilometres blurs away. 240 m, with some 2,918 pairs per bin, was the balance: fine enough to see the rise, populated enough for every point to be a reliable average.

The previous chapter asked for 50 to 100 pairs per bin as a minimum. Here there are thousands in each: that is what the global model bought. With 20 samples per domain not a single bin would have reached a hundred pairs.

The 1.81 line and what it gives away

The sample variance of gold in log10 is 1.81, and in the figures it appears as a horizontal line, the global sill. If the deposit were stationary, that is, if the mean and the variability were the same everywhere in the terrane, the variogram would climb to that line and stay there. At the regional scale, 25 km, it does something else: it stays below 1.81 over a broad interval and, at large distances, climbs above it. That final upturn is a regional trend: the mean of gold changes with position, and pairs far apart differ more than the global variance predicts.

The answer was to bound the scale. The structure that matters for estimation lives in the first 7 km, and that is where the model is fitted; the background trend is left to the kriging with a local mean in chapter 7, which absorbs it by construction.

The signature, step by step

The three lags under the 1.81 line. Over the first 7 km the three curves tell the same story, and all of them stay far below the global variance: local variability is a fraction of the regional one.

The structure, up close. Flat around 0.2 up to some 2.7 km, a sharp jump towards 3 km and oscillations afterwards: not one structure but several, nested.

Three models compete. Spherical (range 4,825 m, error 0.0129), exponential (effective range 11,217 m, error 0.0137) and Gaussian (error 0.0125). They look much alike; one wins by a hair.

The chosen one. Gaussian with nugget 0.105, sill 0.105 + 0.381 = 0.486 and range 4,470 m. Look where the sill sits: 0.486, against a global variance of 1.81.

The variance rose. Every sector is a direction, every ring a distance. Towards the northeast, 045°, the blue holds; towards the southeast, 135°, red hotspots appear between 2,500 and 3,500 m.

The four directions. The 045° one is the most stable and stays under the model sill; the 135° one climbs steeply, pierces that sill towards 2,800 m and passes 2.0.

The curve, drawn from its three numbers

Reading the curve is reading the deposit. At 1,000 m the model gives γ ≈ 0.16: two samples a kilometre apart still resemble each other closely. At 2,000 m, 0.28; towards 2,150 m the curve passes its midpoint, where half of the climb is behind it. At 3,000 m, 0.39. And at 4,470 m, 0.47: 95 % of the sill, which is exactly what practical range means in a Gaussian model, because the curve approaches the sill without ever touching it. By 6,000 m it is at 0.48 and there is nothing left to gain.

Why the Gaussian

The three models differ in how they start. The spherical climbs in a straight line from the nugget and hits the sill abruptly, at 4,825 m. The exponential also starts straight, but approaches the sill so slowly that its effective range comes out at 11,217 m: it never quite arrives. The Gaussian starts as a parabola, almost flat, and that describes a deposit that is very continuous over short distances. In mean squared error against the experimental points they came out at 0.0129, 0.0137 and 0.0125: the Gaussian won, by a hair.

A Gaussian with a zero nugget is notorious for unstable kriging systems and maps that are too smooth, precisely because of that flat start. Here the nugget of 0.105, 22 % of the sill, keeps it in check. And chapter 8 will show that the three models give kriging errors within some 6 thousandths of each other: the choice mattered less than it seemed.

A sill of 0.486 in a world of 1.81

The model sill is 0.486 and the global variance is 1.81: the model explains little more than a quarter of gold's total variance. That is not a flaw, it is a decision. The model describes the local structure, the one in the first 7 km, and deliberately leaves out the regional trend that the 1.81 line gave away. What kriging inherits from here is a practical range of about 4.5 km: beyond it, a sample says nothing about the point being estimated; within it, the closer, the heavier.

Anisotropy: two kinds at once

Until now the variogram was omnidirectional: pairs were grouped by distance without looking at which way they pointed. But a deposit can be more continuous in one direction than in another, and to see it the calculation is repeated along four azimuths: 000°, 045°, 090° and 135°.

The variance rose shows it at a glance: a corridor of low variance towards the northeast, and hotspots of high variance in the southeast quadrant, between 2,500 and 3,500 m. The directional variograms confirm it. The 045° one is the most stable and stays under the global sill; the 135° one climbs steeply, pierces the sill of the omnidirectional model towards 2,800 m and passes 2.0.

There are two ways of being anisotropic, and both occur here:

  • Geometric: same sill, different range depending on direction. Towards the NE gold resembles itself over a longer distance than towards the SE; the continuity ellipsoid is elongated NE-SW, along the regional strike.
  • Zonal: different sill depending on direction. The 135° one levels off far above the 045° one and the omnidirectional one: towards the SE continuity is not only lost sooner, the total variability is larger as well.

A mixed anisotropy, and a concrete warning: an omnidirectional model underestimates the variability towards the southeast. The kriging in the next chapter estimates with that omnidirectional model, warning in plain sight, and the 4.5 km range is read as a ceiling on correlation, not as a promise: local variability is probably higher than the model says.

The 135° spike also has a humbler reading: towards the southeast there are fewer samples, and a directional variogram with few pairs turns unstable. Part of that climb is geology and part is scarcity of data, and with this file the two cannot be separated.

What is now established

Gold has a spatial signature: a Gaussian with nugget 0.105, sill 0.486 and range 4,470 m, built with a 240 m lag over the first 7 km, and a maximum continuity towards the northeast that the regional geology already announced. Chapter 7 turns that signature into weights: that is kriging, and it gets solved by hand before being handed to the machine.
