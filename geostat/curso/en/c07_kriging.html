Kriging: the best honest estimate

THE REINFORCED CHAPTER. What kriging actually is (a 3-sample system solved by hand with the project's own variogram), SK vs OK without tricks (CV cannot tell them apart), kriging variance and its heavy tails, and the moving-neighbourhood facets explained.

Kriging is a weighted average in which the weights are set not by distance but by the variogram. This chapter proves it by solving a three-sample system by hand with the Gaussian of chapter 6, and then looks at the real maps: simple kriging with all 425 samples at once and ordinary kriging with 10 neighbours, both with a validation error of 0.230 in log10, a factor of 1.70 in ppb. That tie has a catch, and here it is told in full.

In this chapter: the system solved by hand, what changes between simple and ordinary kriging, the maps and their variances, the facets, cross-validation and what it cannot tell apart, and whether the result is good enough.

Kriging by hand, with the project's variogram

Picture a point without a sample, the target, and three samples around it: s1 at 500 m to the east, s2 at 1,200 m to the north and s3 at 3,000 m to the west. Kriging wants to estimate the target as λ1 · z1 + λ2 · z2 + λ3 · z3, and its only question is what the three weights λ are worth. The answer comes from the variogram of chapter 6: nugget 0.105, sill 0.486, range 4,470 m. With it, the semivariance is evaluated between each sample and the target, and between each pair of samples:

  • To the target: s1, 500 m → γ = 0.119 · s2, 1,200 m → 0.179 · s3, 3,000 m → 0.387.
  • Between samples: s1-s2, 1,300 m → 0.190 · s1-s3, 3,500 m → 0.425 · s2-s3, 3,231 m → 0.407.

Those six numbers are all the information kriging uses. With them the ordinary kriging system is set up: one equation per sample, saying that the weighted semivariance between that sample and the others must equal the semivariance between that sample and the target, plus a fourth equation forcing the weights to sum to 1. That condition is what frees the estimate from knowing the mean, and it brings an extra unknown with it, the multiplier μ, which balances the books. Four equations, four unknowns. Solved:

  • λ1 = 0.605 (the one at 500 m) · λ2 = 0.278 (at 1,200 m) · λ3 = 0.116 (at 3,000 m) · μ = 0.017.

Three readings. The closest sample rules, but it does not sweep everything: the nugget of 0.105 says that not even a sample right next to the target is fully trustworthy, so kriging spreads the weight. The sample at 3,000 m, two thirds of the range away, still receives 11.6 %. And compared with inverse distance squared, the split anyone would make without a variogram, the weights would be 0.832 / 0.145 / 0.023: kriging takes weight away from the near sample and gives it to the far ones, because it knows, from the variogram, what nearness is really worth and how much new information each sample brings.

The kriging variance, in the same example

Alongside the weights a second number comes for free: the kriging variance, which here is 0.184 (standard deviation 0.43 in log10). It is computed from the weights, the semivariances to the target and the multiplier, and it never uses the sample values: only the geometry and the model. That is why two targets with the same neighbour configuration have the same variance even if one sits in gold and the other in waste. And that is why the sample at 3,000 m matters: without it, with s1 and s2 alone, the variance would rise to 0.193. A far sample changes the estimate little, but it does reduce the uncertainty.

If the three samples were worth, say, 1.30, 1.00 and 0.40 in log10 (20, 10 and 2.5 ppb), the ordinary estimate would be 0.605 · 1.30 + 0.278 · 1.00 + 0.116 · 0.40 = 1.112. Those three values are made up to illustrate the arithmetic; the weights and the variance are not: they come from the project's real variogram.

Simple against ordinary: the difference is the mean

Simple kriging (SK) assumes the mean of the deposit is known and the same everywhere; here, the mean of the logarithms, 1.054. Its weights need not sum to 1: whatever is missing up to 1 goes to the mean. In the example they come out as 0.585 / 0.261 / 0.087, summing to 0.934, and the remaining 6.6 % pulls the estimate towards 1.054. With the same made-up values, SK would give 1.127. Its variance, 0.182, is almost the same.

Ordinary kriging (OK) does not trust that mean: it re-estimates it in every neighbourhood, hence the condition that the weights sum to 1. Where the local mean differs from the global one, OK adapts and SK pulls towards the centre. In a terrane with a regional trend, like the one the 1.81 line gave away, that difference is the one that matters. In the project, SK was run with a unique neighbourhood, all samples at once, and OK with a moving neighbourhood of 10 samples per point, on the same regular grid.

The maps

Simple kriging, unique neighbourhood. Global mean 1.054 in log10. Two zones rule: a high nucleus to the northeast, above 1.4, and a second nucleus to the southwest between 1.2 and 1.4. Transitions are smooth because every point listens to all 425 samples.

Its variance. From 0.02 to 0.25, and it is a map of the sampling geometry, not of gold: dark blue, below 0.05, where samples cluster in the centre and the northeast; yellow, above 0.20, in corners and edges where the estimate extrapolates.

Ordinary kriging, 10 neighbours. The same zones, with more local detail and sharper edges, and something new: polygonal facets. Every time the set of 10 neighbours changes, the estimate jumps.

Its variance. From 0.03 to 0.35, with maxima 40 % above those of simple kriging: the standard deviation of the error runs from 0.17 to 0.59 in log10. Listening to only 10 neighbours buys sharpness and costs certainty at the edges.

Cross-validation. Each sample, estimated without itself, against its true value: RMSE 0.230. Below 1.0 the cloud follows the diagonal; above it, the estimates fall short. That is the smoothing of every linear kriging.

The standardised errors. Error divided by the kriging standard deviation. More than 95 % fall between minus 2 and 2, with a peak taller than the normal, and a right tail reaching 6: in some places the real error is far larger than promised.

The facets of the moving neighbourhood

The ordinary map is stitched from polygons, and that is not a drawing flaw. With a moving neighbourhood, every grid point is estimated from its 10 nearest samples; moving across the grid, there comes a moment when one sample leaves the set and another enters, and the weights change all at once. That boundary is a facet. Simple kriging with a unique neighbourhood has none because it never changes its set: it is always the 425. Facets show more the fewer neighbours are used, as chapter 8 will show with 5 neighbours, and fade with more.

What the variance says

The kriging variance measures the confidence at each point and, as in the hand-solved example, depends only on where the samples are and on the model. With simple kriging it runs from 0.02 to 0.25; with ordinary, from 0.03 to 0.35. Zones below 0.10 are high-confidence, fit for detailed planning; those above 0.20 call for cautious reading and are the natural priority for infill drilling.

The kriging variance assumes the variogram model is the right one. If the real continuity differs from the model, the real errors drift away from the promised variance: it is an optimistic variance under a misspecified model. And chapter 6 left it in writing that the omnidirectional model underestimates the variability towards the southeast.

Cross-validation, and what it cannot tell apart

Cross-validation removes each sample, estimates it from the rest and compares. Three diagnostics: the mean error, the cloud of true against estimated, and the histogram of standardised errors. The RMSE is 0.230 in log10, which undone from the logarithm is a factor of 1.70: a typical estimate strays from the true value by multiplying or dividing by 1.7. In the low and medium range the cloud follows the diagonal; above 1.0 in log10 the estimates fall short. That is the smoothing inherent to a linear estimator that minimises squared error by regressing the extremes towards the mean, the conditional bias: the high is underestimated and the low is overestimated.

The standardised errors, the error divided by the square root of the kriging variance, should follow a normal with mean 0 and deviation 1. They centre on zero, so there is no bias; but the peak is taller than the normal, most errors are smaller than the variance promises, and at the same time there are tails reaching 6, when beyond 3 the normal assigns less than 0.3 %. That is: for the typical site the variance is somewhat pessimistic, and for a few sites it is very optimistic. More than 95 % fall between minus 2 and 2.

And here is the honest part. The validation of ordinary kriging also returned RMSE 0.230, and its point cloud and its histogram are identical point for point to those of simple kriging: the same positions, the same tails, only the colour changes.

Two methods with different neighbourhoods, one with 425 samples and the other with 10, giving the same error at every single sample is exceedingly rare. Either it happened, or the validation was computed once and shown twice. In both cases the conclusion is the same: cross-validation cannot tell SK from OK, and it is no argument for preferring one.

So, simple or ordinary?

Ordinary, but for the right reasons. Not for the RMSE, which ties. For the assumptions: OK does not demand a constant known mean across 62,000 km² of terrane with a regional trend, and its local mean adapts to it. And for what the map shows: sharper enrichment nuclei to the northeast and the southwest. It pays with more variance at the edges (0.35 against 0.25) and with the facets. The maps in the rest of the analysis are ordinary with 10 neighbours.

Is it good enough?

Yes, with caveats. An RMSE of 0.230 sits within what is acceptable for a preliminary resource estimate, the estimator has no bias, and the high zones coincide with the clusters of the exploratory analysis and with the variogram range. The caveats are three and they are serious: the smoothing underestimates the high grades, which is exactly what a mine wants to know; the histogram's tails say that in some places the error is far larger than promised; and at the poorly sampled edges the estimate extrapolates. For defining exploration targets it serves; for scheduling production or classifying reserves, it does not.

What is now established

Kriging turns the variogram into weights, and the hand-solved example shows how: 0.605 / 0.278 / 0.116 with a variance of 0.184. The real maps give RMSE 0.230 with both methods, the validation cannot tell them apart, and ordinary is preferred for its assumptions and its sharpness.
