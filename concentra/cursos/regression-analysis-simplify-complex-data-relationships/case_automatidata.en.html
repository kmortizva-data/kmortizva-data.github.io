Automatidata

The model that predicts best is the one that cannot be deployed, and measuring that gap turned out to be worth more than the model.

Brief

The brief

The New York City Taxi and Limousine Commission wants the passenger to see an estimated fare before getting into the taxi. Automatidata has to build that estimate with a year's history of trips.

It sounds like a solved problem: a taxi fare depends on distance and time, and that is in the data. The project gets complicated as soon as the brief is read properly.

Question

The question

How much is this trip going to cost, knowing it before it starts?

Those last three words decide the whole project. The distance travelled and the duration are by far the two best predictors of the fare, and neither of the two exists at the moment the price has to be given. A model that uses them predicts almost perfectly and cannot be deployed.

So the variables get separated from the start:

Known at bookingOnly known at the end
Origin and destinationDistance travelled
Hour and day of the weekActual duration
Rate typeTolls, tip, total
History of the route
Data

The data

C2_2017_Yellow_Taxi_Trip_Data.csv, 22,699 trips of New York yellow taxi in 2017, with 17 columns. No duplicates and no empty cells, which is more than can be said of almost any real file.

The variable to predict is fare_amount, the meter fare without tip or tolls. Its median is $9.50 and its mean $12.85.

Cleaning

What had to be fixed

165 trips are physically impossible and get discarded: 20 with a zero or negative fare, which are refunds; 27 that end before they start; and 148 with zero distance. 22,534 remain.

The extremes get clipped, not deleted. Everything past the 99.5th percentile is brought down to it: the fare to $55.67, the duration to 73.18 minutes and the distance to 20.55 miles. That is 113 trips in each variable. Deleting them would have silently changed the population the model claims to describe.

And there is the finding that conditions the model, the same one that appeared in Course 3 and now has an explanation: the 514 trips costing exactly $52.00 are not just any expensive rides. 513 of them carry rate code 2, the airport flat fare, where the price is fixed in advance.

The two panels tell two different pricing rules. With the meter, each mile adds $2.86. With the flat fare, each mile adds $0.00. Without a variable that flags that case, the model is being asked to explain with distance something that distance does not govern.

Model

The model, and why this model

Three were fitted, and which one is the answer is the content of the case:

ModelR² on testRMSECan it be delivered?
A. With the actual distance and duration0.9461$2.41No. They do not exist at booking
B. Route means from the whole dataset0.8847$3.53No. Evaluated with information it will not have
C. Route means from the training set only0.6746$5.93Yes

The legitimate substitute for the actual distance is the mean distance of that route, which is known at booking because it comes from the history. The question is which data that mean is calculated with.

Model B is the shortcut of the official exercise: calculating the per-route means over the complete dataset. That puts information from the test trips into the training. Instead of claiming it is wrong, it was measured:

The leak inflates the R² by 0.2101 and hides $2.40 of error.

A model presented that way looks $2.40 more precise than it is going to be on its first day of operation.

Multicollinearity. With everything in, the actual distance and the route mean give variance inflation factors of 32.17 and 28.68: they measure the same thing. In the delivered model none goes past 5.50.

Model C, which is the one delivered:

tarifa = 2,65
       + 1,87 × distancia media de la ruta (millas)
       + 0,32 × duración media de la ruta (minutos)
       + 0,27 × (1 si es hora punta)
       + 3,30 × (1 si es tarifa plana de aeropuerto)
Assumptions

The assumptions, one by one

AssumptionStatus
LinearityHolds on the metered trips
IndependenceReasonable: different trips, different cars
Normality of the residualsDoes not hold. Skewness 3.49 and kurtosis 18.07
Constant varianceDoes not hold. p of 1.74e-06

The two violations have the same cause, and both structures were verified before being labelled in the figure:

  • The vertical stripe is the routes the model never saw. They all receive the same fallback prediction, $12.68, so their residuals pile up in a line.
  • The perfect diagonal is the flat-fare trips. Their real fare is always 52, so the residual is 52 minus the prediction: an exact line, with a correlation of −1.

With the assumptions broken, the coefficients remain usable but their intervals are somewhat more optimistic than they should be. That gets said, not hidden.

Result

The result, translated

On 5,634 trips the model did not see: R² of 0.6746, RMSE of $5.93 and mean absolute error of $3.21.

But that average number hides two very different populations:

TripsMean error
Route already seen in training5,122 (90.9%)$2.42
Route new to the model512 (9.1%)$11.11

The new route is predicted 4.6 times worse, and it concentrates almost all the error of the set.

Decision

What gets decided with this

  1. Deploy the model for known routes, announcing a margin of about $2.42. It covers 90.9% of the trips.
  2. Fix the new-route case before widening the coverage. No other model is needed: today the system answers $12.68 to any unknown route, which is the city's mean, and estimating the geographic distance between the two zones would already be much better.
  3. Do not promise uniform precision. The error depends on whether the route is known, so the passenger should see a range and not an exact figure.
Limits

Limitations

  • The data is from 2017 and from a single city. Fares change.
  • There is no real-time traffic or weather, which is the most likely cause of the large deviations within the known routes.
  • Two assumptions of linear regression do not hold, so the intervals of the coefficients are somewhat optimistic.
  • Only 53.2% of the trips are predicted with less than $2 of error. The mean of $2.42 describes the set, not each trip.
  • The 9.1% of new routes in this test set depends on how much historical data there is. In a city with less history, that fraction and that error would be larger.
