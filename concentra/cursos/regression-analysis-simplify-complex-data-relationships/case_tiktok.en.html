TikTok

A threshold that decides nothing, because the model sends everyone to one of two piles and there is nobody in between.

Brief

The brief

TikTok has a queue of user claims waiting for human review, and wants to clear it. The final goal, which arrives in the next course, is to classify automatically whether a video contains a claim or an opinion.

This project is the previous step: setting up a complete logistic regression on a binary variable that is already known, verified_status, and learning what can be expected of it.

Question

The question

Can you tell whether an account is verified by looking at the video it posted?
Data

The data

tiktok_dataset_raw.csv, 19,382 videos and 12 columns. Synthetic data created by TikTok for the certificate.

And a warning that goes up front because it decides how everything else is measured:

93.7% of the accounts are not verified. A model that always answers "not verified" is right 93.7% of the time and detects nobody. With this split, accuracy stops being a metric and becomes a trap. It is the example of module 5, now with real data.

Cleaning

What had to be fixed

298 rows have missing data, always in the same block of seven columns, and they get removed. 19,084 remain. There are no duplicates.

The majority class gets undersampled down to match the minority: 1,200 and 1,200, that is 2,400 rows, discarding 16,684 videos. Without that, the shortest path the model has to reduce its error is never to predict "verified".

A consequence to keep in mind when reading the results: the accuracy reported is compared against the 50% of a coin, not against the 93.7% of the original file.

The categories get encoded with one column of ones and zeros per level minus one: is_claim with opinion as the reference, and banned and under_review with the active account as the reference.

Exploration

What the exploration showed

The course material points at the video duration as the interesting variable. In this data the mean duration is 31.77 seconds on verified accounts and 32.47 on unverified ones: seven tenths of a second on half-minute videos.

The signal is somewhere else:

The proportion flips over. Unverified accounts post claims a little over half the time; verified ones post opinions four times out of five.

Model

The model, and why this model

The variable to predict is a yes or a no, so binomial logistic regression applies, fitted by maximum likelihood on 1,800 rows, with 600 held out.

The five interaction counters (views, likes, shares, downloads and comments) are correlated with each other above 0.55, and downloads with comments reaches 0.83. The duration, on the other hand, resembles none of them: 0.013 is the most it reaches, so it measures something different and that is why it stays in the model.

Translated into variance inflation factors, which is the number that decides:

Those of the five counters run from 3.8 to 7.8. None reaches the threshold of 10, so keeping only one was a criterion of our own and not an obligation: they contribute noise and none turned out significant.

Of the five variables in the model, a single one has a demonstrated effect:

The last four intervals cross 1, which is the no-effect line when talking about odds. Posting a claim multiplies by 0.20 the odds of being a verified account.

Assumptions

The assumptions, one by one

AssumptionStatus
Binary outcomeHolds: verified or not
Independent observationsReasonable: different videos
No multicollinearityHolds after removing four counters, maximum 3.0
Linearity in the logitNot checkable in a useful way: the only variable with an effect is binary

The last one deserves a line. The assumption requires the relationship to be straight on the logit scale, not in probability. With a binary variable as the only predictor with an effect, there are only two possible values and a line always passes through two points, so the assumption holds by construction and says nothing.

Result

The result, translated

And here is the finding of the case, which came out of a result that looked like a bug: the thresholds 0.4 and 0.5 gave identical metrics down to the last decimal.

It was not a bug. The probabilities the model predicts run from 0.204 to 0.651 and fall into two piles separated by an empty band: zero cases between 0.28 and 0.56. Cutting at 0.30, at 0.40 or at 0.55 produces exactly the same 387 flagged accounts and the same 248 hits.

The cause is that the model leans on a single binary variable, so it has turned into the rule "is it a claim?" with two steps of arithmetic in between. The threshold, which module 5 presents as a business decision, decides nothing here.

It is the same shape as the Course 3 finding in this same scenario, where the views turned out to be two populations with a gap between them. There it was in the data; here, in the output of the model.

Decision

What gets decided with this

  1. Do not use it as an automatic classifier. An AUC of 0.697 and one in three alarms false do not support a decision without a person in front of it.
  2. Do use the finding. What separates the two groups is the kind of content, not the activity of the account or the duration of the video. That redirects where to look for signal.
  3. Move on to the real problem. The variable that here turned out to be the only one that matters is precisely the one that has to be predicted in the next course, and the complete pipeline is already set up.
Limits

Limitations

  • Balancing the classes discarded 16,684 videos. The model learns from a sample that does not resemble the real split of the platform.
  • The pseudo R² is 0.1046: the model explains little. It is a result, not a failure of the procedure.
  • claim_status is what the next course has to predict. Using it here as a predictor is correct for this exercise, but the two projects are not independent and it is worth remembering when comparing results.
  • The data is synthetic. The conclusions hold as an exercise and do not describe TikTok.
