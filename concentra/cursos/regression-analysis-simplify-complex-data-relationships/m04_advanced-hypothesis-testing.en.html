Advanced hypothesis testing

Two tests for two questions the t-test cannot answer, whether two categorical variables are related, with chi-square, and whether three groups or more have different means, with ANOVA. Both come out exact by hand here, and the post hoc shows why ANOVA alone is not enough.

4 h course time9 videos3 readings4 assignments3 practice
Context

What this module solves

The previous course compared two groups with a t-test. Two very common questions were left out.

The first: are two variables that are categories related? For example, whether the shift and whether a lot passes or does not pass specification have anything to do with each other. There are no means to subtract there, only counts. That is chi-square.

The second: are the means of three groups or more different? And the obvious answer, chaining t-tests two at a time, is wrong for a reason that shows with simple arithmetic. That is ANOVA.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

There are two, one per test, with the numbers chosen so they come out whole.

Example A. Chi-square: does the shift influence passing specification?

A hundred lots, split by shift and by result:

In specificationOutTotal
Day shift401050
Night shift302050
Total7030100

Step 1. What we would expect if the shift made no difference

In total, 70 of 100 lots pass, that is 70%. If the shift had no influence, that 70% should appear in both shifts equally:

día,   en spec:  50 × 0,70 = 35        día,   fuera:  50 × 0,30 = 15
noche, en spec:  50 × 0,70 = 35        noche, fuera:  50 × 0,30 = 15

Those four numbers are the expected ones. The general rule is the same one we just used: row total times column total, divided by the grand total.

Step 2. How far reality departs from that

CellObservedExpectedDifferenceDifference² / expected
Day, in spec4035+525 / 35 = 0.714
Day, out1015−525 / 15 = 1.667
Night, in spec3035−525 / 35 = 0.714
Night, out2015+525 / 15 = 1.667

The four differences are exactly 5, and not by chance: in a two-by-two table, if the totals are fixed, the deviation is necessarily the same in all four cells, with alternating signs.

They are divided by the expected value so that a deviation of 5 weighs more when 15 were expected than when 35 were expected. Adding the last column:

chi² = 0,714 + 1,667 + 0,714 + 1,667 = 4,762

Step 3. Decide

With a two-by-two table there is one degree of freedom: once the totals are fixed, knowing one cell is enough to deduce the other three. With one degree of freedom, the 5% threshold is 3.841, and our 4.762 passes it. The exact p-value is 0.0291.

Conclusion: the shift and passing specification are not independent. The night shift has twice the lots out of spec.

Example B. ANOVA: three shifts, do they have the same mean?

Three recovery measurements in each of three shifts:

Turno A    84   86   88      media 86
Turno B    80   82   84      media 82
Turno C    88   90   92      media 90

The mean of the nine measurements is 86.

Step 1. The variation there is between the groups

How far the group means depart from the overall mean, weighted by the size of each group:

A:  3 × (86 − 86)² = 3 × 0  =  0
B:  3 × (82 − 86)² = 3 × 16 = 48
C:  3 × (90 − 86)² = 3 × 16 = 48

variación entre grupos = 96,  con 2 grados de libertad (3 grupos − 1)
96 / 2 = 48

Step 2. The variation there is within each group

Within each shift, the three measurements deviate from their own mean by −2, 0 and +2:

por grupo:  (−2)² + 0² + (+2)² = 8
los tres:   8 + 8 + 8 = 24,  con 6 grados de libertad (9 datos − 3 grupos)
24 / 6 = 4

Step 3. Compare them, which is the whole idea of ANOVA

F = variación entre grupos / variación dentro de los grupos = 48 / 4 = 12

That ratio is the whole method. If the three shifts were equal, the group means would be separated only by the same chance that separates the measurements within a group, and F would hover around 1. An F of 12 means the groups are twelve times more separated from each other than the internal noise justifies.

The p-value is 0.0080, so the equality of the three means is rejected.

Step 4. What ANOVA does not say

ANOVA says that there is a difference. It does not say where. With three shifts there are three possible pairs, and finding out which one or ones differ is a second step, the post hoc, which is in the code and which in this example gives a very instructive surprise.

Terms

Glossary

  • Chi-square. The test for categorical variables, which works with counts and not with means. It is written χ².
  • Observed frequency. What you actually counted. The 40, 10, 30 and 20 of the table.
  • Expected frequency. What there would be if the null hypothesis were true. The four 35 and 15.
  • Test of independence. The version of chi-square with two categorical variables, which asks whether they are related. Example A.
  • Goodness-of-fit test. The version with a single variable, which asks whether the observed split matches one expected in advance.
  • ANOVA. Analysis of variance. It compares the means of three groups or more.
  • One-way ANOVA. When the groups are defined by a single grouping variable, like the shift.
  • Two-way ANOVA. When there are two grouping variables at once, like shift and ore type.
  • Interaction. That the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. It only appears in the two-way one, and it is usually the interesting finding.
  • F statistic. The ratio of step 3: variation between groups divided by variation within the groups.
  • Post hoc. The pairwise comparisons made after ANOVA comes out significant, to know which groups differ.
  • Family-wise error rate. The total risk of a false positive when several tests are run in a row. It is the reason ANOVA and post hoc adjustments exist.
  • ANCOVA. ANOVA with a numeric control variable put inside, to discount its effect.
  • MANOVA. ANOVA with several dependent variables at once.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Choose which of the two tests

The decision comes from the type of the variables, just as in module 1:

QuestionVariablesTest
Are two categories related?Two categoricalChi-square of independence
Does a split match the expected one?One categoricalChi-square goodness of fit
Do the means of 3 groups or more differ?One categorical, one numericOne-way ANOVA
And with two grouping variables?Two categorical, one numericTwo-way ANOVA

The two variants of chi-square get confused a lot, and the difference is simple: the one of independence compares two columns of your table with each other; goodness of fit compares one column against an expectation you bring from outside, like "the supplier promises a 25/25/25/25 split".

Step 2. Why t-tests are not chained

With three groups there are three pairs: A against B, A against C, B against C. Tempting, and wrong.

Each test at 5% gets it right 95% of the time when there is no real difference. Three independent tests all get it right:

0,95 × 0,95 × 0,95 = 0,857

So the risk that at least one gives a false positive is 1 − 0,857 = 0,143, that is 14%, not 5%. With five groups there are ten pairs and the real risk rises to 40%.

ANOVA solves that by running a single test for the whole question. And when it is necessary to go down to the detail, the post hoc does it with the adjustment that compensates for the multiple comparisons.

Step 3. The assumptions, which exist here too

For ANOVA there are three, and the first two are old acquaintances from the previous course:

AssumptionHow it is checked
Normality within each groupHistogram or quantile plot per group
Similar variances between groupsLevene's test, the same one as in the Automatidata case
Independent observationsBy how the data was collected

For chi-square the assumption is different and very concrete: the expected frequencies have to be sufficient. The usual rule is that no expected cell falls below 5, and with bigger tables, that no more than 20% of the cells do. With small expected values the result stops being reliable and categories have to be merged or Fisher's exact test used.

Mind this: the assumption is checked on the expected values, not on the observed ones. A cell with 2 observed is not a problem if its expected value is 15.

Step 4. After ANOVA, the post hoc

If ANOVA comes out significant, the question becomes which differs from which. The usual test is Tukey's, which compares all the pairs and adjusts the threshold so that the total risk stays at 5%.

That adjustment has a consequence that surprises and that shows in the code: it can happen that ANOVA comes out significant and no specific pair is, or that only one is. It is not a contradiction, it is the price of comparing many things at once honestly.

Step 5. The extensions the syllabus names

Four names worth being able to place, even though the course does not develop them:

NameWhat it addsExample
Two-way ANOVATwo grouping variables, and their interactionShift and ore type at once
ANCOVAA numeric control variable that gets discountedComparing shifts discounting the head grade
MANOVASeveral dependent variables at onceComparing shifts by recovery and concentrate grade
MANCOVAThe two previous things togetherThe above discounting the head grade

Of the four, the one most used at the plant is ANCOVA, and for a recognisable reason: you can almost never compare two shifts cleanly, because they did not process the same ore. ANCOVA is the way of discounting that difference, and it is the same idea of "holding everything else constant" from module 3, applied to a comparison of groups.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Chi-square of independence

The contingency table goes in as is, and scipy calculates the expected values on its own.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

observed = np.array([[40, 10],
                     [30, 20]])

chi2, p_value, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(observed, correction=False)

print(f"esperados =\n{expected}")
print(f"chi2 = {chi2:.4f}   gl = {dof}   p = {p_value:.4f}")

Step 2. Chi-square goodness of fit

The other variant. A supplier claims their ore comes in equal parts from four zones. 100 lots are taken and where each one came from is counted.

counts = np.array([30, 20, 28, 22])
promised = np.array([25, 25, 25, 25])

chi2_fit, p_fit = stats.chisquare(counts, promised)
print(f"chi2 = {chi2_fit:.2f}   p = {p_fit:.4f}")
line by line
stats.chisquare(...)
A different function from the previous one. This one compares one list of counts against an expectation you give it; the one in step 1 compares rows against columns of a table.
promised
The expected values do not come out of the data, they come from outside: the supplier's promise. That is the whole difference between the two tests.
prints
chi2 = 2.72   p = 0.4368

A p of 0.44 gives no reason at all to doubt the supplier: a difference of 30 against 20 between two zones is perfectly normal with 100 lots, and the eye tends to see a pattern there that is not there.

 (2 - 1)      8.000     0.006     2.990    13.010

Read it calmly, because it is the lesson of the module:

  • ANOVA said there is a difference between the shifts, with a p of 0.0080.
  • The only pair that differs is B against C, with a p of 0.006 and an interval that runs from 2.99 to 13.01, without touching zero.
  • A differs from neither of the two. Its p against B and against C is 0.109, and both intervals cross zero.

In other words, the difference ANOVA detected is between the worst shift and the best, and the middle one cannot be told from either. Reporting only "yes, there is a difference between shifts" would have implied that all three are different, which is false.

Step 5. What cannot be run here, and what to do

Two-way ANOVA is not in scipy. The usual tool is statsmodels, which is not installed on this machine, so this notebook does not run it. The honest options are three: install statsmodels when needed, calculate the sums of squares by hand with the same scheme as example B, or stay with the one-way one, which answers most questions.

What is not valid is replacing it with two separate one-way ANOVAs, one per grouping variable: that leaves out precisely the interaction, which is usually the interesting part.

Traps

Watch out

  • Chaining t-tests with three groups inflates the risk from 5% to 14%. With five groups, to 40%. That is what ANOVA is for.
  • ANOVA does not say which groups differ. It only says that some do. The rest is the post hoc, and it can contradict the impression the overall result leaves.
  • There can be a significant ANOVA with no significant pair, or with only one, as in the example. It is not an error, it is the multiple comparisons adjustment doing its job.
  • The chi-square assumption is checked on the expected values, not the observed ones. No expected cell below 5.
  • Chi-square says there is a relationship, not how strong it is. With huge amounts of data, an irrelevant association comes out significant. For the size, other measures are needed.
  • Do not confuse the two variants of chi-square. Independence compares two variables with each other; goodness of fit compares one against an external expectation.
  • A large F does not mean a large effect in real units. It means the separation between groups is large compared with the internal noise, which is something else.
  • Chi-square on counts, never on percentages. Putting percentages in the table changes the result completely, because the test uses the sample size.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

These two tests answer two questions you already ask yourself at the plant, and the second one also spares you an expensive mistake.

Chi-square is the quality control question: of the lots that came out of specification, are they split equally between shifts, or is there one shift that concentrates the failures? Example A says the night shift has twice as many, and that that difference is not chance.

ANOVA is the comparison of three reagents. And here is the mistake it avoids: the natural habit is to test A against B, then B against C, then A against C, and keep the comparison that came out well. With three tests at 5%, the risk that one of them gives a false positive is 14%, so one in every seven test campaigns is going to hand you a winner that does not exist. Changing reagent because of that costs money.

The translation table:

At the plantIn the module
Do the failures concentrate in one shift?Chi-square of independence
Does the ore arrive in the agreed proportion of zones?Chi-square goodness of fit
Do three collectors perform differently?One-way ANOVA
And if the ore type matters as well?Two-way ANOVA
And discounting that each shift had another head grade?ANCOVA
Testing two at a time and keeping whatever comes outThe 14% mistake

And the post hoc has its exact equivalent: it is the difference between saying "the three reagents are not equal" and saying which one to buy. The first does not serve to sign a purchase order.

Review

Review

Why can three groups not be compared with three t-tests?

Because each test carries its own risk of a false positive and the risks accumulate. With three comparisons at 5%, the probability that all three get it right when there is no real difference is 0.95 to the power of 3, that is 0.857, so the risk that at least one gives a false positive is 14% and not 5%. With five groups there are ten pairs and the real risk rises to about 40%. ANOVA answers the question with a single test, and if it is necessary to go down to the detail, the post hoc compares the pairs adjusting the threshold so that the total risk stays as declared.

What exactly does the F statistic compare?

The variation between the groups against the variation within the groups. In the example, the shift means depart from the overall mean enough to produce a between-groups variation of 48 per degree of freedom, while the measurements within each shift depart from their own mean only 4 per degree of freedom. The ratio, 12, says the groups are twelve times more separated than the internal noise justifies. If the groups were really equal, both variations would measure the same thing and F would hover around 1.

ANOVA comes out significant but no pair in the post hoc does. Is there a contradiction?

No. They are different tests with different thresholds: Tukey's post hoc adjusts its own so that the total risk of the three comparisons is 5%, so it demands more evidence from each pair than ANOVA demands from the whole. When the overall evidence sits right at the limit, it can get spread among the pairs without any of them reaching its individual threshold. In the module's example something similar happens in a mild version: ANOVA comes out with a p of 0.0080 and only the pair B against C is significant, while shift A cannot be told from either of the other two.

How do the two chi-square tests differ?

In where the expected frequencies come from. The test of independence works with two categorical variables in a table and calculates the expected values from the table's own totals, to ask whether the two variables are related: it is the case of the shift against passing specification. Goodness of fit works with a single variable and compares the observed counts against an expectation that comes from outside, like the split a supplier promises. The arithmetic afterwards is identical in both: squared difference divided by the expected value, summed over all the cells.

When is ANCOVA used instead of ANOVA?

When there is a numeric variable that affects the result and is not equalised between the groups, and you want to discount its effect before comparing the means. The typical case at the plant is comparing the performance of three shifts that did not process the same ore: if one shift received a higher head grade, part of its better recovery belongs to the ore and not to the shift. ANCOVA puts that variable in as a control and compares the already adjusted means, which is the same idea of "holding everything else constant" from module 3 applied to a comparison of groups.
