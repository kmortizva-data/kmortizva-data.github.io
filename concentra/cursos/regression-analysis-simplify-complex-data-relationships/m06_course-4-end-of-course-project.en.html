Context

What this module solves

The five previous modules taught how to fit a model. This is the delivery, and its job is another one: defending the model in front of someone who is going to make a decision with it.

They are three company scenarios, one for each kind of regression in the course. And all three are done, with their data, their figures and their reports, so this module does not explain how they would be done: it links to what came out.

Note

The arc of the course

The three projects started as three textbook exercises and ended up telling the same story from three different places. In all three, the result that gets published is not the one that comes out of the first fit, and in all three the reason is different.

Automatidata: the model that predicts best is the one that cannot be delivered

The distance travelled explains the fare almost perfectly, and it does not exist when the price has to be given. Replacing it with the mean distance of the route is correct, but calculating that mean over all the data puts information from the test set into the training.

The difference was not claimed, it was measured: 0.2101 of R² and $2.40 of hidden error. And the honest model locates where it fails, which is what makes it actionable: $2.42 of error on known routes against $11.11 on new routes.

TikTok: a threshold that decides nothing

With the classes at 93.7 against 6.3, accuracy was a trap before starting. But the finding appeared afterwards, on seeing that the thresholds 0.4 and 0.5 gave identical metrics.

The predicted probabilities fall in two piles with an empty band in the middle: zero cases between 0.28 and 0.56. The model rests on a single binary variable, so it has turned into the rule "is it a claim?" with two steps of arithmetic in between.

Waze: the model was fine, the threshold was not

It gets 82.3% right and detects 8.2% of those who leave, which is almost the same as doing nothing would achieve. And yet its AUC is 0.737: it does rank people by risk.

Lowering the cut to 0.20 it detects 62.3%. There is the lesson of modules 5 and 6 together: a badly calibrated model and a bad model look very much alike in the metrics table, and they are only told apart by looking at the AUC and moving the threshold.

And a variable that disappeared

In Waze, professional drivers churned at 7.56% against 19.88%. Inside the model, with the activity days present, their effect is 0.993 with a p of 0.952: nothing.

It is the paradox of module 3 in its mild version. The sign did not change, but an apparently solid finding evaporated on controlling for what was already being measured.

Terms

Glossary

  • PACE strategy document. The written deliverable of the project: what was planned, what was analysed, what was built and what is recommended.
  • Executive summary. One page for whoever is not going to read the analysis. Finding, number with units, decision and limits.
  • Information leak. When the model is evaluated using something it will not have in production. It inflates the result without any warning going off.
  • Available at decision time. The filter every variable should pass before entering a model: will it exist when the prediction has to be made?
  • Calibration. Whether the probabilities the model gives correspond to the real frequencies. A model can rank well and be badly calibrated.
  • Classification threshold. The cut that turns a probability into a decision. It belongs to the business, not to the model, except when it changes nothing.
  • Deployable model. The one that can actually be put to work, which is not always the one that scores best.
Method

Step by step

What has to be done to deliver each project, in the order it was done.

Step 1. Read the brief looking for the hidden constraint

In Automatidata the constraint was in four words, "before it starts", and it decided which variables could go in. Before looking at the data, you have to know what will be known at decision time.

Step 2. Look at the split of the variable to predict

It is the first thing, and in two of the three projects it changed how everything is measured. TikTok with 93.7% of one class and Waze with 82.3%: in both, accuracy becomes useless before fitting anything.

Step 3. Clean saying every decision out loud

The three that appeared, and none is cosmetic:

  • 165 impossible trips in Automatidata, and the extremes clipped instead of deleted.
  • 298 incomplete rows in TikTok.
  • 700 missing labels in Waze, compared against the rest before excluding them, and 983 divisions by zero that would have put infinities into the fit.

Step 4. Check multicollinearity before reading a single coefficient

In Waze, sessions and drives gave inflation factors of 159. With that inside, no coefficient means what it seems to.

Step 5. Fit, and then check the assumptions

Those of the linear and those of the logistic are not the same, and dragging one set into the other is the classic mistake. In Automatidata two of the four failed, and it was said.

Step 6. Translate the coefficient into a unit someone can picture

In Waze, per single unit all the odds ratios came out 1.000 with an interval from 1.000 to 1.000, with p-values of 3e-48. The effect was real and the unit was absurd. Per year of tenure, the ratio is 0.864 and it explains itself.

Step 7. Write the executive summary starting with the limitation

All three summaries lead with what the model cannot do, not with its R². It is not modesty: it is that the business decision depends on that.

Traps

Watch out

  • A high R² can be an information leak, not an achievement. If the model is evaluated with something it will not have in production, the number is inflated and nothing warns.
  • Always ask yourself whether the variable will exist at decision time. It is the filter that discarded the two best variables of Automatidata.
  • With unbalanced classes, accuracy measures nothing. In Waze, the model's 82.3% is the 82.26% of doing nothing.
  • A badly calibrated model looks like a bad model. Look at the AUC before discarding it, and try moving the threshold.
  • The threshold belongs to the business, except when it changes nothing. Check it by looking at the split of the predicted probabilities, not by taking it for granted.
  • A strong raw predictor can evaporate inside the model. Waze's professional driver went from looking decisive to a p of 0.952.
  • An odds ratio of 1.000 does not mean there is no effect. It can mean the unit is too small. Look at the p-value and scale the variable.
  • The assumptions of the linear are not those of the logistic. Normality of residuals and constant variance do not apply to the second.

Metallurgical bridge

These three reports are the campaign report you already know how to write, in other clothes.

When you close a flotation test campaign, you do not deliver the best result you got: you deliver the one that can be sustained at the plant. And the difference between the two is exactly what separates the three delivered models from the three that scored best.

Review

Review

What is an information leak and how is it detected before publishing a result?

It is using for training or for evaluating something the model will not have available when it has to predict for real. In Automatidata it was produced by the per-route means calculated over the whole set, which carried information from the test trips into the training and inflated the R² by 0.2101. It is detected with a question that has to be asked variable by variable: will this value exist at the moment the decision has to be made? And it is quantified by fitting the model both ways and comparing, which is more convincing than claiming that one is wrong.

A model has 82% accuracy and 8% recall. Is it a bad model?

Not necessarily, and confusing the two things is expensive. That 82% is what you would get by always answering the majority class, so accuracy contributes nothing. But the Waze model with those figures has an AUC of 0.737, which means it does rank users by risk: what was wrong was the threshold. Lowering it from 0.50 to 0.20 the recall goes to 62.3%, in exchange for two out of every three alerts being false. Before discarding a classifier you have to look at the AUC and try moving the cut.

Why was the classification threshold no use at all in TikTok?

Because the probabilities the model predicts are not spread over a continuous fan, but fall into two groups, one around 0.25 and another around 0.64, with no case between 0.28 and 0.56. Any cut inside that empty band flags exactly the same accounts. The cause is that the model leans on a single binary variable, so in practice it has turned into a yes-or-no rule. The way to check it is to draw the split of the predicted probabilities instead of taking for granted that the threshold is a useful knob.

In Waze, professional drivers churn at 7.56% against 19.88%, and the model says being professional has no influence. How can that be?

Because the two sentences answer different questions. The first compares two groups without controlling for anything else, and the second says how much being professional contributes holding the other variables of the model constant. A professional driver is one because they use the app a lot, and the activity days, which are already inside the model, capture that whole effect. When that variable is present, being professional adds no information of its own, and its odds ratio drops to 0.993 with a p of 0.952. It is the same mechanism as the Simpson's paradox of module 3, here without getting as far as changing the sign.

Why do the three executive summaries start with what the model cannot do?

Because it is what changes the decision of whoever reads them. A manager who only sees "R² of 0.67" does not know whether they can deploy the model; one who reads "it gets within $2.42 on 90.9% of routes and misses by 11.11 on the rest" already knows what to do and what to fix first. Besides, it protects the analysis: the limitations one declares are part of the result, and the ones someone else discovers later are a credibility problem. Reporting the pretty number and hoping nobody looks is exactly the mistake this course teaches not to make.
