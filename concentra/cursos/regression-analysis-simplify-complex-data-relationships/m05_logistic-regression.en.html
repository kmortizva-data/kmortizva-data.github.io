Logistic regression

When what you want to predict is not a number but a yes or a no. The ladder from probability to odds to logit, done with three round numbers; why the coefficient is not a probability; and the four metrics of a classification, with the case where getting 70% right is a bad result.

4 h course time8 videos4 readings5 assignments3 practice
Context

What this module solves

Everything before predicted a number: a recovery, a fare. Many real questions are not like that:

  • Does this lot pass or not pass the arsenic specification?
  • Does this user leave or stay?
  • Is this video a claim or is it not?

The answer is a yes or a no, and what is useful is not guessing which, but the probability that it is yes. There the line of module 2 is no use, for a reason that is easy to see: a line has no brake. It keeps rising and ends up predicting a probability of 1.4, which does not exist.

This module is the fix: an S-shaped curve that flattens against 0 and against 1 without ever overshooting.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Step 1. From probability to odds

Odds are another way of saying the same thing as a probability, and it is the one used in betting: how many times it happens against how many times it does not.

momios = probabilidad / (1 − probabilidad)
ProbabilityOddsReads as
0.80.8 / 0.2 = 4Four times yes for every one no
0.50.5 / 0.5 = 1One and one: the same
0.20.2 / 0.8 = 0.25One yes for every four no

Notice what just happened with the scale. Probability lives squeezed between 0 and 1. Odds run from 0 to infinity. The ceiling has been let go, but not the floor, and besides the scale is ugly: twice as probable is not twice the odds.

Step 2. From odds to logit

The second step is taking the natural logarithm of the odds. That is the logit:

ProbabilityOddsLogit
0.84ln 4 = +1.3863
0.51ln 1 = 0
0.20.25ln 0.25 = −1.3863

And look at the right-hand column: +1.3863 and −1.3863, symmetric around zero. That is what we have gained. The logit runs from minus infinity to plus infinity, it is centred on 0.5, and it treats 0.8 and 0.2 as equally far away.

That is the whole idea of logistic regression: since the logit is a scale with no ends, an ordinary line can be fitted to it. The model predicts the logit with a line, and then the path is undone to get back to probability.

Step 3. The way back

Undoing the two steps gives the formula that appears in every book:

probabilidad = 1 / (1 + e^(−logit))

Check it with the 1.3863: e^(−1,3863) = 0,25, so 1 / 1,25 = 0,8. Back to the starting point. That function is the sigmoid, and it is the S-shaped curve that never goes above 1 or below 0.

Step 4. A complete model with ninety lots

Ninety lots of two ore types, with their specification result:

OrePassDo not passTotalOdds of passing
Easy40105040/10 = 4
Hard20204020/20 = 1

The model has a single variable, "is it hard?", which is 1 or 0. And its two coefficients come straight from the table, without fitting anything:

intercepto  = ln(momios del mineral fácil) = ln 4 = +1,3863
coeficiente = ln(momios duro / momios fácil) = ln(1/4) = −1,3863

The model is: logit = 1.3863 − 1.3863 × (hard). And it reads like this:

  • With easy ore, duro is 0: logit 1.3863, that is probability 0.8.
  • With hard ore, duro is 1: logit 0, that is probability 0.5.

Which is exactly what the table says: 40 out of 50 and 20 out of 40.

Step 5. What that −1.3863 means

It is not a probability and it is not a difference in probability. It is on the logit scale, and to read it you have to exponentiate it:

e^(−1,3863) = 0,25

That 0.25 is the odds ratio: the hard ore has a quarter of the odds of passing that the easy one has. Said the other way round, which is usually understood better: the easy one has four times the odds of the hard one.

And mind the translation, because it is where everyone slips: the probability does not get divided by four. It goes from 0.8 to 0.5, which is a drop of 0.3, not of 0.6. Odds get multiplied by a constant factor; probabilities do not.

Terms

Glossary

  • Logistic regression. The model for predicting a yes or a no. It returns a probability, not a label.
  • Binomial logistic. The version with two possible outcomes, which is the one of this course. With more than two it is called multinomial.
  • Odds. Probability divided by its complement.
  • Logit. The natural logarithm of the odds. It is what the model predicts with a line.
  • Sigmoid. The S-shaped curve that turns a logit into a probability.
  • Odds ratio. The exponentiated coefficient. By how many times the odds get multiplied for each unit of the variable.
  • Maximum likelihood. The method this model is fitted with. Instead of minimising squared residuals, it looks for the coefficients that make **the data you actually observed most probable**.
  • Threshold. The probability from which a yes is declared. By default 0.5, and it should almost never stay there without thinking about it.
  • Confusion matrix. The table of hits and misses of a classification, with its four cells.
  • Accuracy. Total hits over the total.
  • Precision. Of everything you flagged as yes, how much really was.
  • Recall. Of all the yeses there were, how many you caught. Also called sensitivity.
  • F1. A single number that combines precision and recall, punishing either of the two being bad.
  • ROC curve and AUC. A summary of the model's behaviour at all thresholds at once, not only at 0.5.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Check that the question is binary

If Y has two possible values, logistic. If it is a number, linear. Module 1 already settled this, and the only new trap is the opposite one: do not turn a number into a yes or a no without a reason. Cutting the recovery into "good" and "bad" by an invented threshold throws away information you already had.

Step 2. The assumptions, which are different

The linear ones do not hold here as they are. The logistic ones are four:

AssumptionWhat it requires
Binary outcomeY has exactly two categories
Independent observationsAs always
No multicollinearityAs in module 3
Linearity in the logitThe straight relationship is required with the logit, not with the probability

The fourth is the one that changes and the most misread. It does not ask the probability to rise in a straight line, because in fact it never does: the sigmoid is flat at the ends and steep in the middle. What has to be straight is the logit.

And notice what is not in the list: normality of the residuals and constant variance are not required. Those two belonged to linear regression.

Step 3. Fit by maximum likelihood

Here there is no closed formula like the 400 divided by 1000 of module 2. The computer tries combinations of coefficients and keeps the one that makes the observed data most probable. It is called maximum likelihood.

The idea, without mathematics: for given coefficients, the model assigns each lot a probability of what actually happened to it. If it gives a lot that passed a 0.9, good; if it gives it a 0.1, bad. All those probabilities get multiplied and the maximum of that product is sought.

Step 4. Interpret the coefficient, which is where it goes wrong

Three sentences, in order from least to most useful:

  1. "The coefficient is −1.3863." It says nothing to anyone.
  2. "The odds ratio is 0.25." Correct and still opaque.
  3. "With hard ore, the odds of passing specification are a quarter." That is the one that goes to the report.

And the quick-reading rule for the sign: positive coefficient, odds ratio greater than 1, the variable raises the probability. Negative, ratio less than 1, it lowers it. A coefficient of 0 gives an odds ratio of 1, which means "this variable changes nothing".

Step 5. Choose the threshold, which is a business decision

The model delivers a probability. Turning it into a yes or a no requires a cut, and the default 0.5 is almost never the right one:

If what is expensive is...ThresholdConsequence
An undetected failureLower, 0.3More alarms, fewer escapes
A false alarmHigher, 0.7Fewer alarms, more escapes

In quality control, letting a lot out of specification through usually costs much more than checking one good lot too many. That calls for lowering the threshold, and it is a decision of the business, not of the model.

Step 6. Evaluate with the four metrics

Accuracy alone misleads, and it misleads exactly when it matters most. The classic case: if only 2% of the lots fail, a model that always says "passes" is right 98% of the time and does not detect a single failure. That is why four numbers are needed and not one:

MetricQuestion it answers
AccuracyWhat proportion of the total did I get right?
PrecisionOf what I flagged as positive, how much was?
RecallOf all the positives there were, how many did I catch?
F1Are the two above reasonably good at the same time?

Precision and recall are bought one with the other by moving the threshold, and that is why they are reported together. And to see the model at all thresholds at once there is the ROC curve, whose area under the curve, the AUC, runs from 0.5 (a coin) to 1 (perfect).

Step 7. Predicting with the different kinds of regression of the course

With this module the repertoire closes, and it is worth seeing it together, because prediction means a different thing in each kind:

Kind of regressionWhat it predictsOutputHow it is evaluated
Simple linearA number, from one variable79.3% recoveryR², RMSE
Multiple linearA number, from several79.3% recoveryAdjusted R², RMSE
Binomial logisticThe probability of a yes0.80 of passing specPrecision, recall, F1, AUC

Three differences worth having clear when choosing:

What comes out the other end is not the same. The linear ones return a quantity in the units of Y; the logistic returns a probability, and one more threshold is needed to turn it into a decision.

The error is measured with different rules. In the linear ones the error is a distance and is summed up with the RMSE. In the logistic there is no distance: either you got the category right or you did not, and that is why four metrics are needed instead of one.

The assumptions are not inherited. Normality of the residuals and constant variance belong to the linear ones. The logistic asks for linearity in the logit, and does not ask for the other two.

What is common to all three, and is the thread of the whole course: a model gets fitted, its coefficients get interpreted with their units and holding everything else constant, its assumptions get checked, and it is said how far its validity reaches.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Fit the model by maximum likelihood

On this machine there is no scikit-learn or statsmodels, so the model gets fitted with scipy by hand. It is not a bodge: it is the chance to see from the inside what those libraries do in one line.

import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize

# 90 lotes: 50 de mineral facil (40 pasaron), 40 de mineral duro (20 pasaron).
hard = np.concatenate([np.zeros(50), np.ones(40)])
passed = np.concatenate([np.ones(40), np.zeros(10), np.ones(20), np.zeros(20)])


def negative_log_likelihood(params):
    """Return how badly these two coefficients explain the 90 observed lots."""
    intercept, coefficient = params
    logit = intercept + coefficient * hard
    probability = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))
    return -np.sum(passed * np.log(probability) + (1 - passed) * np.log(1 - probability))


best = optimize.minimize(negative_log_likelihood, x0=[0.0, 0.0])
intercept, coefficient = best.x

print(f"intercepto      = {intercept:+.4f}")
print(f"mineral duro    = {coefficient:+.4f}")
print(f"razon de momios = {np.exp(coefficient):.2f}")

Step 3. The four metrics, from the confusion matrix

A quality control model applied to 100 lots, where the positive is the lot out of specification, which is what you want to catch:

The model says outThe model says in spec
Was out40 hits20 escapes
Was in spec10 false alarms30 hits
true_positive, false_positive = 40, 10
false_negative, true_negative = 20, 30
total = true_positive + false_positive + false_negative + true_negative

accuracy = (true_positive + true_negative) / total
precision = true_positive / (true_positive + false_positive)
recall = true_positive / (true_positive + false_negative)
f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall)

print(f"exactitud    = {accuracy:.3f}")
print(f"precision    = {precision:.3f}")
print(f"sensibilidad = {recall:.3f}")
print(f"F1           = {f1:.3f}")

Two assignments in one line. The long names are worth it here: tp and fp get confused three weeks later.
precision = tp / (tp + fp)
The denominator is everything the model flagged as positive, rightly or not. It answers "when I raise the alarm, how much do I believe myself?".
recall = tp / (tp + fn)
The denominator is everything that was truly positive. It answers "of the bad lots there were, how many did I catch?". It changes only one letter from the previous one and means something completely different.
2 * p * r / (p + r)
The harmonic mean of the two. This one is used and not the ordinary average because it punishes either of the two being bad: with precision 1 and recall 0, the average would give 0.5 and the F1 gives 0.

And here is the result you have to know how to read. An accuracy of 70% sounds acceptable and the model is bad for its purpose: the recall of 0.667 means that a third of the lots out of specification escape it. In quality control that is unacceptable, and the fix is not to change the model but to lower the threshold, accepting more false alarms in exchange for fewer escapes.

Traps

Watch out

  • The coefficient is not a probability. It is on the logit scale and has to be exponentiated to be read as an odds ratio.
  • The odds ratio is not a ratio of probabilities. Odds four times smaller take the probability from 0.8 to 0.5, not to 0.2.
  • Never report a logit. It means nothing to whoever receives the report. The probability or the odds ratio is reported.
  • Accuracy misleads with unbalanced classes. With 2% failures, always saying "no failure" is right 98% of the time. Always look at the recall.
  • The 0.5 threshold is a convention, not a result. Choosing it is deciding which error you prefer to make, and that is decided by the business.
  • Linearity is required in the logit, not in the probability. The probability never rises in a straight line; that is what the S is for.
  • Normality of residuals and constant variance do not apply here. They are assumptions of linear regression, and dragging them into the logistic one is a frequent mistake.
  • Do not turn a number into a binary without a reason. If you have the measured recovery, using "good or bad" throws away information you had already paid to measure.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The whole plant runs on binary decisions, and none of them is solved with a line.

The clearest is the one we have been using as an example: does this lot pass the arsenic specification? You do not ask how much arsenic the model predicts, you ask whether the shipment is going to be rejected. And what you want from the model is not a yes or a no but the probability, because with a 55% chance of passing you send the lot to blending and with 95% you ship it.

Plant questionPositive
Does the shipment pass the arsenic specification?Does not pass
Is the cell going to overflow in the next shift?Overflows
Does this pump fail before scheduled maintenance?Fails
Is the core of this drill hole economic ore?It is

And there is an idea you already handle that is exactly the odds ratio: odds are the language of betting and of risk. "Four to one" is a sentence understood in a control room, and it is literally what the intercept of the example says.

The threshold also has its equivalent, and it is the most recognisable decision of all: where you set the alarm. An alarm too sensitive ends up ignored by the operator because it always goes off; one too permissive lets the problem through. Choosing that point is not a statistical problem, it is knowing how much each of the two errors costs. In quality control the answer is almost always the same: better a false alarm than a rejected shipment.

Review

Review

Why can a linear regression not be used to predict a yes or a no?

Because a line has no limits and a probability does. Coding yes as 1 and no as 0, the fitted line will keep rising indefinitely and will predict values above 1 or below 0, which do not exist as probabilities. Besides, it would assume that each unit of X changes the probability by the same amount across the whole range, when in practice the effect is small at the ends and large in the centre. The logistic solves both things by modelling the logit, which is a scale with no ends, and returning the probability through an S-shaped curve that flattens against 0 and 1.

A logistic coefficient is 0.7. What does it mean?

That for each unit that variable rises, the odds of yes get multiplied by e raised to 0.7, that is by 2, holding the other variables constant. It does not mean that the probability rises 0.7, nor that the probability doubles: what doubles is the odds. The effect on the probability depends on where you start from, and it is larger near 0.5 than at the ends. The correct way to report it is in odds or by translating into concrete probabilities of cases of interest.

What is the difference between precision and recall, and when does each matter?

Both are calculated with the same positive hits on top and change the denominator. Precision divides by everything the model flagged as positive, and answers how much an alarm can be trusted. Recall divides by all the positives that truly existed, and answers how many real cases were detected. Recall matters more when an undetected case is expensive, like a lot out of specification reaching the customer, and precision more when a false alarm is expensive, like stopping a line. They are bought one with the other by moving the threshold, and that is why they are reported together or summed up in the F1.

A model has an accuracy of 98%. Is it good?

There is no knowing without the class split. If only 2% of the cases are positive, a model that always answers "negative" reaches exactly that 98% without detecting a single real case, and its recall is zero. Accuracy is the metric that misleads most precisely in the problems where the positive is rare, which tend to be the important ones: failures, fraud, rejections. You have to look at the whole confusion matrix and report precision, recall and F1.

What is chosen when the classification threshold is set, and why is 0.5 not the default answer?

What is chosen is which kind of error you prefer to make. Lowering the threshold flags more cases as positive, so recall rises and precision falls: more alarms, fewer escapes. Raising it does the opposite. The 0.5 is only a convention inherited from assuming that the two errors cost the same and that the two classes are equally frequent, and both things are almost never true. In quality control, letting a defective lot through costs quite a bit more than checking a good one, so the right threshold is below 0.5, and that is a decision of the business and not of the model.
