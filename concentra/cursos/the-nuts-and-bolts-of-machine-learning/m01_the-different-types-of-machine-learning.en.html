The different types of machine learning

Six lots of ore, a single grade column, and two completely different ways of working them depending on whether the column you want to predict exists or not. Here the map of the course gets fixed, you see why a model inherits whatever past you give it, and the Python toolbox gets set up.

5 h course time16 videos7 readings7 assignments4 interactive
Context

What this module solves

The previous course ended with models that explain: a coefficient, its interval, its p-value. You could write "for every gram per tonne of collector the recovery rises 0.4 points" and defend it.

This course changes what is asked of the model. Here what matters is getting it right on cases it has not seen, even if the reason cannot be written as a formula. It is a real trade: you gain accuracy and you lose explanation.

This module draws the map before touching anything. It fixes three things: what separates the two big types of learning, which obligations you bring with you when building a model that decides about people, and which Python tools get used from here on.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Six lots of ore, and of each one the gold grade in grams per tonne is known:

lote        1    2    3    4     5     6
ley (g/t)   2    4    6    20    22    24

One glance is enough to see that there are two families: three poor lots and three rich ones, with a huge gap in between. We are going to work these same six numbers in the two ways that exist, and the difference between them is not in the algorithm: it is in what information you have.

Case A. Someone already marked which ones were profitable

Suppose the plant processed all six and noted the result:

LotGrade (g/t)Was it profitable?
12no
24no
36no
420yes
522yes
624yes

Here the column you want to predict exists, and that changes everything. The task is to find a rule that, looking only at the grade, reproduces that last column. And the rule is obvious:

si la ley pasa de 13  ->  rentable
si no                 ->  no rentable

With that rule you get six out of six. This is supervised learning: there is a supervisor, which is the labels someone put in, and the model learns to imitate it.

Notice a detail that looks minor and is not: 13 is nothing special. Any cut between 6 and 20 gets it equally right, and with this data there is no way of choosing. The 13 comes from splitting the difference. With six lots, the model has a lot of freedom and that is exactly what makes it fragile in front of lot number seven.

Case B. Nobody marked anything

Now cover the last column. Only six numbers remain: 2, 4, 6, 20, 22, 24. There is nothing to imitate, so the question changes: it is no longer "what is the rule?" but "are there groups in here?".

A clustering algorithm would do this, and it can be followed by hand. It starts by placing two centres anywhere, say at 2 and at 4, and repeats two steps:

Round 1. Each lot signs up with the nearest centre. The 2 is glued to the first centre; the other five fall closer to the 4. The centres move to the average of their own:

centro 1 = 2
centro 2 = (4 + 6 + 20 + 22 + 24) / 5 = 76 / 5 = 15,2

Round 2. Everything is handed out again with the new centres. Now the 2, the 4 and the 6 are closer to 2 than to 15.2, and the 20, 22 and 24 the other way round:

centro 1 = (2 + 4 + 6) / 3   = 12 / 3 = 4
centro 2 = (20 + 22 + 24) / 3 = 66 / 3 = 22

Round 3. Everything is handed out with the centres 4 and 22, and nobody changes group. When that happens, it is finished.

It found the same division as the hidden label, without ever having seen it. That is unsupervised learning.

And now the important part

The two cases split the same six lots at the same place. But the second one does not know what it has found. Its groups are called 0 and 1 because they have to be called something: it can tell you there are two families and which is which, but not that one of them is the profitable one. That word was not in the data.

SupervisedUnsupervised
Does the column to predict exist?YesNo
What it gives you backAn answer with a nameNumbered groups
How you know if it got it rightBy comparing with the truthThere is no truth to compare with
What you ask of the dataThat someone has labelled itOnly that it exists

That third row is the hardest one. In case A you can count hits. In case B there is nothing to count, and that is why module 3 spends half a class inventing ways of judging a result without a correct answer.

Terms

Glossary

  • Machine learning. Building a model that learns rules from examples, instead of you writing them one by one.
  • Supervised learning. Case A: the column you want to predict exists, and the model learns to reproduce it. The "supervisor" is those labels.
  • Unsupervised learning. Case B: that column does not exist, and what is sought is structure within the data. Grouping is its typical task.
  • Label. The column you want to predict, the correct answer. Also called the target variable.
  • Feature. Each column the model uses to predict. You will see that word everywhere, also translated as characteristic or attribute.
  • Continuous variable. One that can take any value in a range: a grade, a fare, a time. Between any two values another always fits, so the possible values are infinite.
  • Categorical variable. One that takes labels from a closed list: passes or does not pass, day or night shift. The possible values can be counted on your fingers.
  • Classification. Predicting a categorical label. Case A is classification.
  • Regression. Predicting a continuous number. That is the previous course.
  • Clustering. Splitting the data into groups that resemble each other without anyone saying what they are.
  • Recommendation system. The model that suggests what might interest you from what people like you have done, or from what resembles what you already chose. It is the most visible application of machine learning and it almost always mixes the two types.
  • Bias. Here it does not mean what it did in the statistics course. It means that **the model systematically treats one group of people worse**, usually because the historical data already did.
  • Fairness. That the model's performance does not depend on which group you belong to. It is not a single number: there are several definitions and **they are incompatible with each other**, so you have to choose which one you care about and say so.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Ask whether the column you want to predict exists

It is the first question and it decides everything else. Not "which algorithm do I use?", but "has someone already marked the answer in my data?".

If the answer is yes, you are in supervised learning and you can measure whether you get it right. If it is no, you have two ways out: getting those labels, which usually costs money and an expert's time, or changing the question to one that does not need them.

Step 2. If there is a label, look at what type it is

You already know how to do this from the previous course, and the rule has not changed: the variable you want to predict is in charge, not the ones you use to predict.

What you want to predictThe task is calledExample from this project
A numberRegressionThe fare of a trip
One of two labelsBinary classificationWhether a user churns
One of several labelsMulticlass classificationWhich type of ore it is

Step 3. Look at what is inside the variables

Here a distinction appears that the course treats under its own name. A continuous variable has infinite possible values: between 20 and 21 grams per tonne fit 20.5 and 20.53 and 20.531. A categorical one has a closed list.

It matters for a practical reason: almost no model understands text. The column that says "iPhone" or "Android" has to be turned into numbers first, and how it is turned changes the result. That is module 2.

Step 4. Ask whom the model affects, before building it

This step is not a moral appendix: it is part of the work, and the course devotes a whole block to it.

A model learns from what happened, so it repeats what happened. If the historical data records a decision that treated one group worse, the model learns it and applies it faster, to more people and with the appearance of objectivity, which is the worst of all.

Three questions before deploying any model that decides about people:

  1. Whose is this data, and who is not in it? A model trained on whoever was already a customer knows nothing about whoever could never be one.
  2. What does each kind of error cost, and whom does it cost? Confusing a defaulter with a solvent customer costs the bank. The other way round costs a person.
  3. Can the affected person know why that was decided? A forest of three hundred trees gets it right more often than a regression and answers this question much worse. It is a real trade and there are contexts where it cannot be accepted.

Step 6. The resources for solving programming doubts

The course devotes a whole block to this, and rightly so: nobody knows scikit-learn by heart, and looking up an answer is not a defeat but part of the trade. What distinguishes whoever gets ahead is the order in which they look.

  1. Read the whole error message, starting from the bottom. Python writes the complete path, and the last line is the one that says what happened. `ValueError: Expected 2D array, got 1D array instead` is literally the double-bracket error of step 3, and the message itself suggests the solution.
  2. The official documentation of the library. scikit-learn's brings, for each model, what each knob does and a short example that runs as is. It is faster than searching the internet and it is not out of date.
  3. Search for the error message, not for your problem. Pasting the exact text of the error finds the hundred people who had it before. Describing it in your own words does not.
  4. Stack Overflow, looking at the date. An answer from 2015 about pandas may use functions that no longer exist. Always cross-check with the documentation.
  5. If you ask, prepare a minimal example: the shortest piece of code that reproduces the failure, with toy data. Half the time, building it teaches you where the problem was and you no longer have to ask.

And a rule that holds for code you are handed ready-made, including an assistant's: if you cannot explain what a line does, do not put it in a report. The anota blocks of these lessons exist for that.

Step 7. Recommendation systems, which is the learning you already use every day

It is the most visible application of machine learning: what suggests the next video, the next song or the next product. And it works in two ways, which usually get combined:

ApproachWhat it looks atExample
Content-basedHow much a product resembles the ones you already choseYou bought a crochet kit, it offers you more crochet
Behaviour-basedWhat people who behave like you choseWhoever bought that kit also bought thick yarn

The second is more powerful and stranger to explain, because it needs to know nothing about the product: the pattern of who buys what is enough. It is also the one that produces the absurd recommendations when there is little data on a new item.

The course brings here a case study of The Woobles, a company that sells crochet kits for beginners, on how they used recommendation to sell more. The detail is on the platform; what is transferable, and what is worth having clear before reading it, is this:

  • A recommendation system acts, it does not only predict. It changes what people see, so it changes what they buy, so it changes the data the next one is trained on.
  • That is why it reinforces itself: what gets recommended gets bought, and what gets bought gets recommended more. A product that never appears never accumulates evidence that it would work.
  • And that is why it is measured with an experiment, comparing against recommending nothing, which is exactly the A/B test of Course 3. Without that contrast, the system always seems to work.
Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Case A, with the label in place

The same six lots we did with a pencil, to check that the machine finds the same cut.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

grade = np.array([[2], [4], [6], [20], [22], [24]])
profitable = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1])

model = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1).fit(grade, profitable)
print("aciertos:", (model.predict(grade) == profitable).sum(), "de", len(profitable))
print("corte que eligio:", model.tree_.threshold[0])
line by line
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
From scikit-learn, only the tree classifier. Importing the whole library takes time and brings hundreds of things you are not going to use.
np.array([[2], [4], ...])
The double brackets are not a whim. scikit-learn always expects a table: one row per case and one column per variable. Here there are six cases with one variable each, so the table is 6 by 1.
profitable
The label goes separately, as an ordinary vector of six values. And it goes in numbers: 0 and 1, not "no" and "yes".
max_depth=1
A knob of the model: that the tree make a single cut. Without it it would try to refine further, and with six lots that is memorising.
.fit(grade, profitable)
Training. It is scikit-learn's verb and it always receives the table first and the label afterwards. It returns the model itself, now trained, and that is why it can be chained after the parenthesis.
model.predict(grade)
Asking it for its answer on those same lots. It returns a vector of zeros and ones.
(... == profitable).sum()
Comparing two vectors gives a vector of true and false; adding it up counts the trues, because true is worth 1. It is the short way of counting hits.
model.tree_.threshold[0]
Opening the tree from the inside and reading the number it cuts at. The trailing underscore of tree_ is scikit-learn's convention for saying "this only exists after training".
prints
aciertos: 6 de 6
corte que eligio: 13.0

Six out of six, and the cut at 13.0, which is exactly halfway between 6 and 20. The tree had no reason to prefer 13 over 12 or 19: it split the difference, which is what it does when the data does not tell it more.

Step 2. Case B, covering the label

Now exactly the same lots, without telling it anything.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

groups = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(grade)
print("grupo de cada lote:", groups.labels_)
print("centro de cada grupo:", groups.cluster_centers_.ravel())
line by line
n_clusters=2
How many groups you want. You have to tell it, and that is the hard problem of module 3: here two are visible to the naked eye, with real data they are not.
n_init=10
That it repeat the whole process ten times from different starts and keep the best. It is needed because the result depends on where you put the centres at the beginning.
random_state=0
Fixes the randomness of the start, so two runs give the same thing. Without this the result changes between runs and a report cannot be reproduced.
.fit(grade)
A single argument. There is the whole difference from the previous step: there is no label to pass it.
groups.labels_
The group each lot got. The underscore again: it only exists after training.
.cluster_centers_.ravel()
The centres it found. ravel flattens the table into a vector so it prints on one line.
prints
grupo de cada lote: [1 1 1 0 0 0]
centro de cada grupo: [22.  4.]

The centres are 22 and 4, the same ones that came out by hand, and the split is the same as the hidden label.

But look at the names: it called the poor lots group 1 and the rich ones group 0. The other way round from how we had numbered the label, and it does not matter, because those numbers mean nothing. They are two boxes without a tag.

Step 3. What happens with a new lot

new_batch = np.array([[21]])
print("supervisado responde:", model.predict(new_batch)[0])
print("no supervisado responde:", groups.predict(new_batch)[0])
line by line
np.array([[21]])
Double brackets again: one case with one variable is still a table, of 1 by 1. Passing it a bare [21] raises an error, and it is the most common failure when starting out.
.predict(new_batch)[0]
predict always returns a vector, even if you pass it a single case. The [0] pulls that single value out.
prints
supervisado responde: 1
no supervisado responde: 0

Two different answers for the same lot, and both are correct. The 1 of the first means "profitable", because that is what we taught it. The 0 of the second means "from the group whose centre is at 22", and that group turns out to be the rich one, but the model does not know it and cannot tell you.

There is the whole lesson of the module: unsupervised learning finds structure, not meaning. The meaning is put in by you when you look at the groups and christen them.

Traps

Watch out

  • More data does not fix the lack of labels. A million rows without the column you want to predict still do not allow supervised learning. What is needed is the column, and getting it is usually the real bottleneck of a project.
  • Clustering always returns groups. Even if there are none. You ask for three and it gives you three, on completely uniform data. That groups come out does not prove they exist.
  • The group numbers mean nothing and change between runs. Never write "those in group 1" in a report without saying what group 1 is.
  • Do not confuse the bias of this course with that of the previous one. There it was a sample that does not represent the population. Here it is a model that treats one group of people worse. They look alike and are not the same thing.
  • A model with a good average accuracy can be bad for a specific group. The average hides who is doing badly, and that is why the metrics are also looked at by subgroup.
  • That a model is complicated does not make it better. It is the question of the course and the answer is measured, not assumed.
  • Notebooks run in whatever order you like. A result that comes out of cells run out of order is not reproducible even if it is right in front of you.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The two types of learning are two things you already do at the plant, and one of them has its own name in your field.

Supervised is the calibration curve. You send samples to the laboratory, they return the true grade, and with those pairs you build the relationship that lets you estimate the grade of the next ones without sending them. The laboratory is the supervisor, and its analyses are the label: they cost money and time, which is exactly why labels are expensive.

Unsupervised is the classification of geometallurgical domains. You have hundreds of samples with their composition, their hardness and their flotation response, and nobody has yet decided how many ore types there are in that deposit. You group by resemblance, it turns out there are three families, and only then does a geologist look at them and give them a name: the oxidised one, the transitional one, the primary one.

Look at the order, because it is literally the one of the toy example: first the algorithm finds that there are three groups, then a person decides what they are. If the algorithm returned the names, the geologist would be redundant, and they are not.

And the warning travels too. If you ask a deposit with three domains for five, it will give you five, with their maps and their statistics, and two of them will be an arbitrary cut through the middle of a real family. The algorithm does not complain. The one who has to complain is you.

Review

Review

What decides whether a problem is supervised or unsupervised learning?

Whether the column you want to predict exists or not inside your data. The algorithm does not decide it and neither does the size of the set: the available information decides it. With the six lots of the example, having noted which ones were profitable allows learning the rule "profitable if the grade goes past 13" and counting that it gets six out of six. Covering that column, the same six numbers only allow asking whether there are groups, and it turns out there are two, with centres at 4 and 22, but without being able to say which of the two is the profitable one.

The clustering found the same division as the hidden label. Does that mean having labels makes no difference?

No, and this example is built so that it shows. The clustering found the same boundary, but returned two boxes numbered 0 and 1 with no meaning at all, and in fact it called the poor group 1 and the rich group 0, the other way round from the label. It can tell you there are two families; it cannot tell you that one is profitable, because that word was not in the data. Besides, they coincided here because the separation was enormous and the only variable was the relevant one. With ten variables, the clustering can split by something you do not care about.

The tree chose to cut at 13. Why that number and what does it say about the model?

Because it is the midpoint between the richest of the poor lots, 6, and the poorest of the rich ones, 20. Any cut in that gap gets all six equally right, so the data contains no information for choosing and the algorithm splits the difference. That is a sign of fragility, not of precision: with only six lots and such a big gap, the model has a lot of freedom, and a new lot with a grade of 12 will receive an answer that is backed by no similar observation.

Why does ethics come into a technical module and not into an appendix?

Because a model learns from what happened and therefore repeats what happened, applying it faster, to more people and with the appearance of objectivity. If the historical data records a decision that treated one group worse, the model inherits it without anyone having written it. The three questions of step 4 are part of the design and not of the final review: whose the data is and who is missing from it, how much each kind of error costs and whom it costs, and whether the affected person can know why that was decided. That last one turns the choice between a regression and a forest into a decision with consequences, not only a matter of accuracy.

What does it mean for a variable to be continuous and why does it matter in practice?

That it can take any value within a range, so its possible values are infinite: between 20 and 21 grams per tonne fit 20.5 and 20.53 and 20.531. A categorical one, on the other hand, has a closed list of values that can be counted. It matters for two very concrete reasons. The first is that the type of the column you want to predict decides the task: number is regression, label is classification. The second is that almost no model understands text, so a categorical column has to be turned into numbers before training, and the way it is turned changes the result.
