Unsupervised learning techniques

The same six lots of module 1, but now with the question that has no correct answer, how many groups there are. Inertia always falls and never tells you to stop; the silhouette does, and with this data it is 0.8505 and gets calculated by hand.

Context

What this module solves

Module 1 left a question open. When there is no label, there is nothing to compare with, so hits cannot be counted. And still you have to decide whether the result is any good, and above all you have to decide how many groups to ask for, which is a number the algorithm does not calculate: you give it.

This module is about that. The algorithm itself, k-means, is explained in five minutes and is done with a pencil. What takes up the module is how a result without a correct answer gets judged, and the answer is two measures that say different things and sometimes do not agree.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

The same six lots of ore of module 1, with their gold grade:

lote        1    2    3    4     5     6
ley (g/t)   2    4    6    20    22    24

There we asked for two groups because two could be seen. Now the question is another one: what if there are three? What if four? With real data they cannot be seen, so a criterion is needed.

Step 1. The first measure: how much each group spreads inside

With two groups, we already know the centres end up at 4 and 22. Inertia measures how tight they are: the distance of each lot to its own centre is taken, squared, and all of them are added.

grupo del 4:   (2−4)² + (4−4)² + (6−4)²  = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8
grupo del 22:  (20−22)² + (22−22)² + (24−22)² = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8

inercia total = 16

Sixteen. The smaller, the tighter the groups. So far, fine.

Step 2. And now the problem with that measure

Let us try with three groups. The algorithm splits the top group and leaves the centres at 4, 20 and 23, with which the inertia drops to 10. With four groups it drops to 4. With five, to 2.

Groups you ask forInertia

60

Look at the last row. With six groups and six lots, each lot is its own group and the inertia is exactly zero. Perfect, and completely useless.

There is the problem: inertia always drops when asking for more groups, so if you choose the number that minimises it, you always choose as many groups as data points. Inertia measures how tight the groups are, not whether the groups exist.

What is done with it is looking for the elbow: the point where it stops dropping fast. From 16 to 10 there is a big drop; from 4 to 2 it is already splitting hairs. It is a criterion by eye, and that is why the second one is needed.

Step 3. The second measure: is each lot in the right group?

The silhouette asks a different and much better question: for each lot, is it closer to its own than to those of the neighbouring group? It is calculated lot by lot, and with lot 1 it comes out like this.

Its grade is 2. Its own are the 4 and the 6:

distancia media a los SUYOS  = (|2−4| + |2−6|) / 2 = (2 + 4) / 2 = 3

Those of the other group are the 20, the 22 and the 24:

distancia media a los OTROS = (18 + 20 + 22) / 3 = 60 / 3 = 20

And the silhouette is how much it has gained by being where it is, in proportion to the larger distance:

silueta del lote 1 = (20 − 3) / 20 = 17 / 20 = 0,85

Do it with all six and this comes out:

The average of those six is 0.8505, and that is the number that gets reported.

Notice who gets the highest mark: those in the middle of each group, the 4 and the 22, which are exactly the centres. And the lowest, the 6 and the 20, which are the ones looking at the other group. It makes sense, and that is why the silhouette detects things inertia does not.

Step 4. The silhouette does know when to stop

Repeating the calculation for each number of groups:

Inertia always drops and the silhouette collapses. Going from two to three groups the silhouette falls by half, because splitting a real group down the middle leaves its members as close to the neighbouring group as to their own.

The answer is two groups, and now it is justified with a number instead of by eye. Which is exactly what was needed.

Terms

Glossary

  • K-means. The algorithm that splits the data into k groups by moving k centres until they stop moving.
  • Centroid. The centre of a group, which is the average of its members. It usually coincides with no real data point.
  • Inertia. The sum of the squared distances of each point to its centre. It measures how tight the groups are. **It always drops as k grows.**
  • Elbow method. Plotting the inertia against the number of groups and looking for where the curve stops falling fast.
  • Silhouette coefficient. For each point, how much closer it is to its own than to those of the neighbouring group, on a scale from −1 to 1. It is averaged over all the points.
  • Scaling. Putting all the variables on a comparable scale, usually by subtracting the mean and dividing by the deviation. Mandatory before k-means.
  • Convergence. The moment when no point changes group and the centres stop moving. There the algorithm stops.
  • Initialisation. Where the centres get placed at the start. The result depends on it, so it is repeated several times from different starts.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. The algorithm, which is two steps repeated

  1. **Choose k and place k centres**, usually on random points.
  2. Assign each point to the nearest centre.
  3. Move each centre to the average of the points it got.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until nobody changes group.

You already did it by hand in module 1 with these same six lots: starting badly on purpose at 2 and at 4, it converged in three rounds to the centres 4 and 22.

Two properties worth having clear. It always stops, because every round reduces the inertia and it cannot go below zero. And it does not always stop at the best place: depending on where the centres start, it can get stuck in a worse split. That is why the whole process is repeated several times and the one with the lowest inertia is kept, which is what n_init does.

Step 2. Scaling, which is not optional

K-means measures distances, so the variable with the biggest numbers decides the split. And it is not a subtle effect.

Three lots with two variables, tonnage and grade:

LotTonnageGrade
A1000 t2%
B1000 t24%
C1500 t2%

A and B have opposite grades and the same tonnage. A and C have the same grade and different tonnage. The distances:

A con B = 22      (solo cambia la ley)
A con C = 500     (solo cambia el tonelaje)

A resembles B, which has the opposite grade, twenty-three times more than C, which has the same grade. Without scaling, it is not that tonnage weighs more: it is that grade weighs nothing.

Scaling, the two differences are worth 2.12 each, and now the algorithm can decide.

Step 3. Choosing k, which is the real decision

The procedure, and there is no shortcut:

  1. **Run k-means for several values of k**, typically from 2 to 10.
  2. Plot the inertia and look for the elbow.
  3. Plot the silhouette and look for the maximum.
  4. If they agree, decided.
  5. If they do not agree, it gets said, and the context decides: how many groups whoever is going to use this can really handle.

That fifth line is not a cowardly way out. A model proposing eleven customer segments is useless if the marketing team can run three campaigns.

Step 4. What k-means takes for granted, and when it fails

It takes for grantedIt fails when
The groups are roundThe groups are crescent- or ring-shaped
The groups are of similar sizeOne has a thousand points and another twenty
All the variables are worth the sameYou have not scaled
Each point belongs to one groupReality is a continuum without borders

The last one is the most important and the least said. K-means always returns groups, also on completely uniform data where there is none. You ask for three and it gives you three, with their centres and their statistics. That groups come out does not prove they exist; that is what the silhouette is for, which on data without structure comes out close to zero.

Step 5. Compressing an image, which is the same algorithm

The course teaches an application that looks like it is from another world and is identical. A photograph is a list of pixels, and each pixel is three numbers: how much red, how much green and how much blue. That is, a table with three columns, like any other.

Grouping those pixels into, say, three groups, and painting each pixel the colour of its centre, leaves the image with three colours in total. That is compressing: you store three colours and which group each pixel belongs to, instead of storing the exact colour of each one.

It is a good example for two reasons. The first is that here scale does not get in the way, because the three channels run from 0 to 255. The second is that you see at a glance what is lost when lowering k, and that is what the algorithm always does, even though with lots of ore it cannot be seen.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. The two measures, for several values of k

The six lots, trying from two to five groups.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

grade = np.array([2, 4, 6, 20, 22, 24], float).reshape(-1, 1)

for k in range(2, 6):
    model = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=50, random_state=0).fit(grade)
    score = silhouette_score(grade, model.labels_)
    print(f"k={k}   inercia {model.inertia_:6.2f}   silueta {score:.4f}")
line by line
range(2, 6)
The numbers 2, 3, 4 and 5. The second one is not included, which is Python's convention and the source of half the mistakes of whoever starts out. And it starts at 2 because with a single group the silhouette is not defined.
n_init=50
Repeat the whole process fifty times from different starts and keep the best. With six data points it is overkill, but it is the correct habit: k-means can get stuck in a worse split depending on where it starts.
model.inertia_
The sum of squared distances to their centre. Trailing underscore, so it only exists after training.
silhouette_score(grade, model.labels_)
It receives the data and the split, not the model. That is why it serves to judge any clustering, wherever it comes from.
{model.inertia_:6.2f}
Reserves six spaces and two decimals, so the column stays aligned when printing several lines.
prints
k=2   inercia  16.00   silueta 0.8505
k=3   inercia  10.00   silueta 0.4990
k=4   inercia   4.00   silueta 0.1667
k=5   inercia   2.00   silueta 0.0833

The same numbers as in the table above. Inertia drops monotonically and the silhouette sinks, and that is why only the second one answers the question.

Step 2. The scale, with two distances

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

batches = np.array([[1000., 2.], [1000., 24.], [1500., 2.]])

def gap(i, j, matrix):
    return float(np.sqrt(((matrix[i] - matrix[j]) ** 2).sum()))

print(f"sin escalar   A-B {gap(0, 1, batches):6.2f}   A-C {gap(0, 2, batches):6.2f}")

scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(batches)
print(f"escalando     A-B {gap(0, 1, scaled):6.2f}   A-C {gap(0, 2, scaled):6.2f}")

Defining a function of our own, so as not to repeat the same calculation four times. Everything indented below belongs to it.
matrix[i] - matrix[j]
Subtracting two rows gives a vector with the difference of each column. numpy does this without loops.
(... ** 2).sum()
Each difference squared, and added up. It is Pythagoras' theorem in as many dimensions as there are columns.
np.sqrt(...)
The square root of that sum: the straight-line distance.
StandardScaler().fit_transform(...)
Two things at once: fit learns the mean and the deviation of each column, transform applies them. Mind this in a real model: the scaler is learned with the training set only, or you will be leaking information from the test into it.

escalando     A-B   2.12   A-C   2.12

Without scaling, 22 against 500. Scaling, 2.12 against 2.12 exactly: the two differences come to weigh the same, which is what was intended.

Step 3. Compressing colours

A toy photograph: one thousand six hundred pixels of three colours, with sensor noise on top, which is what makes almost no pixel identical to another.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
palette = np.array([[200., 30., 30.], [30., 120., 200.], [240., 230., 200.]])
pixels = (palette[rng.integers(0, 3, 1600)] + rng.normal(0, 8, (1600, 3))).clip(0, 255)

print("colores distintos:", len(np.unique(pixels.round().astype(int), axis=0)))

model = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(pixels)
for centre in model.cluster_centers_.round().astype(int):
    print("  rojo", centre[0], " verde", centre[1], " azul", centre[2])
line by line
np.random.default_rng(0)
numpy's random generator, with seed 0 so the same thing comes out every time. Without a seed, the example would not be reproducible.
palette[rng.integers(0, 3, 1600)]
One thousand six hundred random numbers between 0 and 2, used as indices into the palette. Choosing rows of a table with a vector of positions is one of the things numpy does and that in pure Python would take a loop.
rng.normal(0, 8, (1600, 3))
Bell-shaped noise, mean 0 and deviation 8, one per channel and per pixel. It is what makes two pixels of the same real colour not identical, as in a real photo.
.clip(0, 255)
Trims whatever goes outside the valid colour range. Without this, the noise can produce a −3 of red, which does not exist.
np.unique(..., axis=0)
The distinct rows, not the distinct values. Without axis=0 it would count loose numbers.
.round().astype(int)
Round and convert to integer. A colour is an integer from 0 to 255, and comparing decimals to count repeats would not work.
prints
colores distintos: 1580
  rojo 30  verde 120  azul 200
  rojo 200  verde 30  azul 29
  rojo 239  verde 230  azul 201

From 1,580 distinct colours to 3, and the three centres are almost exactly the three original colours: the noise gets averaged and disappears. The mean error per channel stays at 6.4 over 255, 2.5%.

That is what k-means always does: replacing each data point with the centre of its group. Here it shows because the result is an image; with lots of ore the same thing happens and it does not show.

Traps

Watch out

  • **Inertia never chooses k**, because it always drops. With as many groups as data points it is zero. It serves to look for the elbow and nothing else.
  • Without scaling, k-means groups by the variable with the biggest numbers. It is not that it weighs more: it is that the others do not weigh at all.
  • The scaler is learned with the training set only. If you pass it the whole set, the mean and the deviation carry information from the test inside.
  • K-means always returns groups, also where there are none. The silhouette near zero is the sign that there was nothing to group.
  • The group numbers mean nothing and change between runs. Without random_state, two runs give different labels for the same split.
  • The mean silhouette hides the badly placed ones. A 0.6 average can have one group at 0.9 and another at 0.2. Look at it per group before signing off.
  • A high mean silhouette does not validate the groups as a business concept. It says they are separated in your variables, not that they mean something.
  • If the elbow and the silhouette do not agree, it gets said. Tweaking one of the two until they fit is window dressing.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The number of groups to ask k-means for is, under another name, how many stockpiles you set up in the yard.

Ore arrives from several faces with different grades, and you have to decide into how many piles it gets separated before feeding the plant. And the decision has exactly the same shape:

In k-meansIn the stockpile yard
Each groupA pile
The centre of the groupThe mean grade of that pile
InertiaHow mixed up each pile is inside
Asking for more groupsSetting up more piles

And here is the parallel that explains everything: the more piles you set up, the more homogeneous each one is. With one pile per truck you would have perfect homogeneity and zero internal variability, which is literally zero inertia with as many groups as data points.

And it would be unmanageable. Every extra pile means more space, more loader movements and more blending decisions. Homogeneity is not the only criterion, and that is why nobody sets up one pile per truck even though the internal variability is the smallest possible.

The elbow is exactly that: the point where one more pile no longer improves homogeneity much and does add work. And the silhouette answers the other question, whether the piles are really separate or whether you are splitting material that is in fact the same.

With one honest difference: in the yard you know what one more pile costs, and the algorithm does not. That is why the final decision of step 3 is taken by whoever knows the operation.

Review

Review

Why can inertia not be used on its own to choose the number of groups?

Because it always drops as k grows, until it is exactly zero when there are as many groups as data points and each point is its own group. With the six lots it goes from 16 with two groups to 10 with three, to 4 with four and to 0 with six, so the number that minimises it is always the maximum possible, which describes nothing. What inertia measures is how tight the groups are, not whether the groups exist. It is used by looking for the elbow, which is a criterion by eye, and that is why the silhouette is needed next to it.

In the example, why do lots 2 and 5 have the highest silhouette?

Because they are the ones at the centre of their group, which is exactly what the silhouette rewards. Lot 2, with grade 4, is at a mean distance of 2 from its own and of 18 from the others, so its silhouette is (18−2)/18 = 0.8889. Lots 3 and 6, with grades 6 and 20, are the ones looking at the opposite group and score 0.8125. The silhouette measures, point by point, how much better each one fits where it is than in the neighbouring group, so those at the edge score worse and those at the core better.

One lot weighs 1000 t with a grade of 2% and another 1000 t with a grade of 24%. Why would k-means group them together?

Because without scaling, the distance between them is 22, which comes only from the grade, while the distance to a third lot with the same grade but 1500 t is 500. In terms of distance, two lots with opposite grades resemble each other twenty-three times more than two with the same grade and different tonnage, so the algorithm groups by tonnage and the grade stops counting. It is not that tonnage weighs more: it is that grade weighs nothing. Scaling each variable to mean 0 and deviation 1, the two distances come to be 2.12, and then the algorithm can really decide.

The silhouette coefficient of a clustering comes out 0.05. What does it mean?

That the points are almost as close to the neighbouring group as to their own, that is, the groups are not separated. And since k-means always returns as many groups as you ask for, even on uniform data with no structure at all, that 0.05 is the sign that there was probably nothing to group. The honest answer is to say so, not to raise or lower k until a better number comes out. It is also worth looking at the silhouette per group before concluding, because a low average can come from a single badly formed group while the rest is fine.

What does compressing an image have to do with grouping lots of ore?

That it is the same algorithm on a different table. A photograph is a list of pixels and each pixel is three numbers, the red, the green and the blue, so it is a three-column table like any other. Grouping into three and painting each pixel the colour of its centre leaves the image with three colours, which is what k-means always does: replacing each data point with the centre of its group. In the example, 1,580 distinct colours get reduced to 3 and the centres recover almost exactly the original colours, because the noise gets averaged. With lots of ore the same thing happens, only there it cannot be seen.
