Tree-based modeling

A tree split calculated by hand with ten lots, and then the demonstration of why an unlimited tree gets 100% right in training and 54% in test. Out of that come the two ways of putting many trees together, and the warning that two columns of pure noise take 43% of the importance.

10 h course time17 videos11 readings5 assignments10 practice2 interactive
Context

What this module solves

A decision tree is the easiest model to explain there is: a chain of yes-or-no questions. It gets drawn, read out loud and understood without knowing mathematics.

And it has a serious flaw that this module attacks head on: if you let it grow, it memorises. An unlimited tree can end up asking one question per row of your data, get 100% of what it already saw right, and be of no use with a new case.

Out of that flaw come the two big ideas of the module. The first is putting limits on the tree, and choosing those limits with data instead of by eye. The second is using many trees instead of one, which can be done in two different ways and with opposite logics.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Ten lots processed. Of each one it is known whether reagent was dosed, and whether it passed specification:

PassedDid not passTotal
Without reagent145
With reagent505
Total6410

The tree has to decide whether it is worth asking about the reagent. And it decides with a number, the Gini impurity, which measures how mixed up a group is.

Step 1. The formula, in words

The Gini impurity answers: if I picked two lots at random from this group, what is the probability that one passes and the other does not? Zero means they are all the same; the higher, the more mixed.

It is calculated by subtracting from 1 the sum of the squares of the proportions:

Gini = 1 − (proporción que pasa)² − (proporción que no pasa)²

Step 2. How mixed up the whole group is

Before asking anything, there are 6 that pass and 4 that do not, out of 10:

Gini de la raíz = 1 − (6/10)² − (4/10)²
                = 1 − 0,36 − 0,16
                = 0,48

0.48. Close to 0.5, which is the maximum with two classes, that is very mixed. It makes sense: almost half and half.

Step 3. How mixed up the two pieces are left

If we ask about the reagent, the group splits in two.

The five without reagent, of which one passed:

Gini izquierda = 1 − (1/5)² − (4/5)²
               = 1 − 0,04 − 0,64
               = 0,32

The five with reagent, of which all five passed:

Gini derecha = 1 − (5/5)² − (0/5)²
             = 1 − 1 − 0
             = 0

Exactly zero. That group is pure: they all passed, and there is no need to ask anything more about them. When a tree reaches a zero, it stops splitting there.

Step 4. Was it worth it?

The two pieces have five lots each, so the impurity left after asking is the average of the two:

impureza después = (5/10) × 0,32 + (5/10) × 0
                 = 0,16 + 0
                 = 0,16

And the gain is what got cleaned up:

ganancia = 0,48 − 0,16 = 0,32

Going from 0.48 to 0.16 is cutting the mixing to a third with a single question. That is all a tree does as it grows: it tries every possible question, calculates the gain of each one, and keeps the biggest. Then it repeats the same thing inside each piece.

Step 5. And here the problem starts

Look at the left piece, which was left with Gini 0.32: four that do not pass and one that does. The tree is not satisfied. It is going to look for another question that separates that one from the other four, and it is going to find it, even if it is by asking about something irrelevant like the truck number.

If you let it, it will go on until each leaf has a single lot and every impurity is zero. It will have got 100% of what it saw right, and it will have learned that truck 47 produces good lots. That is overfitting, and the rest of the module is about containing it.

Terms

Glossary

  • Decision tree. A model that asks chained yes-or-no questions. Each answer takes you to another question, until reaching a final answer.
  • Root node. The first question, at the very top.
  • Leaf. An end of the tree, where no more is asked and the answer is given.
  • Depth. How many chained questions there are at most from the root to a leaf.
  • Gini impurity. How mixed up a group is, from 0 (all the same) to 0.5 (half and half with two classes).
  • Gain. How much impurity a question removes. The tree chooses the question that gains most.
  • Overfitting. The model learning the particulars of your specific data instead of the general rule. It is recognised because it gets far more right in training than in test.
  • Hyperparameter. A knob you fix before training and that the model does not learn: the maximum depth, how many trees, how much each one corrects. Here I call them knobs.
  • Grid search. Trying every combination of a list of knob values and keeping the best.
  • Bootstrap. Drawing a sample of the same size as the original with replacement, so some rows come out repeated and others do not come out.
  • Random forest. Many trees trained on different resamples and with only some variables available at each split. Everyone votes.
  • Boosting. Many trees trained in a chain, each one devoted to correcting what the previous one got wrong.
  • Variable importance. How much each column contributed to the splits of the model. It is not a significance test.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. How a tree grows

  1. It looks at every possible question on every column.
  2. It calculates the gain of each one, as in the example.
  3. It keeps the best and splits the group in two.
  4. It repeats inside each piece.
  5. It stops when a piece is pure, or when some knob prevents it.

Two things that come free and are worth knowing. There is no need to scale, because the tree compares each variable with itself and never measures distances between different variables: it is exactly the opposite of k-means. And curved relationships do not bother it, because a curve gets approximated with steps.

Step 2. Why it memorises, and how it shows

Step 5 of the list is the culprit: if nothing prevents it, the tree splits until each leaf has a single case. With 300 training lots that is about 77 leaves, each one saying "this specific lot passed".

And it is recognised with a comparison that always has to be made:

TrainingTest
Unlimited tree1.00000.5400
Tree of depth 30.72670.6100

The first gets everything it already saw right and little more than a coin toss with what it did not. The second gets less right in training and more in test, which is the only thing that matters.

If your model gets far more right in training than in test, it is memorising. The distance between those two columns is the diagnosis.

Step 3. The knobs, and how they are chosen

The ones that contain a tree:

KnobWhat it doesEffect
max_depthHow many chained questions at mostThe most direct
min_samples_leafHow many cases at minimum in a leafPrevents single-case leaves
min_samples_splitHow many cases are needed to splitSimilar to the previous one
max_featuresHow many columns it can look at in each splitGives variety, key in the forest

And how they are chosen: not by eye. A list of values is made for each one, every combination is tried with cross-validation, and whichever wins is looked at. That is grid search.

With 6 depths and 4 leaf sizes there are 24 combinations, and with 5 folds each there are 120 trainings. It sounds like a lot and it takes less than a second with 300 rows. With a hundred thousand rows and a grid of a thousand combinations it takes hours, and that is why the grid gets bounded and it is said how long it took.

Step 4. The first way of putting trees together: the forest

The idea is of a disarming honesty. If a single tree only goes wrong by memorising its data, train many on slightly different data and let them vote. Each one will memorise different things, and their errors cancel each other out.

Variety is achieved in two ways at once:

  1. Resampling. Each tree is trained with a sample of the same size drawn at random with replacement, so it sees some rows repeated and others not at all.
  2. Limited columns. At each split, each tree can only look at a few columns chosen at random. Without this, they would all start with the same question and resemble each other too much.

That second point is what turns plain resampling into a random forest, and it is the part most people skip when explaining it.

What you gain: much less overfitting, and knobs that forgive. What you lose: you can no longer draw the model on a whiteboard, because it is three hundred trees.

Step 5. The second way: boosting

Here the logic is the opposite, and that is why they are different families and not variants.

In the forest, the trees are independent: they could all be trained at once and each one ignores the others. In boosting they go in a chain: one is trained, where it went wrong is looked at, and the next one is devoted to those cases. Then another on what still fails, and so on.

Random forestBoosting
The trees are trainedAt once, independentIn a chain, one after the other
Each tree isDeep and powerfulTiny, sometimes a single split
It combinesBy votingBy adding corrections
It attacksVariance: the model wobblingBias: the model falling short
If it has too many treesIt plateaus, does not get worseIt starts memorising

That last row decides which to use when in a hurry. A forest can have extra trees without penalty; a boosting cannot, and that is why its most important knob is learning_rate, which says how much attention is paid to each correction.

Names you will see: AdaBoost was the first and gives more weight to the cases that were got wrong; gradient boosting is the general version and the one used today; XGBoost is a highly optimised implementation of that idea, and it is the one the course teaches by name.

Step 6. Where tree-based models really get used

The course brings here a case study of Booz Allen Hamilton, a large consultancy, on how a machine learning model uncovered findings for them about how they assign their people to projects. The detail is on the platform; what is transferable is why the problem calls for a tree and not a regression, and that can be reasoned.

A staff assignment problem has three characteristics that tree-based models handle well and a regression handles badly:

What the problem is likeWhy it fits a tree
Almost everything is categories: role, office, client, project typeThe tree asks about them directly, without turning them into dozens of columns
The rules are conditional: what matters for one role does not matter for anotherA tree is literally a chain of nested conditions
The response is not straight: experience helps up to a point and then stops helpingThe steps approximate any shape without anyone declaring it

And a warning this particular case forces us to repeat: a model that decides about people's careers falls squarely into module 1. The third question of that step, whether the affected person can know why, has an uncomfortable answer here, because a forest does not answer it well. An aggregate finding about how people get assigned is one thing; a model that decides who goes to which project is another, and the second needs much more than good accuracy.

Step 7. Variable importance, with its warning

A forest cannot be drawn, but it can tell you which columns it used most to split. It is what returns it to the realm of the explainable, and it is among the first things looked at.

And it carries a warning that is almost never given. In the code example there are four columns: two that really influence and two of pure noise generated at random, which by construction mean nothing. The importance comes out like this:

VariableImportance
pH0.3020
Reagent0.2664
Noise 20.2186
Noise 10.2130

The two noise columns take 43% of the total. It is not a bug: the tree needs to split somewhere, and a random column sometimes separates a little by chance.

So the importance ranks the real variables correctly above the noise, and it does not say that the ones at the bottom are irrelevant nor that the ones at the top cause anything. It is a clue, not a proof.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. The split by hand, checked

The ten lots of the example, to see that the machine calculates the same Ginis.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

# columna unica: reactivo dosificado (1) o no (0)
batches = np.array([[0]] * 5 + [[1]] * 5)
passed = np.array([1, 0, 0, 0, 0] + [1] * 5)

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1).fit(batches, passed)
print(f"Gini raiz      {tree.tree_.impurity[0]:.4f}")
print(f"Gini izquierda {tree.tree_.impurity[1]:.4f}")
print(f"Gini derecha   {tree.tree_.impurity[2]:.4f}")
line by line
[[0]] * 5 + [[1]] * 5
Multiplying a list by 5 repeats it five times, and the + glues them. It is the short way of writing ten rows without typing them.
max_depth=1
A single split, which is exactly the example. Without this knob the tree would go on splitting the left piece.
tree.tree_.impurity
The vector of impurities of all the nodes. Position 0 is the root, 1 the left child and 2 the right one, which is the order in which a tree is walked.
prints
Gini raiz      0.4800
Gini izquierda 0.3200
Gini derecha   0.0000

The three numbers of the example by hand: 0.48, 0.32 and exactly 0.

Step 2. Overfitting, measured

Four hundred lots where only two of the four columns really influence.

from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
reagent = rng.normal(30, 8, n)
ph = rng.normal(10.5, 1.2, n)
noise = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 2))
chance = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(0.10 * (reagent - 30) + 0.8 * (ph - 10.5))))
passed = (rng.random(n) < chance).astype(int)
features = np.column_stack([reagent, ph, noise])

train_x, test_x, train_y, test_y = train_test_split(
    features, passed, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=passed)

for name, model in [("sin limite", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0)),
                    ("profundidad 3", DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=0))]:
    model.fit(train_x, train_y)
    print(f"{name:14} entrenamiento {model.score(train_x, train_y):.4f}   "
          f"prueba {model.score(test_x, test_y):.4f}   hojas {model.get_n_leaves()}")
line by line
rng.normal(30, 8, n)
Four hundred bell-shaped values with mean 30 and deviation 8. It is the simulated reagent dose.
1 / (1 + np.exp(-(...)))
The logistic function of Course 4, used backwards: here the probability of passing gets manufactured from the two variables that influence. That way we know for certain which ones matter, which is what the example wants to prove.
rng.random(n) < chance
Turns that probability into a yes or a no, rolling a die per lot. Without this step the data would be deterministic and there would be nothing to learn.
np.column_stack([...])
Glues the vectors as columns of a table. reagent and ph are one column each and noise contributes two, so four come out.
model.score(...)
Accuracy, which is what score returns by default in a classifier. It is called twice, on training and on test, and the comparison between the two is the diagnosis.
model.get_n_leaves()
How many leaves the tree has. It is the most direct measure of how complicated it has become.
prints
sin limite     entrenamiento 1.0000   prueba 0.5400   hojas 77
profundidad 3  entrenamiento 0.7267   prueba 0.6100   hojas 8

Seventy-seven leaves for three hundred lots, perfect accuracy in training and 0.54 in test, which with almost balanced classes is barely better than a coin.

The depth 3 one gets less right in training and more in test. A model worse on what it already knows and better on what it does not: that is exactly what is sought.

Step 3. Choosing the knobs with the grid

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

grid = {"max_depth": [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, None],
        "min_samples_leaf": [1, 5, 10, 20]}

search = GridSearchCV(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), grid, cv=5,
                      scoring="roc_auc").fit(train_x, train_y)

print("mejor combinacion:", search.best_params_)
print(f"AUC en validacion cruzada: {search.best_score_:.4f}")
print(f"AUC en prueba:             {search.score(test_x, test_y):.4f}")
line by line
grid = {...}
A dictionary: each key is the exact name of a knob and each value the list to try. Every combination gets tried, that is 6 times 4 equals 24.
None en max_depth
No limit. It is included on purpose so the search can choose it if it really were the best, instead of ruling it out by prejudice.
cv=5
Five-fold cross-validation. Each combination is trained five times, so there are 120 trainings in total.
scoring="roc_auc"
What decides who wins. If you do not set it, it uses accuracy, which with unbalanced classes would choose the wrong model.
.fit(train_x, train_y)
On the training set, never on the test set. The grid splits the training set into five internally, and the test set stays untouched.
search.best_params_
The winning combination. And search.best_estimator_ is that model already retrained with the whole training set.
prints
mejor combinacion: {'max_depth': 5, 'min_samples_leaf': 5}
AUC en validacion cruzada: 0.7013
AUC en prueba:             0.6556

Two things to read here. The first: it chose depth 5 and leaves of at least 5 cases, that is, it contained the tree without anyone telling it by hand.

The second is more important. Validation gave 0.7013 and test 0.6556. Validation comes out more optimistic, and it is no coincidence: it is the set used to choose among 24 candidates, so the winner is one partly by luck with those folds. That is why the figure published is the test one, and that is why there are three sets and not two.

Step 4. The three families, on the same data

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from xgboost import XGBClassifier

models = {
    "un arbol, ajustado":      search.best_estimator_,
    "bosque aleatorio":        RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0),
    "refuerzo (scikit-learn)": HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0),
    "refuerzo (xgboost)":      XGBClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=3,
                                             learning_rate=0.1, eval_metric="logloss",
                                             random_state=0),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    model.fit(train_x, train_y)
    print(f"{name:24} AUC {roc_auc_score(test_y, model.predict_proba(test_x)[:, 1]):.4f}")
line by line
n_estimators=300
Three hundred trees in the forest. More almost never makes it worse, it only takes longer, and that is why this knob of the forest forgives.
learning_rate=0.1
How much attention is paid to each correction of the boosting. Lowering it forces putting in more trees and usually generalises better. It is the knob that decides in this family.
eval_metric="logloss"
xgboost asks you to declare what it is measured with during training. Without this it prints a warning.
model.predict_proba(test_x)[:, 1]
All the rows, column 1: the probability of the positive class. The bare colon means "all".
roc_auc_score(...)
Compares the truth with those probabilities. It is used here because it does not depend on the threshold, so it compares models and not decisions.
prints
un arbol, ajustado       AUC 0.6556
bosque aleatorio         AUC 0.6759
refuerzo (scikit-learn)  AUC 0.6787
refuerzo (xgboost)       AUC 0.6868

The order is the textbook one: a tree loses to the forest, and the forest to the boosting. The differences are of two or three hundredths, which with a hundred test cases is little margin.

Keep that reservation, because in the Waze project this same order does not hold and the logistic regression of Course 4 beats all three.

Step 5. What the forest used, and what that does not mean

forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0).fit(train_x, train_y)
names = ["reactivo", "pH", "ruido 1", "ruido 2"]

for position in np.argsort(forest.feature_importances_)[::-1]:
    print(f"{names[position]:10} {forest.feature_importances_[position]:.4f}")
line by line
np.argsort(...)
Returns the positions that would sort the vector from smallest to largest, not the values. It serves to sort the names by their importance.
[::-1]
Reversing a list. Since argsort sorts from smallest to largest and we want the opposite, it gets reversed.
forest.feature_importances_
How much each column contributed to lowering the impurity, summed over the three hundred trees and normalised to add up to 1.
prints
pH         0.3020
reactivo   0.2664
ruido 2    0.2186
ruido 1    0.2130

The two that really influence stay on top, so the order is correct. But look at the numbers: the two columns of pure noise take 0.4316 between them, 43% of the total.

Those two columns are random values that have absolutely no relationship with the result, by construction, because we manufactured them. And still they score almost like the good ones.

The reason is that the tree has to split somewhere, and a random column sometimes separates a little by pure chance. Importance ranks; it does not rule out. If you use it to decide which columns to drop, drop the ones at the bottom carefully and check that the model does not get worse.

Traps

Watch out

  • An unlimited tree always memorises. It is not a risk: it is what it does by design if nothing prevents it.
  • Always compare training against test. A model with 1.0000 in training is suspicious, not good.
  • The validation figure is optimistic. It came out 0.7013 against 0.6556 in test, because the winner was chosen by looking at that validation.
  • Variable importance is not significance. Two columns of pure noise added up to 43%.
  • Nor is it cause. That pH comes out on top does not say that moving it changes the result.
  • A forest can have extra trees without penalty; a boosting cannot. Putting a thousand trees in a boosting with a high learning_rate is a recipe for overfitting.
  • Trees do not need scaling, the opposite of k-means. Scaling does them no harm, it just does nothing.
  • A tree is unstable. Change a few rows and you can get a completely different tree with similar accuracy. That is why drawing "the" tree and telling it as if it were the truth is risky.
  • That the forest beats the tree is not guaranteed. It is the usual thing, and in the Waze project of this same course it does not happen.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The decision tree does not need explaining to you: it already exists, written on a whiteboard next to the control panel. It is the plant's operating tree.

¿La ley de cabeza pasa de 0,8 %?
├── sí  → ¿El pH está por encima de 10,5?
│         ├── sí  → dosis normal de colector
│         └── no  → subir cal antes de nada
└── no  → ¿Hay finos por encima del 30 %?
          ├── sí  → bajar tonelaje
          └── no  → dosis reforzada

That is literally a decision tree, with its nodes, its branches and its leaves. The difference is where the questions come from: the one on the whiteboard was written by someone with twenty years at the plant, and the model's comes from calculating the Gini gain of every possible question.

And the traps travel too. A shift supervisor who added a rule for every odd incident they have seen would end up with an enormous tree, with one branch for the day it rained and another for the lot from truck 47. That tree describes the last three years perfectly and is of no use for tomorrow, which is exactly overfitting in overalls.

The way of containing it is the same in both places: no branch gets added for a single case. That is min_samples_leaf, and at the plant it is called common sense.

And the forest also has its equivalent, which is the reason important decisions are taken in committee. You ask five people who have seen different plants, each one goes wrong on different things, and the joint decision is better than any one of them separately. What is lost is just as real: afterwards you can no longer write in the minutes a single clean reason for why that was decided.

Review

Review

In the example of the ten lots, why is the gain 0.32 and what does it mean?

Because the whole group, with 6 that pass and 4 that do not, has a Gini impurity of 1 − 0.36 − 0.16 = 0.48, very close to the maximum of 0.5 that corresponds to half and half. On asking about the reagent, the piece without reagent is left with 1 of 5 and an impurity of 0.32, and the piece with reagent is left with 5 of 5 and an impurity of exactly 0, because it is pure. Since the two pieces have five lots, the impurity left is the average, 0.16, and the gain is the difference: 0.48 − 0.16 = 0.32. It means that that single question cut the mixing to a third, and the tree always chooses the question with the biggest gain.

A tree gets 100% right in training and 54% in test. What has happened and how is it fixed?

It has memorised. Without any knob to stop it, the tree went on splitting until each leaf held almost a single case, 77 leaves for 300 lots, so it can recite the training set and has learned no general rule. The 54% in test, with almost balanced classes, is barely better than a coin. It is fixed by putting limits on it: with depth 3 the same tree drops to 0.7267 in training and rises to 0.6100 in test, that is a model worse on what it already knew and better on what it did not. And those limits are chosen with a grid search and cross-validation, not by eye.

What real difference is there between a random forest and gradient boosting?

That their trees relate in opposite ways. In the forest they are independent: each one is trained on a different resample and seeing only some columns at each split, and at the end they vote, so that their errors, which are different, cancel out. In boosting they go in a chain: one is trained, where it failed is looked at, and the next one is devoted to those cases, adding corrections. The practical consequence is the one that decides: a forest can have extra trees and at worst plateaus, while a boosting with too many trees starts memorising, and that is why its critical knob is how much attention is paid to each correction.

The grid search gave 0.7013 and the test 0.6556. Which gets published and why?

The test one, 0.6556. The cross-validation figure is inflated because it is the set on which the 24 combinations were compared and the winner chosen, so part of its advantage is having fitted well with those particular folds. Choosing the maximum of twenty-four noisy measures produces an optimistic number even if no combination were really better. The test set took part in no decision, and that is the whole reason it exists and gets set aside before starting.

Two columns of pure noise obtained 43% of the importance. What conclusion has to be drawn?

That variable importance ranks but does not rule out, and is certainly not a test of significance or of cause. Those two columns were generated at random and have no relationship with the result, but the tree needs to split somewhere and a random column separates a little by chance every now and then, so it accumulates importance. What did work is the order: the two variables that really influence stayed above the two noise ones. The correct reading is to treat it as a clue about what to look at, and if it is going to be used to remove columns, remove them and check that the performance on test does not get worse.
