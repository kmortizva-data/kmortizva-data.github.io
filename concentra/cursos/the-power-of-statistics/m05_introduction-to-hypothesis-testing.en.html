Introduction to hypothesis testing

Where the whole course converges. Two plant shifts with five measurements each show why looking at the difference of means is not enough, and why a large effect can come out not significant while a tiny one comes out hugely significant.

5 h course time8 videos8 readings5 assignments3 practice
Context

What this module solves

Here everything comes together: the probability of module 2, the sampling of module 3 and the intervals of module 4 combine to answer the most repeated business question there is.

Is this difference I am seeing real, or is it chance in the sample I happened to get?

It is literally what you did in your three projects. Compare two groups and decide whether the difference justifies acting. This module is the theory underneath those scripts.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

The plant works in two shifts and you suspect the day shift recovers more. You have five recovery measurements from each, in percent:

Turno día    84   86   85   88   87
Turno noche  83   85   82   86   84

Step 1. The difference that jumps out

media día   = (84 + 86 + 85 + 88 + 87) / 5 = 430 / 5 = 86.0
media noche = (83 + 85 + 82 + 86 + 84) / 5 = 420 / 5 = 84.0

diferencia = 2.0 puntos de recuperación

Two points of recovery. In a real plant that is real money, and any shift supervisor would want to act at once. The question is whether those two points are a pattern or what happens when you measure only five times.

Step 2. How much each shift dances on its own

Before believing the difference you have to look at how much the measurements vary within each shift. The distances to their own mean:

Day shiftDistance to 86SquaredNight shiftDistance to 84Squared
84−2483−11
860085+11
85−1182−24
88+2486+24
87+118400
sum10sum10

The two shifts have the same variability. Dividing by 4, which is n minus one:

varianza de cada turno = 10 / 4 = 2.5
desviación de cada turno = √2.5 = 1.5811

Mind what this means: within one shift the measurements already move almost 1.6 points. And the difference between shifts we want to defend is 2. It starts to look less clear.

Step 3. How much could the difference dance by chance alone?

This is the key question, and it is answered with the standard error of module 3, now applied to the difference between two means:

error estándar = √(2.5/5 + 2.5/5) = √(0.5 + 0.5) = √1 = 1.0

Exactly one point. In other words: even if the two shifts were identical, their sample means would typically differ by around 1 point by chance alone.

Step 4. The t statistic is a division

Now we compare the observed difference against that variation by chance:

t = diferencia observada / error estándar = 2.0 / 1.0 = 2.0

The difference is twice what chance normally produces. That is all a t statistic means: how many "units of chance" your difference measures.

Step 5. And the verdict, which surprises

With t = 2.0 and 8 degrees of freedom, the p-value comes out:

p = 0.0805

It is greater than 0.05, so the null hypothesis is not rejected. With this data there is not enough evidence to claim that the shifts differ.

Two points of recovery, which looked crystal clear, do not hold up. And the confidence interval of module 4 says it just as clearly:

IC 95% de la diferencia = [−0.306 , 4.306]

It crosses zero. The real difference could be zero, or even negative.

Step 6. But beware of the opposite conclusion

Here comes what gets misread most. Calculate the effect size:

d de Cohen = 2.0 / 1.5811 = 1.2649

By convention, anything above 0.8 is a large effect. In other words: the effect we observe is huge, and even so the test says no.

How can that be? Because there are only five measurements per shift. The test is not saying "there is no difference": it is saying "with five values I cannot tell it from noise".

Check it by taking twenty measurements per shift instead of five, with exactly the same means and the same variability:

n = 5   →   t = 2.00   p = 0.0805      no significativo
n = 20  →   t = 4.36   p = 0.000096    significativo

The same 2 points of difference. The only thing that changed was how many times you measured.

Terms

Glossary

  • Null hypothesis (H₀). The claim that "nothing is going on here", that there is no difference. It is assumed true until the evidence says otherwise.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Hₐ). What is suspected: there is a difference. It is never proved directly; evidence is accumulated against H₀.
  • How the two hypotheses differ. They are not two opinions on equal footing, and that asymmetry is the whole method:
Null hypothesisAlternative hypothesis
What it claimsNo effect and no differenceThere is one
Who carries the burden of proofNobody: it is assumed true from the startYou: evidence has to be accumulated
What gets calculatedThe p-value assuming it trueNothing directly
What outcomes it admitsIt is rejected, or not rejectedIt is supported, or left unsupported
How it is writtenWith an equals sign: μ₁ = μ₂With not equal, greater or less: μ₁ ≠ μ₂

Both are written before looking at the data, and the alternative decides whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed.

  • Significance level (α). The risk you accept of shouting "there is a difference!" when there is none. By convention 0.05.
  • p-value. The probability of observing a result as extreme as yours **if H₀ were true**. Small means your result would be rare in a world without an effect.
  • t statistic. The observed difference divided by the standard error. How many units of chance your difference measures.
  • Type I error. Rejecting H₀ when it was true. A false positive. Its probability is α.
  • Type II error. Not rejecting H₀ when it was false. A false negative, which is what happened to us with five measurements.
  • Statistical power. The probability of detecting an effect that does exist. It rises with the sample size and with the effect size.
  • Effect size (Cohen's d). How big the difference is in units of standard deviation. Below 0.2 it is negligible, around 0.5 medium, above 0.8 large.
  • One-tailed test. The alternative specifies a direction, like "the day shift recovers more". Two-tailed, it only says they differ.
  • One-sample t-test. Compares a mean against a fixed reference value.
  • Two-sample t-test. Compares the means of two independent groups.
  • Test of proportions. Compares two rates or percentages.
  • A/B testing. A two-sample hypothesis test applied to a product: you split users at random between two versions and compare a metric.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Write H₀ and Hₐ before looking at the data

In the example: H₀ is that the two shifts recover the same, Hₐ is that they do not. If you write them after seeing the result, you are fitting the question to the answer and the test loses all its value.

Step 2. Fix α beforehand too

0.05 by convention. It is lowered to 0.01 when a false positive is expensive, like stopping a plant or withdrawing a batch.

Step 3. Choose the test according to what you compare

What you compareTest
One mean against a fixed valueone-sample t
Means of two independent groupstwo-sample t, with Welch by default
Two proportions or ratesz-test of proportions

Step 4. Calculate and decide, with the right sentence

si p < α   →  se rechaza H₀: hay evidencia de diferencia
si p ≥ α   →  NO se rechaza H₀: no hay evidencia suficiente

Notice the asymmetry of the language. You never say "we accept H₀" or "we have proved they are equal". The correct thing is "there is not enough evidence", because the effect may exist and your sample may be too small to see it, which is exactly what happened in the toy example.

Step 5. Report the effect size, always

A p-value alone says nothing useful, because it depends on the sample size. The two faces of the same coin:

SituationWhat happensReal example
Small sample, large effectHigh p, but the effect is real and you do not detect itThe two shifts: d = 1.26 and p = 0.08
Enormous sample, tiny effectMinuscule p, but it does not matter in practiceAutomatidata: d = 0.09 and p = 6.8e-12

Without the effect size you cannot tell these two cases apart, and they are opposites.

Step 6. The same method outside the plant: the Ipsos case

The course presents a case study of Ipsos, a market research company, on how hypothesis testing is used to help advertisers make more effective ads. The detail of the case is on the platform; what matters here is that it is exactly the test you just ran with two shifts, in another vocabulary:

At the plantIn market research
Day shift against night shiftVersion A of the ad against version B
Recovery measured on each batchBrand recall or purchase intent in each person
Assigning the ore to a shiftSplitting the audience at random between the two versions
H₀: the two shifts recover the sameH₀: the two ads work the same
α = 0.05α = 0.05

And there is a difference that works in its favour: in advertising you can assign at random. At the plant you do not choose which ore each shift gets, so you drag confounding variables along; when an audience is split at random, those variables split themselves and the conclusion gets much closer to a cause.

Three traps of that context, which are the module's own traps in other clothes:

  • Insufficient sample. Differences between two ads are usually small, so the day-shift problem reappears: a real effect and a high p for lack of data. That is why the sample size is calculated before launching the test.
  • Novelty effect. A new ad draws attention for being new. Measuring too soon measures the novelty and not the ad, and the difference fades weeks later.
  • Testing many versions at once. Comparing six ads two at a time is fifteen tests, and with α of 0.05 in each one the risk of a false winner shoots up. It is the multiple comparisons problem, and Course 4 solves it with ANOVA.

What does not change between a plant and an agency is the method. What changes is the cost of being wrong, and that is precisely what decides the α: rejecting a good reagent costs a test campaign; launching the wrong ad costs the quarter's budget.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. The test of the toy example, in three lines

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

dia = np.array([84, 86, 85, 88, 87])
noche = np.array([83, 85, 82, 86, 84])

t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(dia, noche, equal_var=False)
print(f"diferencia = {dia.mean() - noche.mean():.1f} puntos")
print(f"t = {t_stat:.4f}   p = {p_value:.4f}")
line by line
stats.ttest_ind(...)
The t-test for two independent groups, which is what the ind means. There is another version, ttest_rel, for when the data is paired (the same machine before and after). Using the wrong one gives a different result without warning.
dia, noche
The order matters for the sign: the function subtracts the second from the first. If you put them the other way round, t would come out negative and the conclusion would be the same, but the difference would change sign.
equal_var=False
Asks for the Welch test, which does not require the two groups to have the same variability. It is the safe default, as explained in the method.
t_stat, p_value =
The function returns two numbers and they are caught in two names. Always in that order: first the statistic, then the p-value.
prints
diferencia = 2.0 puntos
t = 2.0000   p = 0.0805

The same 2.0 and 0.0805 we got by hand.

Step 2. The decision, worded the way it should be worded

alpha = 0.05
if p_value < alpha:
    print("Se rechaza H0: hay evidencia de diferencia entre turnos.")
else:
    print("No se rechaza H0: no hay evidencia suficiente de diferencia.")
line by line
if ... :
A conditional. It reads "if this holds, do what is below". Just as in the loop of module 2, the colon announces a block and the indentation marks what belongs to it.
p_value < alpha
The comparison returns true or false, and that is what decides which branch runs.
else:
"And if it does not hold, do this other thing". Only one of the two branches runs, never both.
alpha = 0.05
The threshold is in a named variable, not typed by hand inside the if. That way it is changed in one place and it stays on record which one was used.
"No se rechaza H0..."
The text is worded on purpose. It does not say the shifts are equal, it says there is not enough evidence. That difference is the content of the module and it is worth having even the program's message respect it.
prints
No se rechaza H0: no hay evidencia suficiente de diferencia.

Step 3. The effect size, which turns the reading around

pooled = np.sqrt((dia.var(ddof=1) + noche.var(ddof=1)) / 2)
d = (dia.mean() - noche.mean()) / pooled
print(f"d de Cohen = {d:.4f}")
line by line
pooled
The combined deviation of the two groups, that is the typical variability of any one measurement regardless of which shift it comes from.
(var + var) / 2
Averaging the two variances. This shortcut only holds because the two groups have the same size; with different sizes you have to weight by the number of values in each.
ddof=1
The same argument as in module 1, and for the same reason: dividing by n minus one.
d = diferencia / pooled
The whole idea of the effect size: measuring the difference in units of the natural variability of the process, instead of in recovery points. That way it can be compared with any other study on any other subject.
prints
d de Cohen = 1.2649

A large effect the test does not detect. That is not a failure of statistics, it is a sign that more measurements are needed.

Step 4. Check that the problem was the sample size

dia_20 = np.tile(dia, 4)
noche_20 = np.tile(noche, 4)

t4, p4 = stats.ttest_ind(dia_20, noche_20, equal_var=False)
print(f"medias: {dia_20.mean():.1f} vs {noche_20.mean():.1f}  (identicas)")
print(f"t = {t4:.4f}   p = {p4:.6f}")
line by line
np.tile(dia, 4)
Repeats the array four times in a row. From five measurements they go to twenty, and the means and the deviation do not change at all because they are exactly the same values repeated.
:.6f
Six decimals, because with four the p-value would come out as 0.0001 and the real magnitude of the change would be lost.
prints
medias: 86.0 vs 84.0  (identicas)
t = 4.3589   p = 0.000096

This block is a controlled experiment on statistics itself: the only thing that moved was the number of measurements, and the verdict changed completely. It is what to remember when someone says "the test found no difference".

Step 5. Compare two proportions without extra libraries

When what you compare are rates and not means. For example, 120 conversions out of 1,000 users in version A against 150 out of 1,000 in B.

exitos = np.array([120, 150])
totales = np.array([1000, 1000])

p1, p2 = exitos / totales
combinada = exitos.sum() / totales.sum()
error = np.sqrt(combinada * (1 - combinada) * (1 / totales[0] + 1 / totales[1]))
z = (p1 - p2) / error

print(f"tasas: {p1:.1%} vs {p2:.1%}")
print(f"z = {z:.4f}   p = {2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z))):.4f}")
line by line
exitos / totales
Dividing two arrays divides them element by element: 120 by 1000 and 150 by 1000, in a single operation. With ordinary Python lists this would not work.
p1, p2 =
And the result, which is two numbers, is handed to two names.
combinada
The rate that would come out if the two groups were really the same one. The test starts by assuming there is no difference, so it estimates the error under that assumption.
totales[0]
Square brackets with a number pull out an element by its position. Python starts counting at zero, so [0] is the first and [1] the second.
abs(z)
The absolute value, that is without sign. It is needed because the tail is measured towards the same side regardless of whether the difference came out positive or negative.
2 * (1 - cdf(...))
The times two is what makes this a two-tailed test: the upper tail and the lower one are both counted. Removing that 2 would give a one-tailed test, with a p-value half as big.
prints
tasas: 12.0% vs 15.0%
z = -1.9630   p = 0.0496

Look at the result: p = 0.0496, below 0.05 by six thousandths. With two fewer conversions in version B it would have come out not significant. That teaches better than any speech that 0.05 is a convention and not a frontier of nature.

Traps

Watch out

  • The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true. It is the probability of seeing a result this extreme if H₀ were. It is a conditional statement about the data, not about the hypothesis.
  • "Not significant" is not "no difference". The toy example has a large effect, d of 1.26, and even so p comes out 0.08. The correct conclusion is that data is missing.
  • With an enormous sample, any trivial difference comes out significant. Automatidata gave a p of 6.8e-12 for a difference of $1.22 with a d of 0.09. Significant and irrelevant at the same time.
  • Never say "we accept H₀". Not rejecting is not proving. The correct wording is "there is not enough evidence".
  • Choosing a one-tailed test after seeing the data is cheating. The direction is decided beforehand and for a business reason, not because that way the p drops.
  • Lowering α to avoid false positives raises the false negatives. The two errors are in tension, and the only thing that improves both at once is more sample.
  • 0.05 is a convention. The proportions example comes out at 0.0496 and could have come out at 0.0504 with two fewer cases. Treating that frontier as sacred is an error of judgement.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

A hypothesis test is exactly deciding whether an operating change really moved the recovery, and the toy example is literally that.

Reacting to every two-point fluctuation as if it were a real signal, adjusting reagent every shift, is committing a type I error over and over. The result is not a better tuned plant: it is a more unstable plant, because you are chasing noise. That is the reason statistical process control exists.

And the pair of errors has its direct translation in quality control:

What it isWhat it costs
Type I errorRejecting a batch that did meet specificationUnnecessary reprocessing
Type II errorAccepting a batch that did not meet itCustomer complaint

No sampling protocol eliminates both at once without taking more samples. Each plant decides which of the two errors hurts it more, and that decision is exactly what statistics calls choosing α.

Review

Review

What exactly is the p-value?

It is the probability of observing a result as extreme as the one obtained, or more, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the result is chance in a general sense: it is a very specific conditional statement about the data under the assumption that there is no effect. A small p-value means that result would be rare in a world without a difference, which is evidence against that assumption.

In the shifts example there is a difference of 2 points and a large effect, but p comes out 0.08. What is concluded?

That with five measurements per shift there is not enough evidence to claim they differ, and nothing more. It is not concluded that they are equal. In fact the effect size, d of 1.26, is large, and the confidence interval runs from −0.31 to 4.31, which leaves plenty of room for a real difference. The correct reading is that the study lacks statistical power and what follows is taking more measurements, not dropping the suspicion.

How can a tiny p-value and an irrelevant effect coexist?

Because the p-value depends on the sample size as well as on the effect size. With 22,532 trips, the difference of $1.22 of the Automatidata case produces a p of 6.8 times ten to the minus twelve, and yet its Cohen's d is 0.09, a negligible effect. The test only answers whether the difference can be told from chance, not whether it is worth acting on. That is why the effect size is mandatory in any serious report.

Why is "we accept the null hypothesis" never said?

Because not rejecting it is not the same as proving it is true. It only means the available data was not enough to rule it out, and that is compatible both with there being no effect and with there being one that could not be detected. The shifts example shows the second case clearly: the effect was large and even so it was not detected. The correct wording, "there is not enough evidence", leaves that possibility open instead of closing it improperly.

You are going to compare the recovery of two reagents at the plant. What do you do before touching the data?

I write the null hypothesis, that both reagents give the same mean recovery, and the alternative, together with the significance level, all before seeing any result. I also decide the sample size by thinking about which difference I would care to detect, which is precisely the mistake the toy example made by stopping at five measurements. And I check that the assignment is not confounded with another variable, for example that one reagent has not been tried only on the night shift or only with ore from one particular zone, because then I would not know whether I am measuring the reagent or the shift.
