Automatidata

Management's hunch was right, but the effect size deflates it: the case that teaches that significant is not the same as important.

22,532 trips2 forms of paymentWelch + Mann-Whitneyα = 0.05
Brief

The brief

You work as an analyst for Automatidata, a data consultancy hired by the New York City Taxi and Limousine Commission (the NYC TLC), the body that regulates the city's yellow taxis.

Management has a hunch about revenue. It suspects that the form of payment changes what the customer ends up paying, and wants to know whether it is worth pushing card payment. Before setting up a campaign, someone has to check whether that hunch holds up with the real data.

Question

The question

Do customers who pay by credit card leave a higher fare than those who pay in cash?

The temptation is to calculate the two averages, see which is higher and call the matter settled. But a couple of dollars of difference in a sample can be pure chance of that sample. To claim that the pattern is real, a hypothesis test is needed.

Hypotheses

The hypotheses

They are written before looking at the results, not after:

  • Null hypothesis (H₀). There is no difference between the mean fare paid by card and the one paid in cash. It is the default position, the "nothing is going on here" one.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Hₐ). There is a real difference between the two.

The significance level is set at α = 0.05, the standard. It is the amount of risk we accept of shouting "there is a difference!" when in fact there was none.

Data

The data

New York yellow taxi trips, year 2017. The file holds 22,699 trips and the analysis works with the 22,532 that have an identified form of payment.

Exploration

What the exploration showed

One panel per group, so that each is read on its own scale, and then the two splits compared.

Panel 1. The card payments

The columns count how many trips fall in each fare band, with the axis on a logarithmic scale because rides go from a few dollars to close to a thousand.

The split into three bands says that half of the card trips are short rides of less than 10 dollars, 42% are medium and 8% are long.

There is also an isolated column on the right that does not fit the general shape: it is 514 trips of exactly 52.00 dollars, the flat fare of JFK airport. A spike that narrow in a histogram is almost never chance; it is almost always a business rule hidden in the data.

Panel 2. The cash payments

The same shape, and that resemblance is the story of the case. But the split changes a little: 57% of cash payments are short rides, against 50% of the card ones.

As in the other case, the vertical scale of each panel is its own, because there are 15,265 card trips and 7,267 cash ones.

And here appears, drawn, the confounding factor the limitations section talks about: cash concentrates in the short rides and card in the long ones. Since long rides cost more, part of the difference in mean fare could come from distance and not from the form of payment. This analysis cannot separate the two things.

How to read this figure

This figure did not exist in the original project and it is the one that best sums up the case.

The dot marks the observed difference, 1.22 dollars. The bar is the 95% confidence interval, the range where the real difference can be. The vertical line is zero, the position of "no difference".

Here the bar does not touch zero, so the difference is real. But look at the other half of the message: the whole interval lives between 0.87 and 1.56 dollars. The difference is not only real, it is small and well bounded. This figure is what stops the finding being sold as something bigger than it is.

Method

The test, and why this test

The means of two independent groups are compared, so a two-sample t-test is called for. Within that family a choice has to be made, and the choice gets justified.

First, check whether the variances are equal

The classic t-test, Student's, assumes that both groups have the same spread. That is verified with Levene's test:

prints
Levene: estadístico = 9.6815     p = 1.86e-03

That p = 0.00186 is less than 0.05, so the variances are not equal. It makes sense: it could already be seen in the panels that the card group reaches further towards the high fares.

That is why Welch is used, not Student

Since the equal-variances assumption does not hold, Welch's t-test (equal_var=False) is used, which does not require it. Using Student here would have given a pretend precision.

And normality, with distributions as skewed as those in the histogram? Enter the central limit theorem of module 3: with more than 22,000 trips, the distribution of the means is approximately normal even if the individual trips are not.

And then it is checked that the result is not a mirage

The analysis does not stop at a single test. It repeats the contrast removing the outliers by the IQR rule, and adds Mann-Whitney U, which compares ranks instead of means and assumes no distribution shape at all:

Welch, without outliers (n₁=13,778 / n₂=6,711)t = 8.4911, p = 2.26e-17Reject H₀

All three agree. The difference is not an artefact of the extreme values nor of the distribution not being normal.

Result

The result, translated

prints
Welch t = 6.8668          p = 6.80e-12
IC 95% de la diferencia = [0.87 , 1.56] dólares
Cohen's d = 0.0922

What each line means:

  • t = 6.87. The means are separated by almost seven standard errors. From 2 upwards it is already considered a notable separation.
  • p = 6.80 × 10⁻¹². The probability of seeing a difference this big **if in fact there were none. In decimals it is 0.0000000000068. For practical purposes, zero. H₀ is rejected.**
  • 95% confidence interval = [$0.87 , $1.56]. The real difference is somewhere in that range. It does not include zero, which is another way of seeing the same result.
  • Cohen's d = 0.0922. And here comes the interesting part.

The lesson that makes this case valuable

A p with twelve zeros seems to shout "enormous difference!". But Cohen's d = 0.0922 is a small effect: by convention, below 0.2 it is considered almost imperceptible.

How can both things be true at once? Because the p-value depends on the sample size. With 22,532 trips, even a minuscule difference becomes statistically certain. The confidence interval confirms it: the real difference is between 87 cents and 1.56 dollars, a small and well-bounded range, not a spectacular jump.

Said in one sentence: the difference is real and consistent, but modest. Confusing "statistically significant" with "important" is the mistake this case teaches not to make.

Decision

What gets decided with this

Yes, it is worth encouraging card payment, but with realistic expectations. Electronic payment is associated with higher fares in a statistically reliable way, and at the same time the effect is around 1.22 dollars per trip. Nobody should promise a jump in revenue from promoting cards.

Translated into concrete actions:

  1. Signage inside the taxis inviting card payment, presented as convenience and speed.
  2. Budget the impact with the real number, not with the impression the p-value leaves.
  3. Investigate the confounding factors before drawing causal conclusions.
Limits

Limitations

  • It is an observational study, not an experiment. Nobody assigned at random who pays by card. One can claim that an association exists, not that paying by card causes a higher fare.
  • And there is an obvious confounding factor: long trips cost more and also tend to be paid by card, because nobody carries sixty dollars in cash. Distance could explain the whole difference, and this analysis does not control for it.
  • The effect size is small. Any business decision should start from the 1.22 dollars and the interval [0.87, 1.56], not from the p-value.
  • The data is from 2017 and from a single city. The split between cash and card has changed a lot since then.
