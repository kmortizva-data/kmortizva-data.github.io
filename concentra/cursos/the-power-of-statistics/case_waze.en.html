Waze

The only one of the three where the answer is that there is no difference, and why that is a complete result and not a failed project.

14,999 users2 devicesWelch + Mann-Whitneyα = 0.05
Brief

The brief

You work on the data team at Waze, the navigation app. The business problem occupying the company is user churn: people who install the app and stop using it.

The original project is above all a prediction job, with logistic regression, Random Forest and XGBoost. But in the middle of all that there is a single formal hypothesis test, and it is the one examined here: does the kind of phone influence how much the app gets used?

And this case has a peculiarity that makes it the most instructive of the three. The original notebook ran the test with equal_var=False without justifying why, and without any of the backups a complete analysis requires: no Levene, no Mann-Whitney, no effect size and no confidence interval. Here those gaps get filled.

Question

The question

Is the number of drives (drives) different between iPhone users and Android users?

If it were, it would have product consequences: it would justify prioritising development on one platform over the other.

Hypotheses

The hypotheses

  • Null hypothesis (H₀). There is no difference in the mean number of drives between iPhone and Android users.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Hₐ). There is a real difference.

Significance level: α = 0.05.

Data

The data

14,999 users of the app, from a public Kaggle dataset.

GroupUsersWeight
iPhone9,67264.5%
Android5,32735.5%

The variable measured is drives, the number of drives of each user.

Right from the start, the means come in very close: 67.86 drives on iPhone against 66.23 on Android, with medians of 48 and 47. The difference is 1.63 drives, 2.5% relative. For comparison: in Automatidata the difference was 10%, and in TikTok 65%. Here the starting point is already almost nothing.

Exploration

What the exploration showed

Panel 2. The Android users

The same shape, the same bands, and its own vertical scale because there are 9,672 iPhone users against 5,327 Android ones.

The percentages are practically identical, down to the tenth in the first band. This table demonstrates the absence of a difference much better than two shapes that look alike, because it compares proportions and not bar heights.

If the device influenced how much the app gets used, it would show here before it showed in any statistical test.

How to read this figure

This is the figure that sums up the whole case, and the one that did not exist in the original project.

The dot is the observed difference between the two groups, 1.63 drives. The bar around it is the 95% confidence interval, that is the range where the real difference can be. And the vertical line marks zero, which is the position of "no difference".

The whole result reads at a glance: the bar goes through zero. The real difference could be negative, could be nil, could be almost four drives. With that uncertainty it cannot be claimed that a difference exists, and that is why the figure is in grey and not in colour: grey is the signal that there is no finding here.

Method

The test, and why this test

First, the split check

prints
SRM (chi²) = 1,258.6856     p = 1.08e-275     FAIL

Just as with the verification status in TikTok, this "failure" is expected and invalidates nothing. The device is the user's real preference, that is, which phone they bought, not a random traffic assignment. Comparing a 64.5/35.5 split against a theoretical 50/50 is always going to fail. The only thing it confirms is that in this sample there are more iPhones than Androids.

Second, the variances, and here it changes from the other cases

prints
Levene: estadístico = 2.3596     p = 0.1245

This p = 0.1245 is greater than 0.05, so the variances do seem equal. It is the opposite of Automatidata, where Levene gave p = 0.0019.

That means the classic Student's t-test would have been technically valid here. Even so Welch is used, which is what the original notebook already had: when the variances are equal, Welch gives practically the same result as Student, so no price is paid for using it unnecessarily. It is the safe default.

Third, the three validations

ValidationResultDecision
Welch, full datat = 1.4635, p = 0.1434H₀ not rejected
Welch, without outliers (n₁=9,187 / n₂=5,078)t = 0.7756, p = 0.4380H₀ not rejected
Mann-Whitney UU = 25,964,593.5, p = 0.4232H₀ not rejected

All three agree, and this time they agree on not rejecting. The robustness here confirms the absence of an effect, which is a result as valid as the opposite one.

The original notebook reported t = 1.4057 and p = 0.1599, similar but not identical. The difference comes from the test there being run after trimming the outliers at the 95th percentile in several columns, whereas here drives is used untouched. The conclusion is the same in both cases.
Result

The result, translated

prints
Welch t = 1.4635          p = 0.1434
IC 95% de la diferencia = [-0.55 , 3.81] viajes
Cohen's d = 0.0247
  • p = 0.1434. It is greater than α = 0.05, so H₀ is not rejected. Mind how it is said: it has not been shown that the groups are equal, there is simply not enough evidence to claim that they are different.
  • 95% confidence interval = [−0.55 , 3.81]. This is the most eloquent piece of data: the interval crosses zero. The real difference could be negative, could be nil or could be almost four drives. With that uncertainty nothing can be claimed.
  • Cohen's d = 0.0247. A practically nil effect.

The three signals point the same way

And here is the value of having the three cases together, because each one illustrates a different combination:

Casep-valueCohen's dWhat it means
TikTok2.61e-120−0.544 (medium)There is a difference, and it matters
Automatidata6.80e-120.0922 (small)There is a difference, but it barely matters
Waze0.14340.0247 (nil)No evidence of a difference

Waze is the cleanest case of the three: large p-value, almost nil effect size and an interval that crosses zero. When statistical and practical significance agree, the conclusion admits no argument.

Decision

What gets decided with this

Do not prioritise platform-specific development based on the number of drives. On this metric, iPhone and Android behave the same.

And two recommendations that come from looking at the complete project:

  1. The real business question, churn, still has no formal hypothesis test. The classification model is a complementary approach, not a substitute. Its recall on test is 0.19, which means it lets through most of the users who are actually going to abandon the app.
  2. If this same framework is to be applied to churn, the grouping variable should be label (churned or stayed) on variables like activity_days or km_per_driving_day. The exploratory analysis itself already suggests there is signal there: 7.6% churn among professional drivers against 19.9% in the rest.
Limits

Limitations

  • "No evidence of a difference" is not the same as "no difference". With a larger sample a small effect could appear. What can be claimed is that, if it exists, it is too small for these 14,999 users to detect, and from the confidence interval we know it would not reach four drives.
  • The device was not assigned at random. Whoever buys an iPhone may differ from the Android user in income, country or age, and any of those things could influence use. It is an observational study.
  • drives was analysed, not churn. The test answers a side question, not the main business question, something worth saying out loud when presenting it.
  • The result depends on what is done with the outliers. With the full data it comes out p = 0.1434 and without outliers p = 0.4380. Both point the same way, but they show that the treatment of the extremes moves the numbers, and that is why it is documented.
