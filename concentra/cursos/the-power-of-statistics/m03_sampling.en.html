Sampling

The hinge module of the course. With six drill holes and all their possible pairs you watch the central limit theorem being born without a single formula, and why the only way to fix a biased sample is not to make it bigger.

5 h course time11 videos7 readings4 assignments3 practice2 interactive
Context

What this module solves

Here the probability of module 2 connects with the real inference of modules 4 and 5. The underlying question is the one that holds up all of applied statistics: how can a small sample speak on behalf of an enormous population?

There are two answers and both matter. One is mathematical, the central limit theorem, which explains why averages behave predictably even when the data does not. The other is not fixed with mathematics: how you chose the sample. If the selection method is biased, no later calculation saves it.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

A tiny deposit with six drill holes, and their copper grades in percent:

1   2   3   4   5   6

Look at the shape of this population: it is flat. Each value appears exactly once. There is no bell, no prominent centre, nothing at all like a normal distribution.

The mean of the population is easy:

(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 21 / 6 = 3.5

Step 1. Now draw samples of two holes, all the possible ones

We are going to take samples of size 2, putting the hole back each time. That gives 6 × 6 = 36 possible pairs. For each one we calculate its mean. For example, the pair (1, 6) has mean 3.5; the pair (2, 2) has mean 2.

Step 2. Look at what just happened

The population was flat. The means of the samples form a peak.

Without doing anything more than averaging two at a time, a shape with a centre and tails appeared. That is the central limit theorem, and you have just watched it happen with primary-school arithmetic. With samples of 5 or of 30 instead of 2, that peak would look more and more like the normal bell.

Why does it happen? Because for the mean to come out 1.0 two extremes have to coincide (1 and 1), and there is only one way to get that. For it to come out 3.5 there are six different combinations (1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4, and their reverses). The extreme values cancel each other out and the central ones pile up.

Step 3. Two more things that come out of the same table

The mean of the means is exactly the mean of the population. If you average the 36 means, you get 3.5. No more, no less. The sample mean is not biased: on average it gets it right.

But the means spread less than the data. The population stretches from 1 to 6, while the means crowd around 3.5. Putting numbers on that spread:

desviación de la población  = 1.7078
desviación de las medias    = 1.2076

And now the beautiful part. That second figure does not need to be calculated by counting the 36 pairs, because there is a formula that gives it directly:

1.7078 / √2 = 1.7078 / 1.4142 = 1.2076

It matches exactly. That formula, the deviation of the population divided by the root of the sample size, is called the standard error, and it is the piece on which the whole of module 4 is built.

Step 4. Why sampling more stops paying off

Since the formula divides by the root of n, and not by n, the benefit runs out:

To cut the error in half you have to quadruple the sample. Going from 2 to 8 is well worth it; going from 32 to 128 costs four times as much and only improves by the same amount again. In a drilling campaign that is the difference between a sensible budget and an absurd one.

Terms

Glossary

  • Population. The complete set you care about. Our six drill holes, or the millions of users of an app.
  • Sample. The subset you actually measure.
  • Parameter. A number that describes the population, like its real mean. It is almost never known.
  • Statistic. The same number calculated on the sample. It is the estimate you do have.
  • Probability sampling. Every element has a known, non-zero probability of being chosen. It is what allows generalising with rigour.
  • Non-probability sampling. The selection does not follow controlled chance, like surveying whoever walks past. Cheaper, and it does not generalise the same way.
  • Simple random sampling. Everyone has the same probability, with no further structure.
  • Stratified sampling. The population is divided into subgroups and sampled within each one, guaranteeing that none is left out.
  • Cluster sampling. Whole groups are chosen at random and everything inside the chosen ones is sampled.
  • Sampling bias. When the method systematically favours a certain kind of element. It is not corrected with more data, only with a better method.
  • Sampling distribution. The distribution the statistics form when the sampling is repeated many times. Our table of 36 means is exactly that.
  • Central limit theorem. The sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal as the sample size grows, regardless of the shape of the population.
  • Standard error. The standard deviation of the sampling distribution. It is calculated as the deviation of the population divided by the root of the sample size.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. The sampling process has five stages, and they go in order

Before the details, the complete sequence. Each stage rests on the previous one, and skipping one gives no error: it gives a result that answers another question.

StageWhat gets decidedWhere it is explained
1. Identify the target populationWho you want to talk about in the endRight below
2. Define the sampling frameWhere you can really draw fromStep 3
3. Choose the methodHow the individuals get selectedStep 2
4. Determine the sizeHow many are neededStep 5
5. Draw the sample and check itThat what was asked for came outRight below

The first stage is the one most taken for granted and the one most often got wrong. "The deposit" is not a population: you have to say whether it is all the blocks of the model, only the ones in the oxide zone, or only the ones going into the plant this year. Everything else depends on that sentence, and if it changes halfway through the analysis, the sampling stops being valid.

And the fifth is not a formality. Once the sample is drawn, you check that it looks like what was asked for: that the strata came out in the planned proportion, that no whole zone is missing, that the final size is the one calculated and not the one left after discarding. It is the practice of validating from the previous course, applied to sampling.

Step 2. Choose the method before touching a single data point

MethodWhen it makes sense
Simple randomHomogeneous population with a complete list available
StratifiedThere are subgroups you cannot afford to lose, by zone or by type
ClusterThe population is scattered and visiting every point is expensive
Non-probabilityQuick exploration, knowing you will not be able to generalise

Step 3. Ask yourself who is NOT in your sample

This is the question that saves analyses. An internet survey excludes whoever has no internet. A satisfaction survey answered voluntarily excludes whoever was so unhappy they did not even reply. Surface sampling excludes the fresh rock at depth.

And the important part: that is not fixed by sampling more. A million online surveys still do not include whoever has no internet.

Step 4. Lean on the central limit theorem for the rest

You have already seen it works. The practical consequence is enormous: you do not need to know what shape your variable has to make inferences about its mean. It can be skewed, it can have two peaks, it does not matter; with enough sample, the distribution of the means behaves like a normal and you can use the formulas that assume it.

The practical rule is n ≥ 30, with a caveat: very skewed populations need more.

Step 5. Calculate the standard error to know what your estimate is worth

It is the translation of "how firm is this average?" into a number. And remember the lesson of step 4 of the example: to improve it by half you have to quadruple the effort.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Manufacture a deliberately ugly population

In the toy example the population was flat, which was already quite un-normal. Now the extreme case: many small values and a long tail upwards, which is how incomes, waiting times or the views of a video behave.

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
population = rng.exponential(scale=1000, size=100_000)

print(f"media de la poblacion   = {population.mean():.1f}")
print(f"mediana de la poblacion = {np.median(population):.1f}")

They are not identical because they are a thousand samples and not infinite, but the difference is 3%.

Traps

Watch out

  • More sample does not fix bias. It is the trap that costs the most. If the selection method excludes a group, enlarging the sample only produces more data from the same wrong group.
  • The theorem talks about the mean, not about the data. The individual values stay as skewed as ever. What becomes normal is the distribution of the repeated means, which is something else.
  • The n ≥ 30 rule is a guide, not a law. Very skewed populations or ones with extreme values need more for the approximation to be reasonable.
  • The standard error and the standard deviation are not the same thing. One talks about how much the data varies, the other about how much your average would vary. Confusing them produces absurdly wide confidence intervals in module 4.
  • Convenience sampling is tempting and almost always biased. Taking the material that is closest to hand, or surveying whoever answers first, favours whoever is most available, who rarely represents the whole.
  • Quadrupling the sample only halves the error. There is a point where continuing to sample no longer pays for what it costs, and it is worth knowing how to calculate it before presenting the budget.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

This whole module is the theory of mine sampling, and even the names match.

In statisticsIn the deposit
Simple random samplingRandom aliquots from a homogeneous stream
Stratified samplingSampling by geological domain or by lithology, so as not to average zones that are chemically different
Cluster samplingChoosing a few benches at random and sampling everything inside, when visiting every point of the pit is hugely expensive
Sampling biasA probe that only reaches the shallow oxidised zone, or a badly placed stream cutter that always grabs the coarse fraction

And that last case is the one that hurts most, because drilling twice as much does not fix it. If the cutter is badly placed, a thousand badly taken samples are a thousand bad samples. The cutter has to be moved. It is exactly the trap of the previous section, said in plant language.

The central limit theorem, for its part, is the mathematical justification of something you do without thinking: compositing. An individual drill sample is erratic and not very reliable, but the composite of several behaves predictably and allows putting a confidence interval on the mean grade. That is what makes it possible to declare reserves under JORC or NI 43-101, and it is literally the table of 36 means of the toy example, applied to a real deposit.

Review

Review

What exactly does the central limit theorem say?

That the distribution of the means of many samples approaches a normal as the size of each sample grows, regardless of what shape the original population has. It does not say the individual values become normal: it says their averages do. In the toy example it shows in a single pass, because a perfectly flat population produces, when averaged two at a time, a distribution with a central peak and tails.

Why is it so important that the population does not have to be normal?

Because almost no real variable is. Incomes, waiting times, the views of a video or the grades of a deposit are skewed. If inference demanded normality of the data, it could almost never be applied. Since the theorem moves the demand to the distribution of the means, which does become normal, the formulas based on the bell work on data that has nothing of a bell about it.

A company surveys only whoever answers its satisfaction email. Is it enough to send more emails?

No. The problem is not in the size but in the method: whoever answers voluntarily does not represent the whole customer base, because the very satisfied and the very annoyed tend to reply, while the indifferent majority stays out. Sending more emails produces more responses of the same biased kind. The fix goes through a probability method, like selecting at random whom to contact and following up actively to raise the response rate.

Why does quadrupling the sample not cut the error to a quarter?

Because the standard error divides by the root of the sample size, and the root grows more slowly than the number. Multiplying n by four multiplies its root by two, so the error is cut to half, not to a quarter. The practical consequence is that past a certain size each new data point adds less and less precision, and in a drilling campaign that determines when to stop drilling.

What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

The standard deviation describes how much the individual values vary around their mean. The standard error describes how much the mean itself would vary if you repeated the sampling. In the toy example, the population spread 1.7078 while the means of the pairs only 1.2076, and that second figure comes from dividing the first by the root of 2. The whole of module 4 is built on the standard error, never on the standard deviation.
