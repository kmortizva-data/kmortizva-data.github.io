Context

What this module solves

Before testing hypotheses you have to know how to describe. This module separates two questions that get confused all the time: what does this data say? and what can I say about everything I did not measure, from this sample?

The first is descriptive statistics and is solved with arithmetic. The second is inferential statistics, it comes with uncertainty, and it is where the rest of the course lives.

Here you learn the three families of numbers that describe any variable: where the centre is, how much it spreads, and where each value falls relative to the rest.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Six samples from a lot of ore, with their copper grade in percent. Already sorted from smallest to largest so we do not get dizzy:

1.8   2.0   2.0   2.2   2.4   7.6

Five samples hover around 2%, and there is one at 7.6% that is out of line with everything. We are going to describe this lot in three ways and you will see that they give different answers.

Step 1. Where is the centre? Three answers

The mean is adding up and dividing:

(1.8 + 2.0 + 2.0 + 2.2 + 2.4 + 7.6) / 6  =  18.0 / 6  =  3.00

The median is the value in the middle. With six values there is no single one in the centre, so the two central ones, the third and the fourth, are averaged:

(2.0 + 2.2) / 2  =  2.10

The mode is the value that repeats most. Here 2.0 appears twice and the others once:

moda = 2.00

Three numbers for "the centre" of the same lot: 3.00 · 2.10 · 2.00.

Step 2. Why does the mean say 3.00 if almost every sample is close to 2?

Because that sample at 7.6 pulls on it. The mean shares the total out among everyone, so one extreme value drags it upwards even if it is a single one out of six.

Check it by removing that sample: the mean of the other five is 2.08, and the median barely moves to 2.00. The median hardly notices there was a 7.6, because it only cares about the order, not the magnitude.

This is the most profitable lesson of the module. If someone gives you the mean of a lot and does not give you the median, you do not know whether they are describing the lot or a handful of odd values.

Step 3. How much do they spread?

The simplest thing is the range, maximum minus minimum:

7.6 − 1.8 = 5.8

But the range uses only two values out of six, so one odd value wrecks it. The measure that uses all of them is the variance. It is calculated by looking at how far each value is from the mean (3.00), squaring so the distances do not cancel each other out, and averaging:

GradeDistance to 3.00Squared

The sum of the last column is 25.60. Notice that a single sample contributes 21.16 of those 25.60: 83% of all the spread comes from the odd value.

We divide by 5, which is the number of values minus one (the why is in the traps):

varianza = 25.60 / 5 = 5.12

And since the variance is in "percent squared", which means nothing, the root is taken to get back to the original units. That is the standard deviation:

√5.12 = 2.263

Step 4. Where does each value fall relative to the others?

The quartiles split the sorted data into four equal parts:

Q1 = 2.00      Q2 = 2.10 (la mediana)      Q3 = 2.35

The distance between the first and the third is called the interquartile range, and it encloses the central half of the data:

IQR = 2.35 − 2.00 = 0.35

With that, odd values get detected automatically. The usual rule flags as suspicious anything more than 1.5 IQR outside the box:

límite superior = 2.35 + 1.5 × 0.35 = 2.875

Our 7.6 is far above 2.875, so the rule flags it without anyone having to look. That is the mechanism behind the box plot.

Terms

Glossary

  • Descriptive statistics. Summarising the data you do have. It says nothing about what you did not measure.
  • Inferential statistics. Using a sample to draw conclusions about a population you did not measure in full. It always comes with uncertainty.
  • Population. The complete set you care about, like all the ore in the deposit. It is almost never measured in full.
  • Sample. The subset you do measure, our six grades.
  • Measures of central tendency. The formal name of the three that come next: mean, median and mode. All three answer "what is the typical value?" and **they answer different things when the data is not symmetric**, which is exactly what the toy example teaches. That is how they appear in the quizzes.
  • Mean. The sum divided by the count. Sensitive to extreme values.
  • Median. The middle value once sorted. Resists the extremes.
  • Mode. The most frequent value. It is the only measure of centre that works with categories.
  • Which of the three to use. The median with skewed or dirty data, which is the default case at the plant. The mean when the distribution is symmetric and you need to operate with it afterwards. The mode when the data is categorical and the other two cannot even be calculated.
  • Measures of dispersion. The other group, the one that says how far the data spreads from that centre: range, variance, standard deviation and interquartile range. A centre without a spread next to it describes nothing.
  • Range. Maximum minus minimum. It uses only two values, so it breaks easily.
  • Variance. The average of the squared distances to the mean. It is in squared units, which is why it is almost never reported directly.
  • Standard deviation. The root of the variance. It says how much a typical value varies around the mean, in the original units.
  • Percentile. The value below which a percentage of the data falls. The 90th percentile leaves 90% below.
  • Quartile. The three percentiles that split the data into four: Q1 (25%), Q2 (50%, which is the median) and Q3 (75%).
  • Interquartile range (IQR). Q3 minus Q1. It encloses the central half of the data.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Decide whether your question is descriptive or inferential

If you ask "what is my data like?", it is descriptive and is answered with arithmetic. If you ask "what can I claim about something I did not measure?", it is inferential and you need everything that comes in modules 2 to 5. Confusing them leads to claiming things about a population that only hold for your sample.

Step 2. For the centre, calculate all three and compare them

Do not pick one up front. Calculate mean, median and mode, and look at the distance between the mean and the median, which is a free problem detector:

SituationWhat to report
Mean and median similarThe mean works, the distribution is reasonably symmetric
Mean well above the medianThere are high values pulling on it; report the median
Mean well below the medianThe same, but with low values
Categorical dataOnly the mode, the other two do not exist

Step 3. For the spread, the standard deviation, with the range as a warning

Report the standard deviation, because it is in the units of the data and can be interpreted. Look at the range only as an alarm signal: if it is huge compared with the standard deviation, there is some extreme value around.

Step 4. For position, quartiles and the IQR rule

The quartiles give you the shape without having to draw, and the 1.5 IQR rule flags the candidates for outlier. Mind the word candidate: the rule flags them, it does not decide whether to remove them. That is decided with domain judgement.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Load the tools and put the data in

Python on its own knows nothing about statistics. You have to borrow two toolboxes and store our six grades in something that can be calculated with.

from scipy import stats

grades = np.array([1.8, 2.0, 2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 7.6])

Step 2. The three measures of centre

Now the three answers of the toy example, calculated by the computer.

print(f"media   = {grades.mean():.2f}")
print(f"mediana = {np.median(grades):.2f}")
print(f"moda    = {stats.mode(grades, keepdims=False).mode:.2f}")

Shows something on screen. Without this the computer calculates but shows you nothing.

A string of text "with holes". The f in front warns that whatever goes between braces has to be calculated and placed in there.

Inside the braces goes what gets calculated. grades.mean() is "to the data stored in grades, apply the mean".
.mean()
The dot means "ask this of". The empty parentheses mean "do it now, with no further data".

Format: show the number with 2 decimals. Without it you would get 3.0 instead of 3.00.

Here the form changes: the median is asked of numpy by passing it the data in parentheses, instead of asking the data itself. Both forms coexist in Python and there is no rule; you learn which one each function uses.
stats.mode(...)

The function returns two things (the value and how many times it appears). With .mode we ask only for the first.

The same three numbers we got by hand. And the problem already shows in the output: if you had printed only the mean, you would walk away with 3.00 as "the grade of the lot".

Step 3. The spread, and the argument almost nobody explains

print(f"rango       = {grades.max() - grades.min():.2f}")
print(f"varianza    = {grades.var(ddof=1):.3f}")
print(f"desv. est.  = {grades.std(ddof=1):.3f}")
line by line
grades.max() - grades.min()
The largest minus the smallest. Operations can be chained inside the braces as in a calculator.
.var(ddof=1)
The variance. This argument really matters.
ddof=1
Tells numpy to divide by n − 1 (by 5) and not by n (by 6), which is exactly what we did by hand. Without writing it, numpy divides by 6 and returns a number smaller than the correct one, without any warning.
.std(ddof=1)
The standard deviation, with the same care. It is the root of the variance.
:.3f
Here we ask for 3 decimals instead of 2, because the variance needs more precision to look right.
prints
rango       = 5.80
varianza    = 5.120
desv. est.  = 2.263

That ddof=1 is the perfect example of why it pays to read the code. It is a tiny detail that changes the result and raises no error if you forget it.

Step 4. Position and the automatic hunt for outliers

q1, q3 = np.percentile(grades, [25, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
limite = q3 + 1.5 * iqr

print(f"Q1 = {q1:.2f}   Q3 = {q3:.2f}   IQR = {iqr:.2f}")
print(f"límite superior = {limite:.3f}")
print(f"atípicos: {grades[grades > limite]}")
line by line
np.percentile(grades, [25, 75])
Asks for two percentiles at once: the one that leaves 25% of the data below and the one that leaves 75%.
q1, q3 =
Since the function returns two values, Python hands them to two names at once. It is the same as writing two lines, one for each.
iqr = q3 - q1
Here you see what variables are for: q3 and q1 already hold their numbers, so you operate with the names and not with the figures.
1.5 * iqr
The asterisk is multiply. The 1.5 is the constant of the rule, it comes from no calculation: it is a convention.
grades > limite
Compares each of the six numbers with the limit and returns a list of true or false, one per value.
grades[...]
Square brackets after an array mean "give me only these". Putting the comparison inside keeps the ones that came out true.
prints
Q1 = 2.00   Q3 = 2.35   IQR = 0.35
límite superior = 2.875
atípicos: [7.6]

The last line is the most interesting one as code: grades[grades > limite] is read from the inside out, and the rule finds the 7.6 sample on its own without anyone telling it where to look.

Traps

Watch out

  • The mean alone is an incomplete and sometimes misleading summary. Two datasets with the same mean can have completely different shapes. Always report at least mean, median and standard deviation.
  • We divide by n − 1, not by n. The intuitive reason is that you already spent one value calculating the mean, so you have one degree of freedom less of independent information. Dividing by n underestimates the real spread of the population.
  • The variance is not reported, the standard deviation is. A variance of "5.12 percent squared" means nothing intuitive; a deviation of 2.26 grade points is understood.
  • The range breaks with a single bad value. Never use it alone.
  • Percentile is not percentage. Being in the 90th percentile means you beat 90% of the sample, not that you scored 90 out of 100.
  • The mode barely exists with continuous data. If you measure with enough decimals, no two values are equal and the mode loses its meaning. It works for categories or for rounded data.
  • The IQR rule flags candidates, not culprits. In our example the 7.6 could be a laboratory error or a genuinely rich vein, and statistics cannot tell them apart: that is decided by whoever knows the deposit.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The difference between descriptive and inferential is the one between the shift report and the declared reserve of the deposit.

The shift report is pure description: the mean grade processed, the deviation of the tonnage, the 90th percentile of reagent consumption. It describes what happened and generalises nothing.

The declared reserve is inference: you drilled a few holes and from there you infer the grade of a whole deposit you will never measure in full. That is why reserves are classified as measured, indicated and inferred, according to how much confidence there is in that extrapolation. Modules 3 and 4 of this course are the statistical formalisation of that same problem.

And the toy example is literally a sampling campaign. If you report to the client that the lot has 3.00% copper because one sample gave 7.6, you are selling a deposit that does not exist. The median of 2.10 describes what is really there. The difference between the two figures is the difference between an honest report and a lawsuit.

Review

Review

What is the difference between descriptive and inferential statistics?

Descriptive statistics summarises the data that was actually measured, with mean, spread and charts, without generalising beyond it. Inferential statistics uses a sample to draw conclusions about a population that was not measured in full, and that is why it always comes with a quantification of the uncertainty. The whole course from module 2 onwards builds the tools to do that inference rigorously, and this first module is the descriptive base it rests on.

You have a lot with mean 3.00 and median 2.10. What is going on and what do you report?

There are high values pulling the mean upwards, because the mean is sensitive to extremes and the median is not. I would report the median as the main measure, because it describes the typical sample better, and mention the mean together with the existence of those extreme values, which are often the interesting finding. Reporting only the mean would give an idea of the lot between 40% and 50% richer than it really is.

Why is the variance divided by n − 1 and not by n?

Because the mean used to calculate the distances was estimated from the same data, so one of the values stops contributing independent information: n − 1 degrees of freedom remain. Dividing by n produces an estimate systematically smaller than the real spread of the population. In numpy and pandas this is controlled with ddof=1, and forgetting it is a silent error that gives no warning.

Why is the standard deviation reported and not the variance?

Because the variance is in squared units of the original variable and cannot be interpreted intuitively: nobody knows what "5.12 percent copper squared" means. The standard deviation is its square root, returns to the original units, and allows saying something understandable like "the grades vary about 2.3 points around the mean". It is the same number, expressed on a scale that can be communicated.

The IQR rule flags a value as an outlier. Do you remove it?

Not automatically. The rule is a detector, not a judge: it points out that the value is far from the bulk of the data, but it cannot know why. Before deciding you have to investigate whether it is a demonstrable measurement error, in which case it gets documented and handled, or a real and extreme value, in which case it is often the most valuable data point in the set. In a deposit, a sky-high grade can be sample contamination or a genuinely rich zone, and that difference is settled with knowledge of the ground, not with a formula.
