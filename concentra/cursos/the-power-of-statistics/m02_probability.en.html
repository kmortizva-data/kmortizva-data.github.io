Probability

The language hypothesis tests are written in. With a bag of ten drill cores conditional probability gets built, and then Bayes' theorem shows that the same analyser can be right 66% or 19% of the time depending on what you already knew.

Context

What this module solves

Without probability, a p-value is a magic word. With probability, it is a number that can be calculated and argued about.

The module has two halves. The first is the rules: how events combine, what changes when one depends on another, and how a belief gets updated when new evidence arrives. The second is the distributions: the three moulds that describe almost any real phenomenon, and when to use each one.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

A bag with ten drill cores. Three are mineralised and seven are barren:

M  M  M  E  E  E  E  E  E  E

You reach in without looking. The whole module gets built with this.

Step 1. The simplest probability

You take one out. What is the probability that it is mineralised?

casos favorables / casos posibles = 3 / 10 = 0.30

30%. That is the entire definition: how many of the possible outcomes work for you.

Step 2. You take two, and here the interesting part appears

You take out a core, it turns out mineralised, and you do not put it back. Now you take another. What is the probability that this second one is mineralised too?

There are no longer ten cores, there are nine. And there are no longer three mineralised, there are two:

2 / 9 = 0.2222

It dropped from 30% to 22.2%, and not because the bag changed by magic, but because you took information out. That is a conditional probability: the probability of something given that you already know something else. It is written P(segundo M | primero M), and the vertical bar reads "given that".

Step 3. And the probability that both come out mineralised?

They get multiplied, but with care. The second one has to be the conditional, because the first one already changed the bag:

P(los dos) = 3/10 × 2/9 = 6/90 = 0.0667

6.7%. Now compare it with what would happen if you put back the first core in the bag before taking the second:

P(los dos) = 3/10 × 3/10 = 0.09

9%. The same question, two different answers, and the only difference is whether you put the core back. When putting it back changes nothing for the next one, the events are independent. When it does change something, they are dependent and the conditional has to be used.

Step 4. Bayes, or how to turn the question around

Now what really matters. You have a portable analyser that detects mineralisation. You know two things about it:

  • When a core is mineralised, it gets it right 90% of the time.
  • When it is not, it gets it wrong and gives a positive 20% of the time.

You run the analyser over a core from the bag and it gives a positive. The million-dollar question: what is the probability that this core is really mineralised?

The intuitive answer is "90%, which is what the device gets right". And it is wrong. Let us count it with concrete cores. Imagine 100 cores from this bag:

How many there areGive a positiveHow many positives
Mineralised3090%27
Barren7020%14
Total positives41

Of the 41 cores that give a positive, only 27 are truly mineralised:

27 / 41 = 0.6585  →  65.9 %

It is not 90%, it is 65.9%. The 14 false positives come from the fact that there are many more barren cores than mineralised ones, so even a 20% error on them produces a pile of false positives in absolute terms.

Step 5. And now what makes Bayes indispensable

Repeat the count in a poor zone, where only 5% of the cores are mineralised. The analyser is exactly the same:

How many there areGive a positiveHow many positives
Mineralised590%4.5
Barren9520%19
Total positives23.5
4.5 / 23.5 = 0.1915  →  19.1 %

The same device, the same positive, and now only a 19% probability of being right.

The only thing that changed was what you knew before measuring. That is the whole idea of Bayes' theorem: new evidence does not speak alone, it combines with what you already knew. And that is why a positive in a rich zone means something very different from the same positive in a poor zone.

Terms

Glossary

  • Fundamental concepts of probability. The block that runs through this module, and that appears under that name in the quizzes: the experiment and its sample space, the event, how events combine with the addition and multiplication rules, independence and conditional probability. Everything else in the module, Bayes and the distributions, is built on top of those five.
  • Experiment and sample space. The experiment is the action with chance in it, taking a core out of the bag. The sample space is the list of everything that can come out. Writing that list is what turns a confusing problem into a countable one.
  • Event. A possible outcome of the experiment, like "it comes out mineralised".
  • Probability. Favourable cases over possible cases, a number between 0 and 1.
  • Addition rule. For the probability that one or the other event happens: they are added and what they share is subtracted, so it is not counted twice.
  • Multiplication rule. For the probability that both happen: they are multiplied, using the conditional of the second if they are not independent.
  • Objective probability. Calculated with data or with theory, like our 3 out of 10.
  • Subjective probability. An informed judgement when there is not enough data.
  • Independent events. One happening does not change the probability of the other. Drawing with replacement makes them independent.
  • Conditional probability. P(A|B), the probability of A given that B already happened. It is the central idea of the module.
  • Bayes' theorem. The formula that turns a conditional around: it combines what you already knew (the prior probability) with the new evidence.
  • Random variable. A number that comes out of a process with chance in it, like how many drill holes intersect ore in a campaign.
  • Probability distribution. The list of all the values a random variable can take and with what probability each one occurs.
  • Binomial distribution. Counts successes in a fixed number of trials, with a constant probability of success.
  • Poisson distribution. Counts how many times something infrequent happens in an interval of time or space.
  • Normal distribution. The symmetric bell. It describes continuous variables that cluster around a centre.
  • z-score. How many standard deviations a value is from the mean.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Write the question before looking for the formula

Half of the mistakes in this module are translation mistakes, not calculation ones. "What is the probability that the core is mineralised if the analyser gave a positive?" is P(M | +). "What is the probability that the analyser gives a positive if the core is mineralised?" is P(+ | M). They are different questions with different answers: 65.9% against 90%.

Step 2. To combine events, first decide whether they are independent

Ask yourself: does the first outcome change the conditions of the second? If you draw without replacement, yes. If today's drill hole does not affect tomorrow's, no.

SituationRule
IndependentP(A y B) = P(A) × P(B)
Dependent`P(A y B) = P(A) × P(BA)`
One or the otherP(A o B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A y B)

That subtraction at the end is there so the cases where both happen are not counted twice.

Step 3. To turn a conditional around, count cases

You can memorise Bayes' formula, but it is harder to go wrong counting over 100 cases, which is what we did in the toy example. Build the table, add up the positives column, and divide the ones you care about by the total. It comes out the same and it is understood.

Step 4. Choose the distribution according to what you are counting

Your questionDistribution
How many successes in a fixed number of trials?Binomial
How many times does something infrequent happen in an interval?Poisson
What value does a continuous variable that clusters around a centre take?Normal

The question that separates them is whether there is a countable number of trials (binomial) or a continuous flow of opportunities (Poisson).

Step 5. With the normal, everything goes through the z-score

Converting a value to z tells you at once whether it is common or rare, because the bell always shares out the same way: around 68% of the data falls within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Store the three numbers of the problem under names that make sense

Before calculating anything it pays to make clear what each number is. A bare 0.90 in the middle of the code says nothing a month from now.

prior = 0.30              # 3 de cada 10 testigos estan mineralizados
sens = 0.90               # acierta cuando SI hay mineral
false_pos = 0.20          # se equivoca cuando NO lo hay
line by line
prior = 0.30
Stores 0.30 under the name prior, which is what statistics calls "what you already knew before measuring".
# 3 de cada 10...
Everything after a hash is a comment: Python ignores it completely. It is there only for whoever reads the code, and that someone will probably be you six months from now.
sens
Short for sensitivity. Short names are fine if the comment explains what they are.

Step 2. The Bayes calculation, written the way it is thought

p_positive = sens * prior + false_pos * (1 - prior)
print(f"P(positivo)      = {p_positive:.4f}")
print(f"P(mineral | +)   = {sens * prior / p_positive:.4f}")
line by line
sens * prior
The true positives: of those that do have ore (30%), the device gets 90% right. Multiplying two probabilities is what we did by hand in the table.
(1 - prior)
The proportion that does not have ore. If 30% has it, the rest is 1 minus 0.30, that is 0.70. Subtracting something from 1 is the usual way of saying "the others".
+
The two routes by which a positive can come out are added: being right about ore and being wrong about barren rock.
p_positive =
The result is stored because it is going to be used twice. Calculating something once and reusing it avoids mistakes.
sens * prior / p_positive
Python multiplies and divides from left to right, so this is (sens × prior) ÷ p_positive, which is exactly what we want.
:.4f
Four decimals. They are needed here because the result is compared with the calculation by hand.
prints
P(positivo)      = 0.4100
P(mineral | +)   = 0.6585

The 0.41 is the 41 positives out of every 100 in our table, and the 0.6585 is the answer. Identical to counting it by hand.

Step 3. Repeat the calculation changing only what you already knew

Here appears the most useful tool in the whole course: making the computer repeat the same thing with different data, instead of copying and pasting the block.

for prior in (0.30, 0.05):
    p_positive = sens * prior + false_pos * (1 - prior)
    print(f"si el {prior:.0%} esta mineralizado -> P(mineral | +) = {sens * prior / p_positive:.1%}")
line by line
for prior in (...)
A loop. It reads like this: "for each value in this list, call it prior and do what is below". It runs twice, once with 0.30 and once with 0.05.
(0.30, 0.05)
Parentheses with commas are a tuple, which is a list that cannot be modified. For walking through fixed values it is the usual thing.
:
The colon at the end announces that a block is coming. Whenever you see a colon at the end of a line, what follows goes indented.
la sangría
The two lines below carry four spaces in front. That is not decoration: in Python the indentation is what marks what is inside the loop. Remove it and it stops repeating.
:.0%
Percentage format: multiplies by 100 and adds the symbol, with zero decimals. That is why 0.30 comes out as 30% and not as 0.3000.
:.1%
The same with one decimal, so that 65.9% does not get rounded to 66%.
prints
si el 30% esta mineralizado -> P(mineral | +) = 65.9%
si el 5% esta mineralizado -> P(mineral | +) = 19.1%

These three lines are the whole module. The device did not change, the ground did.

Step 4. The binomial, to count hits in a campaign

Ten drill holes, each with a 30% probability of intersecting ore.

from scipy import stats

print(f"exactamente 3 aciertos = {stats.binom.pmf(3, n=10, p=0.3):.4f}")
print(f"al menos 1 acierto     = {1 - stats.binom.pmf(0, n=10, p=0.3):.4f}")
line by line
stats.binom
Inside the statistics drawer there is a sub-drawer for each distribution. This is the binomial's.
.pmf(...)
Short for probability mass function. It answers "what is the probability that exactly this number comes out?".
n=10, p=0.3
Named arguments. Instead of trusting the order, you say what each thing is: n trials and p of probability. It is longer to write and much harder to get wrong.
1 - stats.binom.pmf(0, ...)
The complement trick: instead of adding the probabilities of 1, 2, 3 and so on up to 10, the probability of none is calculated and subtracted from 1.
prints
exactamente 3 aciertos = 0.2668
al menos 1 acierto     = 0.9718

Step 5. The Poisson, for what happens rarely

A crusher that stops twice per shift on average.

print(f"ningun paro este turno = {stats.poisson.pmf(0, mu=2):.4f}")
print(f"5 paros este turno     = {stats.poisson.pmf(5, mu=2):.4f}")
line by line
stats.poisson
The sub-drawer of the Poisson distribution. The way of calling it is identical to the binomial, and that is no accident: scipy uses the same pattern for all of them.
mu=2
The Greek letter mu is what the average number of events per interval is called by convention. Here, two stops per shift.
pmf(0, ...)
No stops. The zero is asked about just like any other number.
prints
ningun paro este turno = 0.1353
5 paros este turno     = 0.0361

That five stops have only a 3.6% probability is what turns a shift like that into a sign that something is wrong, and not into bad luck.

Step 6. The normal and the z-score

A deposit with a mean grade of 2.40% and deviation 0.25, where a sample of 2.90 turns up.

z = (2.90 - 2.40) / 0.25
print(f"z = {z:.2f}")
print(f"muestras por encima de 2.90: {1 - stats.norm.cdf(z):.4f}")
line by line
(2.90 - 2.40) / 0.25
The parentheses rule: first the subtraction, then the division. Without them Python would divide 2.40 by 0.25 before subtracting, and anything could come out.
stats.norm
The sub-drawer of the normal distribution. Same pattern again.
.cdf(z)
Short for cumulative distribution function. It returns what proportion of the data lies below that point.
1 - stats.norm.cdf(z)
Since cdf gives what lies below, subtracting it from 1 gives what lies above. It is the same complement trick as in step 4.
prints
z = 2.00
muestras por encima de 2.90: 0.0228

It is two standard deviations from the mean, and only 2.3% of the samples would get that high by chance. That same calculation is the one that holds up the p-values of module 5.

Traps

Watch out

  • P(A|B) and P(B|A) are almost never equal, and confusing them is the most expensive mistake of the module. Our analyser is right 90% of the time on ore, but a positive from it only means ore 65.9% of the time.
  • The rarer what you are looking for, the worse a good test performs. With 5% mineralisation, the same analyser drops to 19.1%. It is not that the device fails more: it is that there is vastly more barren rock to be wrong about.
  • Independence is not assumed, it is checked. Two sensors on the same machine usually fail together, and treating them as independent multiplies probabilities that cannot be multiplied.
  • The binomial requires the probability not to change between trials. Drawing without replacement changes it, as we saw in step 2, and then the binomial is not the right distribution.
  • The Poisson assumes the events do not trigger each other. If one crusher stop causes two more, it underestimates the real variability.
  • Not everything continuous is normal. Waiting times, incomes and times between failures are skewed. Check with a histogram before applying anything that assumes normality.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

Bayes' theorem is exactly how an experienced geologist interprets an anomalous assay, only written with symbols.

A result of 8% Cu arrives from a zone where the regional geology says 2% is normal. Nobody accepts it blindly or throws it in the bin: what was already known about the deposit is combined with the known reliability of the laboratory, and out of that comes an updated belief. That is Bayes. The difference is that the geologist does it by intuition and the formula does it with numbers that can be defended in a committee.

And the three distributions live at the plant:

DistributionWhere it appears
BinomialDefective parts in a batch; drill holes that cut economic ore in a campaign
PoissonUnscheduled crusher stops per shift; failures of a piece of equipment per month
NormalGrade of a homogeneous ore around its mean; error of a well-calibrated analyser

And the 68-95-99.7 rule is not theory: it is the criterion of quality control charts. A point outside three deviations is not treated as noise, it is treated as an alarm, precisely because the probability of it happening by chance is 3 in 1,000.

Review

Review

Why can P(A|B) be so different from P(B|A)?

Because they condition on different things, and the relationship between the two depends on how frequent each event is on its own. The analyser in the example is right 90% of the time when there is ore, but since barren cores are the majority, its false positives are numerous in absolute terms and drag down the probability that a positive means ore, which ends up at 65.9%. Ignoring this difference is one of the most common statistical mistakes outside the classroom, and it shows up in medicine, in security and in quality control.

The same analyser gives 65.9% in one zone and 19.1% in another. How can that be?

Because what changes is not the device but what was known before using it. In the rich zone there are 30 mineralised cores per 100 and the analyser produces 27 true positives against 14 false ones. In the poor zone there are only 5 mineralised, so it produces 4.5 true positives against 19 false ones, and the proportion flips. New evidence never speaks alone: it combines with the prior probability, and that is exactly the content of Bayes' theorem.

When would you use the binomial and when the Poisson?

The binomial when there is a fixed, countable number of trials with a constant probability of success, like ten drill holes with a 30% hit rate each. The Poisson when counting how many times something infrequent happens within a continuous interval of time or space, with no defined number of trials, like the stops of a crusher per shift. The question that separates them is whether the trials can be counted or only the interval.

What does a z-score of 2.00 mean?

That the value is two standard deviations above the mean. Since the bell shares 95% of the data within plus or minus two deviations, a value at 2.00 leaves only 2.3% of the distribution above it. It is the same calculation behind a p-value: a significant result is, at bottom, a statistic far enough from the centre for chance to explain it poorly.

You take two cores from the bag without putting the first one back. Why can you not multiply 3/10 by 3/10?

Because by not putting the first one back, the bag changes: nine cores remain instead of ten and two mineralised instead of three. The events stop being independent, so the probability of the second has to be the conditional, 2/9, and the correct product is 3/10 times 2/9, which gives 0.0667. Multiplying 3/10 by 3/10 would give 0.09, which is the answer to another question: drawing with replacement.
