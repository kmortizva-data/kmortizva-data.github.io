TikTok

Do videos from verified accounts get more views? The result contradicts intuition, and the secondary finding turned out more useful than the main one.

19,084 videos2 groupsWelch + Mann-Whitneyα = 0.05
Brief

The brief

You work as a data analyst for TikTok. The platform receives thousands of user reports every day flagging videos as problematic, and the human moderation team cannot keep up with reviewing them all.

The idea is to build, in the future, a system that automatically prioritises which videos to review first. But before building anything, the team needs to understand the terrain: what sets some videos apart from others in terms of reach? Specifically, there is a suspicion about verified accounts, and it has to be checked with data before designing anything on top of it.

Question

The question

Do videos published by verified accounts receive a different number of views from videos by unverified accounts?

It looks like a trivial question: the two averages are calculated and compared to see which is higher. But an average that came out of a sample proves nothing on its own: it could be chance of this particular sample. To claim that the difference is real, a hypothesis test is needed, which is exactly what module 5 of this course teaches.

Hypotheses

The hypotheses

Before touching the data, the two competing claims are written down. This is done first: if they are formulated after seeing the results, the test loses validity.

  • Null hypothesis (H₀). The conservative position, the "nothing is going on here" one: there is no difference between the mean number of views of videos from verified and unverified accounts.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Hₐ). What is suspected: there is a real difference.

The significance level is set at α = 0.05. It means that at most a 5% probability of shouting "there is a difference!" when in fact there was none is accepted.

Data

The data

  • 19,084 videos analysed in total.
  • The variable measured (the target metric) is video_view_count: how many times each video was viewed.
  • The variable that separates the groups is verified_status: verified or unverified.

And here the first important finding appears, even before starting:

GroupHow many videos
Unverified17,884
Verified1,200

The groups are very unbalanced: for every video from a verified account there are almost 15 from ordinary accounts. That does not invalidate the analysis, but it conditions which test can be used and has to be kept in mind until the end.

Exploration

What the exploration showed

Before any statistical test you have to look at the data. One panel per group, and what each one teaches.

Panel 1. The unverified accounts

The columns count how many videos fall in each band of views, and the axis runs on a logarithmic scale because the values go from a few views to more than a million.

The three blocks at the top split the group into named bands, and there is the finding: 48% of these videos are barely watched and 47% are watched enormously, with only 5% in between. It is not one mountain with a long tail, it is two mountains separated by a void.

And notice where the median falls, at 46,723: right in the void. Only 1.1% of the videos in this group are within 20% of that value. It is a statistic that describes almost no real video.

Panel 2. The verified accounts

The same axis, the same split into three bands, and a different story. Here 83% of the videos live in the low-reach band and only 16% make it to the high-reach one.

Mind one thing before comparing: the vertical scale of this panel is not the previous one's, because this group has 1,200 videos and the other 17,884. That is why the figures go separately, so that each is read on its own scale without the smaller one being flattened.

What shows when the two splits are put in a table

BandUnverifiedVerified
Low reach (up to 10 thousand)48.0%82.6%
The empty zone (10 thousand to 100 thousand)4.9%1.7%
High reach (more than 100 thousand)47.1%15.8%

Here is the real conclusion, and it is more precise than comparing means. The two groups have the same two populations; what changes is what fraction of each group reaches the high-reach population: almost half in the ordinary accounts, one in six in the verified ones.

Said in product language: it is not that a video from a verified account is watched less, it is that it has a much lower probability of taking off.

How to read this figure

The dot is the observed difference between the two groups and the bar is its 95% confidence interval, the range where the real difference can be. The vertical line marks zero, the position of "no difference".

Here the bar is miles away from zero, and that distance is the whole result of the analysis in a single image. Compare it with the same figure in the Waze case, where the bar goes through zero: they are two opposite answers drawn the same way.

Videos from unverified accounts receive on average almost three times more views, a 65.6% relative difference. It is the opposite of what intuition would expect, and that is why it deserves a formal test before believing it.

The secondary finding, which turned out stronger

There is a second variable in the dataset: whether the video contains a claim or an opinion. The contrast is of another order:

Kind of videoMean views

About 100 times more views. This secondary finding turned out much more powerful than the main one, and it is the one that really serves the original goal of prioritising moderation.

Method

The test, and why this test

The mean of two independent groups is compared, so the right family is the two-sample t-test. But within that family a choice has to be made, and the choice is justified by checking an assumption.

First, check whether the variances are equal

The classic t-test (Student's) assumes that both groups have the same spread. That is verified with Levene's test:

prints
Levene: estadístico = 392.01     p = 2.22e-86

That p-value is a number with 85 zeros before the first significant decimal. It is overwhelmingly smaller than 0.05, so it is concluded that the variances are not equal, something that could already be seen in the table above: 325,682 against 221,139.

That is why Welch is used, not Student

Since the equal-variances assumption does not hold, Welch's t-test (equal_var=False) is used, which does not require it. Using Student here would have given a result with a pretend precision.

And the normality assumption, if the distributions were so skewed? Here enters the central limit theorem of module 3: with 17,884 and 1,200 observations, the distribution of the means is approximately normal even if the individual values are not. That is why the test is valid despite the skew the two panels show.

Result

The result, translated

prints
Welch t = 25.4994          p = 2.61e-120
IC 95% de la diferencia = [-187.626 , -160.823]
Cohen's d = -0.544
Mann-Whitney U: p = 1.93e-91

And this is what each line means:

  • t = 25.50. The means of the two groups are separated by about 25 standard errors. As a reference, from 2 upwards it is usually considered a notable separation: 25 is enormous.
  • p = 2.61 × 10⁻¹²⁰. The probability of seeing a difference this big **if in fact there were no difference at all**. Written in decimals it would be a 0 followed by 119 zeros before the 261. It is, for practical purposes, zero. H₀ is rejected.
  • 95% confidence interval = [−187,626, −160,823]. The real difference is somewhere in that range. Notice that it does not include zero: it is another way of seeing the same result, and it connects directly with module 4.
  • Cohen's d = −0.544. The effect size. It is the piece that stops "significant" being confused with "important": a value around 0.5 is considered a medium effect. That is, the difference is very real, but moderate in magnitude, not colossal.
  • Mann-Whitney U: p = 1.93 × 10⁻⁹¹. A second, backup test, which compares ranks instead of means and does not assume any distribution shape. It reaches the same conclusion, which rules out the result being an artefact of the skew or the outliers.
Decision

What gets decided with this

The difference is real: videos from unverified accounts receive significantly more views, with a medium-sized effect.

But the operational recommendation comes from the secondary finding, which turned out much more powerful: the variable that really predicts a video's reach is not the verification status but whether the video contains a claim (501,029 against 4,956 mean views).

Translated into action: a future moderation-prioritisation system should lean on claim_status as its main signal, not on verified_status. This analysis has served to discard an intuitive hypothesis and find a better signal, which is exactly what a hypothesis test is for.

Limits

Limitations

  • The groups are very unbalanced (17,884 against 1,200). Welch handles it correctly, but the estimate for the verified group rests on far fewer data points and is therefore less precise.
  • A sample ratio mismatch (SRM) was detected: the check gave chi² = 14,586 with p ≈ 0. In a real A/B experiment this would be a red flag forcing a review of the assignment mechanism. Here it is to be expected, because the groups were not assigned at random: they are a pre-existing characteristic of the accounts.
  • This is an observational study, not an experiment. One can claim that an association exists between verification and views, but not that verification causes fewer views. There could be background factors (kind of content, age of the account, behaviour of the algorithm) that explain both things at once.
  • The outliers were not removed, a deliberate and correct decision for this domain, but it implies that the means are influenced by the viral videos. The validation with Mann-Whitney is precisely the defence against that objection.
  • The distribution is bimodal, and that weakens the comparison of means. When the data has two separate populations, the mean falls between them and represents neither. The test remains valid (the central limit theorem does not demand normality), but the interesting question stops being "which group has more views on average?" and becomes "what proportion of each group falls in the high-reach population?". That analysis is not done and would be the natural next step.
  • The void between the two populations is suspicious. A cut that clean around 10,000 views may be a real effect of the distribution algorithm, or an artefact of how this teaching dataset was generated. With the data available the two cannot be told apart, and it is worth saying so before building anything on top of it.
