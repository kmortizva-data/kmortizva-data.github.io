Confidence intervals

How to turn a bare number into an honest range about what you do not know. We build an interval by hand with six numbers before touching a single formula, and take apart the sentence almost everyone says wrong about the 95%.

4 h course time7 videos3 readings4 assignments3 practice
Context

What this module solves

When you say "the mean fare is $12.30" you are hiding something important: that 12.30 came out of a sample, not of every trip that exists. Had you taken a different sample you would have got 12.10 or 12.55.

A confidence interval is the honest way of saying it. Instead of a single number, you give a range: "the mean is between 11.80 and 12.80". This module is how that range is calculated and, above all, what it really means, because the intuitive interpretation is wrong and it is the mistake most repeated in the whole course.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Forget formulas for a moment. We have six measurements of the copper grade of a lot, in percent:

2.1   2.5   2.3   2.8   2.2   2.5

We want to know the mean grade of the whole lot, but we only measured these six samples.

Step 1. The best number we have

We add and divide by six:

(2.1 + 2.5 + 2.3 + 2.8 + 2.2 + 2.5) / 6  =  14.4 / 6  =  2.40

Our best estimate is 2.40%. But it is only that, an estimate from six samples.

Step 2. How much do the values move relative to each other?

We look at how far each measurement is from 2.40:

MeasurementDistance to 2.40Squared distance
2.1−0.300.09
2.5+0.100.01
2.3−0.100.01
2.8+0.400.16
2.2−0.200.04
2.5+0.100.01

We add the last column: 0.09+0.01+0.01+0.16+0.04+0.01 = 0.32. We divide by 5, not by 6 (we come back to that in the traps), and take the root:

0.32 / 5 = 0.064        →        √0.064 = 0.253

That is the standard deviation: 0.253. It tells us the individual measurements dance about 0.25 units around the average.

Step 3. How much would the average move if we repeated the sampling?

Here is the key idea of the module. We are not interested in how much the loose measurements dance, but in how much the average would dance if we took another six samples, and another six, and another six. That number is called the standard error and it is smaller, because averaging smooths:

error estándar = desviación estándar / √n = 0.253 / √6 = 0.253 / 2.449 = 0.103

The average is much more stable (0.103) than the individual measurements (0.253). Averaging six things cancels part of the chance.

Step 4. Stretch left and right

Now we build the range. We take our 2.40 and stretch to both sides a certain number of standard errors. With six samples that multiplier is 2.571; where that exact number comes from we see in the method, for now accept it as "a bit more than two and a half":

margen de error = 2.571 × 0.103 = 0.265

límite inferior = 2.40 − 0.265 = 2.135
límite superior = 2.40 + 0.265 = 2.665

Result: the mean grade of the lot is between 2.14% and 2.67%, with 95% confidence.

Notice what just happened. We swapped a falsely precise number (2.40) for a range that admits what we do not know. That is the whole module. The rest is details of which multiplier to use and how to say it without getting it wrong.

Terms

Glossary

  • Point estimate. A single number as the answer, our 2.40%. Convenient, but it pretends to a precision it does not have.
  • Confidence interval. The range we built above, from 2.14% to 2.67%. It acknowledges the uncertainty instead of hiding it.
  • Standard error. How much your average would vary if you repeated the sampling. It is the standard deviation divided by the root of n. Not to be confused with the standard deviation, which talks about the individual values.
  • Margin of error. Half the width of the interval, that is, what you stretch to each side. In the example, 0.265.
  • Confidence level. The percentage that names the interval: 95%, 90%, 99%. It is a property of the method, not of one particular interval.
  • Critical value. The multiplier of step 4, our 2.571. The more confidence you want, the bigger it is and the wider the interval comes out.
  • Student's t distribution. Where the critical value comes from when you do not know the real variability of the population, which is practically always. With small samples it is wider than the normal bell, and that is why it forces you to stretch more.
  • Degrees of freedom. The n − 1 we used when dividing by 5 instead of by 6. It is the number you tell the t so it knows how small your sample is.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Calculate the mean and the standard error

It is what we did by hand above. The mean is your estimate; the standard error says how firm that estimate is. The more data, the smaller the standard error, because you divide by the root of a bigger number.

Step 2. Choose the multiplier, or critical value

Here is the question everyone struggles with: why 2.571 and not 1.96?

The famous 1.96 is the value for the normal bell, and it only holds when you know the real variability of the whole population, something that almost never happens. When you estimate it from your own sample, as we did, there is extra uncertainty, and that uncertainty is paid for with a bigger multiplier. It comes from Student's t and depends on how much data you have:

Sample sizeDegrees of freedom (n−1)Multiplier for 95%
652.571
1092.262
30292.045
100991.984
∞ (known population)1.960

Read the table from top to bottom: with little data it forces you to be more cautious, that is, to stretch more, and as the sample grows it gets closer to the 1.96 of the normal. With 100 values the difference is already 1%.

Step 3. Choose the confidence level according to what a mistake costs

95% is the industry standard, but it is not sacred. With 90% the interval comes out narrower, and you fail one time in ten. With 99% it comes out wider and therefore less useful, but you fail one time in a hundred.

There is no way of having a narrow interval and a very safe one with the same data. The only thing that improves both at once is more sample.

Step 4. Say the result without getting it wrong

And this is the part almost everyone says wrong:

  • Incorrect. "There is a 95% probability that the mean grade is between 2.14 and 2.67."
  • Correct. "If we repeated this sampling many times and built an interval each time, 95% of those intervals would contain the real mean grade."

Why does it matter so much? Because the real mean grade of the lot is already a fixed number. It is not jumping from one side to the other. Your interval either contains it or it does not, that is already decided, only you do not know which of the two cases you got. The 95% describes how reliable your procedure is in the long run, not this particular interval in front of you.

An analogy: if an archer hits 95% of their shots, that is a property of the archer. Once the arrow is loosed and the target covered, it no longer makes sense to say "there is a 95% probability that this particular arrow hit the centre". It either hit, or it did not.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Reproduce by machine what we did with a pencil

The same six numbers of the toy example, to check that the computer says the same as we do.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

grades = np.array([2.1, 2.5, 2.3, 2.8, 2.2, 2.5])

mean = grades.mean()
sem = stats.sem(grades)          # standard error of the mean
print(f"media = {mean:.2f}")
print(f"error estandar = {sem:.3f}")
line by line
mean = grades.mean()
Storing the result under a name instead of calculating it every time. It is used three more times below, and calculating it once keeps a variant from slipping in.

Short for standard error of the mean. It does in one line what cost us a whole table: dividing the deviation by the root of n.
# standard error...
A comment in English because the code goes in English, even though the explanation goes in Spanish. It is the convention of the project so that anyone who clones the repository understands it.

Three decimals here, because the standard error is small and with two, information would be lost.

The same 2.40 and 0.103 we got with a pencil.

Step 2. Ask for the multiplier, which is the number that confuses most

t_star = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=len(grades) - 1)
print(f"multiplicador = {t_star:.3f}")
line by line
stats.t
The sub-drawer of the t distribution, just as in module 2 we used stats.norm or stats.binom. Same pattern always.
.ppf(...)
Short for percent point function, and it is the inverse operation of cdf. While cdf answers "what proportion lies below this value?", ppf answers "what value leaves this proportion below?".
0.975
The detail most often got wrong. The 5% left outside is split between two tails, 2.5% below and 2.5% above. We ask for the point that leaves 97.5% below, so what is left above is exactly 2.5%. Asking for 0.95 would build a one-tailed interval, which is something else.
len(grades)
How many elements it has. The name comes from length.
df=len(grades) - 1
The degrees of freedom: 6 minus 1, that is 5. The same 5 we divided by by hand.
t_star
The name with an asterisk is the convention in statistics for a critical value. In Python you cannot write an asterisk in a name, so the word is written out.
prints
multiplicador = 2.571

Step 3. Put it all together in one line

With the mean, the standard error and the multiplier, scipy builds the whole interval.

low, high = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(grades) - 1, loc=mean, scale=sem)
print(f"IC 95% = [{low:.3f}, {high:.3f}]")
line by line
.interval(0.95, ...)
Returns both ends at once. Here it does get 0.95 and not 0.975, because this function already knows how to split the 5% between the two tails on its own. It is easy to mix up with the previous step.
low, high =
Two values returned and two names to catch them, as in module 1.
loc=mean
In scipy, loc is always "where the centre is". Here, the mean of the sample.
scale=sem
And scale is always "how much it spreads". The standard error goes here, not the standard deviation. It is the most common mistake when writing this line and it produces an absurdly wide interval without any warning.
prints
IC 95% = [2.135, 2.665]

Identical to the one we built by hand in step 4 of the toy example.

Step 4. The same scheme, but with a proportion

When what you measure is a percentage, for example what fraction of trips was paid by card, the standard error is calculated differently. The logic is identical: estimate plus or minus multiplier times error.

successes, n = 620, 1000            # 620 de 1000 viajes pagados con tarjeta
p = successes / n
se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
z_star = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)      # 1.96: con n grande se usa la normal

print(f"proporcion = {p:.1%}  ±  {z_star * se:.1%}")
line by line
successes, n = 620, 1000
Two variables in a single line. Python hands out the values by position: the first goes to the first name and the second to the second.
p = successes / n
The observed proportion: 620 out of 1000, that is 0.62.
np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
The formula of the standard error of a proportion. It is different from that of a mean, but it does the same job: saying how much the result would dance if you repeated the sampling.
p * (1 - p)
This product is largest when p is 0.5 and shrinks towards the extremes. Translated: a percentage close to 50% is the hardest to estimate precisely, and one close to 0% or 100% the easiest.
stats.norm.ppf(0.975)
The multiplier, but asked of the normal instead of the t, because with a thousand values the two are practically the same. It returns the famous 1.96.
z_star * se
The margin of error: multiplier times standard error, exactly as in step 4 of the toy example.
±
It is written as is inside the text. Python does not calculate it, it only prints it.
prints
proporcion = 62.0%  ±  3.0%

That is, between 59% and 65%. Here we use 1.96 because with a thousand values the t and the normal are practically the same thing, as the last row of the table in step 2 shows.

Traps

Watch out

  • The intuitive interpretation is wrong, and it is the mistake of the module. "95% probability that the value is in this interval" sounds natural and is incorrect. The real value is fixed; what varies from sample to sample is your interval.
  • We divide by n − 1, not by n. In the toy example we divided 0.32 by 5 and not by
  • The intuitive reason is that you already "spent" one value calculating the mean, so you have one degree of freedom less of genuine information. Dividing by n underestimates the real variability.
  • A narrower interval is not better for being narrower. It can be narrowed by lowering the confidence, but that only means you will be wrong more often. Precision and confidence are bought one with the other.
  • scale=sem, never scale=std. It is the classic code mistake. The interval is built on how much the mean varies, not on how much the loose values vary.
  • A confidence interval does not say where the individual values fall. That the mean is between 2.14 and 2.67 is perfectly compatible with loose samples of 1.8 or 3.1. For that there is something else, the prediction interval, which this course does not cover.
  • **With small samples, using 1.96 instead of the t gives intervals that are too optimistic.** With 6 values, 1.96 instead of 2.571 narrows the interval by 24% with no justification at all: you would be pretending to know more than you know.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

A confidence interval is exactly the laboratory certificate you already know how to read.

When the laboratory hands you "2.40% ± 0.27% Cu (95% confidence)", it has just done the same thing we did in the toy example, in fact with the same numbers. And that distinction that is so hard in class is one you already live at the plant: the certificate does not promise that this particular lot has its grade within the range, it promises that the protocol of sampling and analysis, applied over and over, gets it right 95 times out of 100. If a lot falls outside, the laboratory has not lied: you got the 5%.

And the trade-off between precision and confidence is the reserve classification of all time:

In statisticsIn the deposit
Lots of data, narrow intervalMeasured reserve: dense drilling, little uncertainty
Moderate data, medium intervalIndicated reserve
Little data, wide intervalInferred reserve: a lot of extrapolation

Nobody declares a measured reserve with three drill holes, and for the same reason nobody can give a narrow interval with high confidence using six samples. The only way out, in both worlds, is the same: drill more.

Review

Review

What does a 95% confidence interval really mean?

That if the sampling process were repeated many times and an interval built each time with the same method, approximately 95% of those intervals would contain the real value of the population. It does not mean that this particular interval has a 95% probability of containing it: the real value is fixed and the interval already calculated either contains it or it does not. The 95% describes the reliability of the procedure in the long run, just as an archer's aim describes the archer and not an arrow already loosed.

Why does the code ask for ppf(0.975) if the interval is 95%?

Because the 5% left outside the interval is split between the two tails of the distribution: 2.5% below and 2.5% above. The ppf function returns the point that leaves accumulated below it the percentage you ask for, so to leave only 2.5% above you have to ask it for 0.975. Asking for 0.95 would give the critical value of a one-tailed test, which corresponds to another kind of interval.

What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

The standard deviation measures how much the individual values vary around their mean; the standard error measures how much the mean itself would vary if you repeated the sampling. The standard error is always smaller, because it is obtained by dividing the standard deviation by the root of the sample size: averaging several values cancels part of the chance. In the example of the six grades, the values danced 0.253 but the average only 0.103. The confidence interval is always built on the standard error, never on the standard deviation.

When is the t distribution used instead of the normal, and what happens if you get it wrong?

The t is used when the real variability of the population is not known and has to be estimated from the sample itself, which is the normal situation in practice. The t produces bigger multipliers to compensate for that extra uncertainty, and the difference is bigger the smaller the sample. If the normal is used with small samples, the interval comes out artificially narrow: with six values, using 1.96 instead of 2.571 shrinks the interval by almost a quarter and conveys a precision the data does not support.

A colleague says their 99% interval is "better" than the 95% one. Are they right?

Not necessarily. A 99% interval gives more guarantee of containing the real value, but in exchange it is wider and therefore less informative: saying "the grade is between 1.9% and 2.9%" is safer and less useful than "it is between 2.14% and 2.67%". The choice depends on the cost of being wrong in that particular context, not on one number being bigger. And with the same data you cannot win on both at once: the only thing that narrows the interval without sacrificing confidence is increasing the sample size.
