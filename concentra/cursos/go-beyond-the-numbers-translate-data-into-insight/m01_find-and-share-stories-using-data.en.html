Go Beyond the Numbers: Translate Data into Insights / Module 1Español

Find and share stories using data

A number is not a story. It is shown with six measurements that, arranged in two different orders, give exactly the same mean, the same median and the same deviation, and tell two opposite things. Here come the six practices of exploratory analysis and how they fit with PACE.

Context

What this module solves

You are handed a file and asked for "an analysis". The temptation is to calculate the average of the column that matters, put it on a slide and move on to the next thing.

This module exists to teach that that average can be true and mean nothing. The summary of a dataset is a compression, and like every compression, it throws information away. Sometimes it throws away precisely what was needed.

What is learned here is a procedure for finding what the summary hides, and a framework for knowing where in the project you are while you do it.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Two flotation circuits, six months each, recovery in percent:

mes           1     2     3     4     5     6
circuito A   88    87    86    85    84    83
circuito B   85    88    83    87    84    86

Look at them for a moment before going on.

Step 1. The usual summaries

circuito A:  suma = 88+87+86+85+84+83 = 513     media = 513 / 6 = 85,5
circuito B:  suma = 85+88+83+87+84+86 = 513     media = 513 / 6 = 85,5

The mean is the same. And it is neither coincidence nor luck: they are the same six numbers in another order. So the median (85.5), the deviation (1.8708), the minimum (83) and the maximum (88) also coincide. Every summary calculated without looking at the order is identical by construction.

Step 2. What happens when sorting by date

Circuit A drops exactly one point every month, without a single exception:

88 → 87 → 86 → 85 → 84 → 83        diferencias: −1, −1, −1, −1, −1

Circuit B goes up and down with no direction:

85 → 88 → 83 → 87 → 84 → 86        diferencias: +3, −5, +4, −3, +2

Putting a number on that difference: the slope of circuit A is −1.0000 points per month and that of B, −0.0857, practically flat.

Step 3. What just happened

The two circuits have the same average and do not have the same problem. In A there is something degrading steadily and it is going to keep dropping next month. In B there is variability, which is another conversation and another action.

A report saying "the two circuits recover 85.5% on average" is true and seriously incomplete. That is the whole thesis of the module:

The summary is the end of the analysis, not the beginning. And it does not replace looking at the data.
Terms

Glossary

  • Exploratory data analysis. The process of examining a dataset to understand what it contains before drawing conclusions from it. EDA for short, and the initials show up everywhere.
  • The six practices. Discovering, structuring, cleaning, joining, validating and presenting. They are the six kinds of EDA work, and they are not an order: you jump from one to another.
  • Discovering. Getting to know the shape, size and content of the dataset.
  • Structuring. Reorganising the raw data: sorting, grouping, splitting columns, pivoting.
  • Cleaning. Dealing with what is missing, repeated, extreme and wrongly typed.
  • Joining. Bringing in data from another source to complete the picture.
  • Validating. Checking that what you just did broke nothing.
  • Presenting. Delivering the finding to someone who is going to decide with it.
  • PACE. The framework of the certificate: Plan, Analyze, Construct, Execute. It says which phase of the project you are in; EDA says what you are doing with the data.
  • Stakeholder. Whoever is going to use your result to decide something. If you do not know who it is, you do not know which analysis to do.
  • Data storytelling. Telling what the data shows with the structure of a story: context, conflict and resolution.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Know who is going to decide something with this

Before opening the file. The same dataset gives different analyses depending on who asks: the plant manager cares about which knob to move tomorrow, the finance director about how much it costs, and the quality lead about whether the batch passes.

If you cannot name the person who is going to decide, do not start. It is the most expensive and most frequent mistake, because it produces no symptom until you deliver.

Step 2. The six practices, and why they are not a list

They are drawn in a row and used out of order. Cleaning reveals something that forces you to discover again; joining another source forces you to clean again.

PracticeWhat you doWhat it usually reveals
DiscoveringLooking at shape, types and first rowsThat there is less useful data than you thought
StructuringSorting, grouping, pivotingThe trend the average was hiding
CleaningMissing, duplicates, outliersThat 5% of the rows should not be there
JoiningBringing in another sourceThat the two sources do not agree on the keys
ValidatingChecking what you just didThat a transformation deleted rows without warning
PresentingDelivering itThat the real question was another one

Validating is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that catches your own mistakes. After every transformation: are they still the same rows? Do the totals add up? Did something become null?

Step 3. Fit EDA inside PACE

The two things coexist, and confusing them is normal at first. PACE says which phase of the project you are in. EDA says what you are doing to the data.

PACE phaseWhat happens thereEDA practices that dominate
PlanDefining the question and who caresNone yet: here you talk to people
AnalyzeGetting to know and preparing the dataDiscovering, structuring, cleaning, joining
ConstructModelling or testingValidating, non-stop
ExecuteDelivering and recommendingPresenting

The bulk of EDA lives in Analyze, and that is where most of the real time of a project goes. It is not wasted time: it is the time that saves redoing everything.

Step 4. Turn the finding into a story

A bare finding moves nobody. The structure that works has three parts:

  1. Context. What was expected. "The two circuits recover 85.5% on average."
  2. Conflict. What breaks that expectation. *"But circuit A has been dropping a point every month for six months."*
  3. Resolution. What to do. *"If it goes on, in January it is at 78. It has to be checked before then."*

Notice that the number in the context is the same one the incomplete report gave. The story does not change the data, it changes what is seen of it.

Step 5. Choose the chart according to the phase

Module 4 goes into detail, but it is worth knowing from now that a chart for yourself and a chart for presenting are not the same chart:

PhaseFor whomWhat it is like
AnalyzeFor youUgly, quick, many. It serves to see
ExecuteFor whoever decidesA single one, clean, with the finding written on top

And the Deloitte case the course presents is about exactly that: a consultancy that works for clients who are not going to read the analysis, only the conclusion. The detail is on the platform; what has to be retained is that the work does not end when the finding exists, it ends when someone understands it.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. Check that the summaries do not tell the two circuits apart

The same six numbers of the example, in the two orderings.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

month = np.arange(1, 7)
falling = np.array([88, 87, 86, 85, 84, 83])
shuffled = np.array([85, 88, 83, 87, 84, 86])

plant = pd.DataFrame({"month": month, "circuit_a": falling, "circuit_b": shuffled})
print(plant.describe().loc[["count", "mean", "50%", "std", "min", "max"]].round(4))
line by line
import pandas as pd
The tables library. The nickname pd is the universal convention, just like np for numpy.
np.arange(1, 7)
The integers from 1 to 6. The second number is not included, which is the most common source of Python errors when starting out.
pd.DataFrame({...})
Builds a table from a dictionary: each key is a column name and each value, its data. It is the most direct way of creating data by hand.
plant.describe()
The summary of all the numeric columns at once: count, mean, deviation, minimum, quartiles and maximum. It is one of the five lines of the ritual of the next module.
.loc[[...]]
Selects rows by name. The double brackets are a list of names, so it returns several rows and not one.
"50%"
That is what the median is called inside describe(): the 50th percentile.
.round(4)
Four decimals, enough to see that the deviations match down to the last digit.
prints
        month  circuit_a  circuit_b
count  6.0000     6.0000     6.0000
mean   3.5000    85.5000    85.5000
50%    3.5000    85.5000    85.5000
std    1.8708     1.8708     1.8708
min    1.0000    83.0000    83.0000
max    6.0000    88.0000    88.0000

The two columns are identical in all six rows. No summary in this table tells a circuit that is degrading from another that only has noise.

Step 2. Structure by date, which is what reveals the story

for name in ["circuit_a", "circuit_b"]:
    fit = stats.linregress(plant.month, plant[name])
    print(f"{name}: pendiente {fit.slope:+.4f} puntos por mes   R2 {fit.rvalue ** 2:.4f}")
line by line
for name in [...]
A loop over a list of column names, so as not to write the same calculation twice.
plant[name]
Takes a column by its name. With a variable inside the brackets you can walk through several columns in a loop.
stats.linregress(x, y)
Fits the line that best passes through the points. Here the x is the month, so the slope is how much recovery changes for every month that passes.
{fit.slope:+.4f}
The + forces the sign to be written even when it is positive. With a slope, seeing the sign written keeps you from reading it backwards.
fit.rvalue ** 2
The R2. Here it matters as a measure of how clean the trend is: a 1 means the six points fall exactly on the line.
prints
circuit_a: pendiente -1.0000 puntos por mes   R2 1.0000
circuit_b: pendiente -0.0857 puntos por mes   R2 0.0073

There is the story the summary did not have. Circuit A loses exactly one point every month, with an R2 of 1: the six points are on the line, not near it. B is going nowhere.

And notice how cheap it was: a column of dates and a line. The information was in the order, and no average looks at the order.

Traps

Watch out

  • An average can be true and hide the only thing that mattered. It is the message of the module, and the example proves it with the same six numbers.
  • Mean, median and deviation do not see the order. If the data has a date, sorting by it is mandatory before summarising.
  • The six practices are not a sequence. You constantly go back, and whoever does them in a row just once usually has not finished cleaning.
  • Validating is not optional. It is the practice that catches your own mistakes, and the first one dropped when there is a rush.
  • Without a stakeholder there is no analysis. If you cannot name who is going to decide, what you are doing is entertainment.
  • PACE and the six practices do not compete. One says which phase you are in, the other what you are doing with the data.
  • A finding without context is not understood and without action is useless. Context, conflict and resolution, in that order.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

This is the shift log against the monthly report, and you already know which of the two warns you in time.

The monthly report says the plant recovered 85.5%. The log, read by date, shows it has been dropping for six shifts. And at the plant that difference has immediate consequences: a recovery that falls a point a month steadily is not variability, it is something wearing out. A liner, the calibration of a dosing pump, the mill's grind size opening up little by little.

In the courseAt the plant
The average of the setThe number in the monthly report
Structuring by dateReading the log in order
A constant slopeWear, not noise
Variability with no directionAn unstable circuit, a different problem
The stakeholderWho is going to move the knob tomorrow

And you also recognise the warning about the six practices: nobody does a metallurgical balance in a single pass. You reconcile, find that a stream does not close, measure again, correct and reconcile again. That is exactly the back and forth between discovering, cleaning and validating.

Review

Review

Two data series have the same mean, the same median and the same deviation. Can they tell different stories?

Yes, and it is the normal thing when the data has an order. In the module's example the two circuits are literally the same six numbers in a different sequence, so every summary that does not look at the order coincides by construction: mean 85.5, median 85.5, deviation 1.8708. When sorted by date, one drops exactly a point a month with an R2 of 1 and the other is flat, with a slope of −0.0857. The mean was true in both cases and in one of them it hid the only thing that had to be reported.

What are the six practices of exploratory analysis and why are they not a list of steps?

Discovering, structuring, cleaning, joining, validating and presenting. They are not a sequence because each one can send you back to another: cleaning reveals something that forces you to discover again, joining a second source forces you to clean again, and validating exists precisely to send you back when something does not add up. Whoever goes through them once in a row has almost always left something uncleaned.

How do PACE and the six EDA practices differ?

They answer different questions and that is why they coexist. PACE says which phase of the project you are in: plan, analyse, construct or execute. The six practices say what you are doing to the data at that moment. Most of EDA falls inside the Analyze phase, validation accompanies Construct, and presenting belongs to Execute. Confusing them is frequent at the start; the shortcut for not doing so is that PACE talks about the project and EDA talks about the data.

Why does the module insist on identifying the stakeholder before opening the file?

Because the same dataset admits different analyses depending on who is going to decide with it, and choosing wrong produces no visible error: it produces a correct analysis that is of no use to anyone. The plant manager cares about what to change tomorrow, finance about how much it costs and quality about whether the batch meets specification. If the person who is going to make the decision cannot be named, the conversation of the Plan phase is still missing.

What structure does a data story have and why is giving the finding not enough?

Context, conflict and resolution. The context is what was expected, the conflict is the data point that breaks that expectation, and the resolution is what to do about it. The bare finding is not enough because whoever listens needs to know what to compare it against and what is expected of them: "circuit A drops a point a month" only means something after "the two average 85.5" and before "if it goes on like this, in January it is at 78". The data does not change; what is seen of it does.
