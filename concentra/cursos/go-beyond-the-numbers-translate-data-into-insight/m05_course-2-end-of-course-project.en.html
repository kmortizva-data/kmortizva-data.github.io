Context

What this module solves

The four previous modules taught how to explore, clean and tell. This is the delivery, and its job is the one almost never taught: saying what a file does not yet allow you to do.

They are three company scenarios, the same three that reappear in the following courses with more ambitious questions. Here the question is the first of all: which parts of this data can be trusted?

All three are done, so this module does not explain how they would be done: it links to what came out.

Waze: the defect no automatic rule flags

The file passes every routine check. No duplicates, no missing values outside the label, clean categories, and the outlier candidates by the interquartile range criterion are a perfectly normal 3 to 5%.

The problem appears when building a variable the file does not bring: kilometres per driving day. There, 1,994 users come out above 1,000 km in a single day and 461 above 24 hours at the wheel in a day, with a maximum of 172 hours.

No statistical rule would have flagged it, because it is not an extreme value, it is an impossible value, and to know that 172 hours do not fit in a day you have to know how many hours a day has. That is not in the data.

TikTok: the file points out its important variable on its own

501,029 mean views on the videos with a claim against 4,956 on the opinion ones, 101 times more, and the two distributions not overlapping on a logarithmic scale.

It is the variable that two courses later has to be predicted, and here it appears without having tested anything: just by looking at the distribution.

The other finding is one of shape: its 298 incomplete rows do not have loose gaps, they are missing seven columns at once. A gap has a shape, and its shape says where it comes from.

Automatidata: the question nobody had asked the file

As soon as the dates stop being text, the file answers something that was in no report: the peak hour is 19, with 1,454 trips, the slowest is 4, with 233, and 29.5% of the day happens between 17 and 21.

And it brings the opposite case to Waze's: a spike that looks like a defect and is not. The 514 trips of exactly $52.00 are the airport flat fare, and an automatic outlier filter would have deleted them for being expensive.

What the three have in common

In all three cases, what decided the result was knowing the domain, not applying a rule:

  • Knowing that a day has 24 hours.
  • Knowing that an airport has a flat fare.
  • Knowing that a negative fare is a refund and not a cheap trip.
Automatic rules flag candidates. Whoever knows the phenomenon decides.
Terms

Glossary

  • Data quality report. The deliverable of this course: what the file contains, what it lacks, what is impossible and which decisions remain pending.
  • Impossible value. One that cannot exist in the phenomenon measured. It is not the same as an outlier, and no statistical rule finds it.
  • Derived variable. One built from others, like the duration of a trip or the kilometres per day. It is where the inconsistencies between columns appear.
  • Shape of the gaps. Whether the missing values are loose cells or whole rows. It tells a measurement failure from a record that came in empty.
  • PACE strategy document. The written deliverable: what was planned, what was analysed and what is recommended.
  • Executive summary. One page for whoever is not going to read the analysis: finding, number with units, decision and limits.
Method

Step by step

What has to be done to deliver each project, in the order it was done.

Step 1. Always run the same six passes

Whatever the file. Shape and duplicates, missing values per column, types, cardinality of the categories, outlier candidates and impossible values. They are in projects/curso2/quality.py and they are identical for the three.

The first five are done by the code on its own. The sixth is declared by whoever knows the domain, and it is the one that found the Waze finding.

Step 2. Look at the shape of the gaps, not only count them

In TikTok, 298 gaps in seven columns could have been 2,086 cells spread around. They were 298 rows with all seven empty. Counting gives a number; looking at the shape gives a cause.

Step 3. Build the derived variables early

Because they are the ones that uncover the inconsistencies between columns. The trip duration in Automatidata and the kilometres per day in Waze do not exist in the files, and both revealed problems the original columns were hiding.

Step 4. Separate the impossible from the extreme

And treat them differently:

What it isWhat is done
OutlierA far-off value that may be realIt is investigated and decided: keep, clip or remove
ImpossibleA value the phenomenon does not admitIt is removed or corrected, no discussion

Step 5. Write what the file does not allow yet

It is the part that distinguishes an exploration report from a list of statistics. In Waze, the deliverable conclusion is not a number: it is a question for the team that generates the file and a recommendation not to use two columns until there is an answer.

Step 2. The quality pass, which is the same for the three

from quality import full_report

full_report(
    raw, results,
    numeric_expected=["sessions", "drives", "driven_km_drives"],
    outlier_columns=["sessions", "drives", "driven_km_drives"],
    impossible={
        "mas de 1000 km en un solo dia conducido":
            lambda d: d.km_per_driving_day.gt(1000),
        "mas de 24 horas al volante en un dia conducido":
            lambda d: (d.duration_minutes_drives / d.driving_days).gt(24 * 60),
    },
)
line by line
numeric_expected=[...]
The columns that should be numbers. If any arrives as text, the pass says so. The code cannot guess it: only you know what should be numeric.
outlier_columns=[...]
On which ones to apply the interquartile range rule. The candidates are counted, none is touched.
impossible={...}
A dictionary of name and rule. This is where domain knowledge comes in, and it is the only part of the pass that cannot be automated.
lambda d: ...
A short function written in one line. It receives the table and returns true wherever the rule is broken. It is stored without running and the pass calls it when its turn comes.
.gt(24 * 60)
"Greater than", written as a method. It is equivalent to > and reads better chained. The 24 times 60 is left unresolved on purpose: it says where the number comes from.

Traps

Watch out

  • The impossible is not found by a statistical rule. The interquartile range criterion gave normal results on the Waze file while it contained users with 172 hours of daily driving.
  • A file can pass every routine check and be broken. No duplicates, no missing values, correct types, and still inconsistent between columns.
  • Derived variables are where the inconsistencies appear. Build them early.
  • Count the shape of the gaps, not only how many there are. 298 rows with seven empty columns is not the same as 2,086 loose cells.
  • A spike is not always a defect. The 514 trips of 52 dollars are a fixed price, and an automatic filter would have deleted them.
  • The IQR rule flags nothing when the spread is already enormous. In the TikTok views its fence sits above the real maximum.
  • The deliverable includes what cannot be done yet. A well-posed question to the team that generates the data is worth more than another table.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

These three reports are the review of a lot before accepting it, and you do it with a criterion that is written in no procedure: you know what is possible.

When a certificate arrives declaring a recovery of 103%, you do not consult an outlier table. You know it cannot be, because you cannot recover more metal than went in. That is exactly the Waze case: 172 hours do not fit in a day, and no statistic is going to say it for you.

Review

Review

What is the difference between an outlier and an impossible value?

An outlier is a value far from the rest that may be real, and that is why the interquartile range rule only flags it as a candidate: it has to be investigated and a decision made between keeping it, clipping it or removing it. An impossible value is one the measured phenomenon does not admit, like 172 hours of driving within a day of 24, a negative fare or a recovery of 103%. There is no decision to make there, it is a defect. The distinction matters because no statistical rule finds the impossible: you have to know how what is being measured works.

Why does the Waze file pass every routine check and still be broken?

Because the routine checks look at the columns one at a time, and the defect is in the relationship between two of them. There are no duplicates, no missing values except in the label, the categories are clean and the outlier candidates hover around a perfectly normal 3 to 5%. The problem only appears when building a derived variable, kilometres per driving day, which the file does not bring: then 1,994 users come out above 1,000 km daily and 461 above 24 hours at the wheel in a day. The lesson is that derived variables have to be built early, because they are where the inconsistencies between columns show.

What is learned by looking at the shape of the gaps and not only counting them?

Where they come from. In TikTok there are 298 missing values in each of seven columns, which could be 2,086 cells scattered at random through the file. They are not: they are 298 rows missing all seven columns at once. That rules out a column-by-column measurement failure and points to a record created empty or an interrupted load, which is a different cause with a different solution. Counting gives a number; looking at the shape gives a hypothesis about the origin.

Why did the interquartile range rule flag no outlier in the TikTok views?

Because that rule measures the spread with the central half of the data, and in that column the central half is already enormous: the first quartile is at 4,942 and the third at 504,327, so the interquartile range is almost half a million and the upper fence goes to 1,253,404, above the real maximum of the file. In columns spanning several orders of magnitude, the rule has nothing to point at, and the criterion has to change: look at the drawing on a logarithmic scale or split the data into named bands.

What should the deliverable of an exploration project include, besides the statistics?

What the file does not allow yet, and what would be needed to be able to do it. In the Waze project the deliverable conclusion is not a figure but a specific question for the team that generates the file, about what exactly the driving-days column measures, plus the recommendation not to use two columns until there is an answer. A report that only lists means, minimums and maximums leaves the work half done: whoever receives it does not know what to trust or what to ask.
