Data visualizations and presentations

Four datasets with the same mean, the same deviation, the same correlation and the same line, and four shapes that look nothing alike. It is Anscombe's quartet, and it is the best reason there is to draw before summarising. Plus the life cycle of a chart and what can and cannot be done with Tableau.

Context

What this module solves

The three previous modules were about finding what is in the data. This one is about the part that decides whether anyone does anything with it: showing it.

And it starts with an argument that admits no discussion. Datasets can be built that match on every number one would calculate out of habit, and that on being drawn turn out to be completely different things. It is not a rare laboratory case: it is the demonstration that the summary and the drawing see different things, and that the drawing sees more.

Example

Before the theory: a toy example

Four sets of eleven points each, with their x and their y. They are called I, II, III and IV.

Step 1. The summaries of the four

SetMean of xMean of yDeviation of yCorrelationLine
I9.007.502.030.816y = 3.00 + 0.50 x
II9.007.502.030.816y = 3.00 + 0.50 x
III9.007.502.030.816y = 3.00 + 0.50 x
IV9.007.502.030.817y = 3.00 + 0.50 x

The four rows are the same row. Any report that summarises these sets with mean, deviation, correlation and fitted line will say the four are interchangeable.

Step 2. And now they get drawn

SetWhat it really is
IA cloud around the line. The only one where the summary does not lie
IIA perfect curve. The line is wrong on the high side in the middle and on the low side at the ends
IIIEleven points on a different line, and one wild one that bends the one that gets fitted
IVTen measurements at the same x and a single point that decides the line on its own

Step 3. What each case means

The last three are three different ways for a summary to lie, and all three occur in real data:

  • II is a curved relationship summarised with a line. The correlation of 0.816 sounds strong and the model will be systematically wrong at the ends, which is exactly where one usually wants to predict.
  • III is the outlier of the previous module. Without it, the ten remaining points sit almost on a perfect line with another slope. **One value out of eleven decides the result.**
  • IV is worse: without that single point on the right there would be no relationship at all to measure, because all the others share the same x. **The whole correlation rests on a single observation.**

And that is why the module starts here:

If you have not drawn it, you have not looked at it.
Terms

Glossary

  • Visualisation life cycle. That a chart gets planned, prototyped, reviewed and refined. The first one that comes out is almost never the one presented.
  • Exploratory chart. The one you make for yourself while analysing. Ugly, quick and many.
  • Explanatory chart. The one you make for whoever is going to decide. A single one, clean, with the finding written on top.
  • Tableau. A desktop tool for building charts and dashboards by dragging fields. It is the one this module teaches.
  • Tableau Public. The free version. It publishes on an open web profile, so nothing confidential gets uploaded.
  • Dimension. In Tableau, a categorical or date field: what slices the data.
  • Measure. A numeric field that gets aggregated: what is counted or summed.
  • Dashboard. Several visualisations together in one view.
  • Filter. Restricts which data enters the view.
  • Data storytelling. Telling the finding with structure: context, conflict and resolution.
  • Accessibility. That the chart is understood in black and white, with colour blindness and at a small size.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Draw before summarising, and several times

It is the conclusion of the quartet and it applies always, not only when there are suspicions. With a numeric variable, the minimum is two drawings: a histogram to see the shape and a scatter plot against whatever interests you, to see the relationship.

And module 2 already gave its warning: the histogram is drawn with several cuts, because the number of bars changes what it seems to say.

Step 2. Choose the chart according to the question

There are not many cases and almost all the mistakes come from this table:

The questionThe chart
How are the values of one variable spread?Histogram
How do two numeric variables relate?Scatter plot
How much does each category have?Bars
How does it evolve over time?Lines
How do the distributions of several groups compare?One histogram per group, with the same axes
How much is one part of the total?Bars, almost always better than a pie

The pie deserves its sentence: the eye compares lengths much better than angles, so with more than three categories or with similar values, bars read better.

Step 3. The rules that make a chart honest

RuleWhy
The axis of the bars starts at zeroCutting it exaggerates small differences
Same axes when comparing groupsDifferent scales make different things look the same
Logarithmic scale if the data spans orders of magnitudeOtherwise everything squashes against the axis
Axes are labelled with their unitsA number without a unit is not data
Colour cannot be the only carrier of meaningColour blindness and black and white
Write on the chart what to look atIf a separate paragraph is needed, it is not finished

The last one is the most skipped and the most rewarding. A title that says "Recovery by shift" describes the axes; one that says "The night shift recovers four points less" says the finding.

Step 4. The life cycle, which explains why the first one is never good enough

A chart gets planned, prototyped, reviewed and refined. In practice:

  1. Plan. Which question it answers and who is going to look at it.
  2. Prototype. Quick and ugly, to see whether the data says what you think.
  3. Review. Show it to someone who has not been in the analysis and see what they understand.
  4. Refine. Remove everything that does not help, and only then care about the looks.

Step 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that discovers that the chart you have spent two hours polishing is understood backwards.

Step 5. Tableau, and what is done with it

Tableau builds charts and dashboards by dragging fields, without writing code. The distinction that has to be understood is the one that organises the whole tool:

  • Dimensions slice the data: shift, mineral, month.
  • Measures get aggregated: recovery, tonnage, number of lots.

And a practical warning: Tableau Public publishes on an open web profile. It works for a portfolio and does not work for company data.

This part has to be done in Tableau, not here. It is a desktop tool and is learned by using it. What can be done in Python, and is what the projects of this course do, is the equivalent in charts: the same decisions, a different button.

Step 6. The datasets to download for practice

The module asks you to download its datasets and start building with them. They are the NOAA lightning strike records, with three columns that go straight to a map: the date, the coordinates and the number of strikes.

FileWhat it holdsSize

You already have them downloaded, in your course material folder. And they are big, which brings two practical consequences worth knowing before opening them:

  • In Tableau they can be used as they are, because it is built for that.
  • In Python it pays to read them once, aggregate and save the summary. A 300 MB file takes time to load every time you run the notebook, and what gets drawn at the end is a few hundred rows aggregated by month or by map cell.

That is exactly the path the project of this course follows: read once, aggregate, and draw from a summary of a few kilobytes.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

shared_x = [10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5]
quartet = pd.DataFrame({

    "y1": [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68],
    "x2": shared_x,
    "y2": [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74],
    "x3": shared_x,
    "y3": [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73],
    "x4": [8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8],
    "y4": [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89],
})

rows = []
for n in (1, 2, 3, 4):
    x, y = quartet[f"x{n}"], quartet[f"y{n}"]
    fit = stats.linregress(x, y)
    rows.append({"conjunto": n, "media x": x.mean(), "media y": round(y.mean(), 2),
                 "desv y": round(y.std(ddof=1), 2),
                 "correlacion": round(fit.rvalue, 3),
                 "pendiente": round(fit.slope, 3),
                 "intercepto": round(fit.intercept, 2)})

print(pd.DataFrame(rows).to_string(index=False))

An f-string to build the column name inside the loop: on the first pass it is "x1", on the second "x2". It is the way of walking through columns that are named alike with a number at the end.

The deviation of a sample, dividing by n − 1. Without ddof=1 numpy divides by n, which is the deviation of a population, and gives a different number.

Dictionaries get accumulated in a list and at the end get turned into a table in one go. It is faster and more readable than adding rows to a DataFrame one by one.

Step 2. Draw them, which is the only thing that tells them apart

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9, 7), sharex=True, sharey=True)

for ax, n in zip(axes.flat, (1, 2, 3, 4)):
    x, y = quartet[f"x{n}"], quartet[f"y{n}"]
    fit = stats.linregress(x, y)
    span = np.array([2, 20])
    ax.plot(span, fit.intercept + fit.slope * span, linewidth=1.6)
    ax.plot(x, y, "o", markersize=8)
    ax.set_title(f"Conjunto {n}", loc="left")

fig.savefig("anscombe.png", dpi=200, bbox_inches="tight")

Traps

Watch out

  • The four sets of the quartet have the same numbers. If your report rests only on mean, deviation and correlation, it does not tell a line from a curve.
  • A correlation of 0.816 does not mean the line works. In set II the relationship is curved and the line is systematically wrong at the ends.
  • A single point can decide the result. In III it bends the line and in IV it is the only reason a correlation exists.
  • When comparing groups, the same axes. Different scales make different things look the same, which is the most frequent design mistake.
  • The axis of a bar chart starts at zero. Cutting it turns a 2% difference into a mountain.
  • The first chart is not the one presented. Prototype, show it to someone from outside and refine.
  • Tableau Public is public. Company data does not get uploaded to an open profile.
  • A title that describes the axes wastes the best spot on the chart. The finding goes there.
  • Colour cannot be the only carrier of meaning, or the chart stops working in black and white and for whoever cannot tell red from green.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

Anscombe's quartet has an exact equivalent at the plant, and it is the reason nobody in process accepts an average without seeing the curve: the grade-recovery curve.

Two circuits can deliver the same mean recovery and the same mean concentrate grade, and have completely different curves. One can be operating on the flat part, where tightening the grade costs little, and the other on the drop, where every point of grade is paid with several of recovery. The average does not tell those two cases apart and decides opposite things.

In the quartetAt the plant
Set I, the cloud in a lineA stable circuit, where the average describes well
Set II, the curveThe drop zone of the grade-recovery curve
Set III, the wild pointThe campaign with one anomalous lot that drags the month
Set IV, a single xThe test with a single condition tried and one loose point

And you also recognise the life cycle of the chart: the chart that goes to the committee is not the one you made to understand it yourself. The working one carries every point, the conditions and the annotations by hand. The committee one carries a curve, the current operating point and an arrow saying which way to move.

What does not change between the two is the axis: if it starts at 80% instead of 0% so the improvement looks big, that is not design, it is something else.

Review

Review

What does Anscombe's quartet demonstrate and why does it matter?

That four datasets can coincide on every usual summary, mean of x of 9.00, mean of y of 7.50, deviation of 2.03, correlation of 0.816 and the same fitted line, and still have completely different shapes: a cloud in a line, a curve, a line with a wild point and a single x with one far-off point. It matters because it means that a report resting only on those numbers does not tell those four cases apart, and three of them demand different decisions. That is why you draw before summarising and not after.

Why is sharing the axes mandatory when comparing groups across several panels?

Because when each panel scales its axis to its own data, the heights stop being comparable: two groups of very different magnitudes get drawn equally tall and look the same, and two similar groups can look very different. The reader compares shapes and sizes without reading the axes, so an independent axis per panel is a silent way of misleading. With shared axes, what is seen corresponds to what is there.

What does it mean that the number of bars of a histogram is an analytical decision?

That the choice changes the conclusion, not only the looks. The same twelve recoveries with three bars give a flat split of [4, 4, 4], which tells nothing, and with six bars give [4, 0, 2, 2, 0, 4], where three groups separated by gaps appear. Few bars merge distinct groups and too many turn noise into peaks. The correct habit is to try several cuts before claiming anything about the shape, and if the drawing shows groups, to report them separately instead of summarising them with a mean that only describes a part.

What is the difference between an exploratory chart and an explanatory one?

The audience, and from there everything else. The exploratory one is for yourself during the analysis: many are made, quick and ugly, and they serve to see. The explanatory one is for whoever is going to decide: it is a single one, clean, with nothing that does not help, and it carries the finding written in the title instead of a description of the axes. Confusing them produces the two typical mistakes, which are wasting hours polishing a working chart or presenting a working chart in a meeting.

What has to be kept in mind before publishing on Tableau Public?

That it is genuinely public: what gets uploaded stays on an open web profile accessible to anyone. It works very well for a portfolio, which is exactly what the certificate uses it for, and it does not work for company data, personal data or anything under a confidentiality agreement. And since publishing is the final step, the check of what the file contains, including the fields that are not shown but travel with it, is done before uploading and not after.
