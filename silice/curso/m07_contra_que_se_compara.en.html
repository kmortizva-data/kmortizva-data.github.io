Context

What this module solves

Module 5 left a question open on purpose. Its best variable was the previous hour's silica, and its toy example already warned that this column alone predicts fairly well.

Here it is answered with two tools. The first measures what the model holds on to. The second puts the right rival in front of it.

Both point to the same place, and the result forces a rereading of modules 5 and 6.

By hand

Before the theory: a toy example

The same six hours as module 5, predicting from 2 to 6.

Hour123456
% SiO₂123456

Step 1. How much variation there is to explain

The mean of hours 2 to 6 is 4.

total variation = (2−4)² + (3−4)² + (4−4)² + (5−4)² + (6−4)²
                =   4    +   1    +   0    +   1    +   4
                = 10

R² is built by comparing a model's error against that 10.

Step 2. A model that looks very good

A model predicts 1.5 · 2.5 · 3.5 · 4.5 · 5.0.

actual:     2      3      4      5      6
predicted: 1.5    2.5    3.5    4.5    5.0
miss:     −0.5   −0.5   −0.5   −0.5   −1.0
error =   0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 1.00 = 2
R² = 1 − (2 / 10) = 1 − 0.2 = 0.80

R² 0.80. A result that any report would present as a success.

Step 3. What a rule without a model achieves

Repeating the previous hour, as in module 5.

actual:     2      3      4      5      6
predicted:  1      2      3      4      5
miss:      +1     +1     +1     +1     +1
error =    1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5
R² = 1 − (5 / 10) = 1 − 0.5 = 0.50

Step 4. Share out the credit

the model explains              0.80
persistence already explained   0.50
                                ----
the model's own contribution    0.30

Of that 0.80, two thirds were already there for free. The model adds 0.30, not 0.80.

Step 5. And how to know what the model holds on to

Shuffle one column at random, score again, and see how much it fell. If destroying a column sinks the model, that column was holding it up.

Terms

Glossary

9 terms, in plain words
  • Persistence baseline. Predicting that the next value will equal the last one measured. No model and no cost.
  • The model's contribution. The difference between what the model scores and what the right baseline scores. It is the only thing that can be credited to it.
  • Permutation importance. Shuffling a column and measuring how much the model worsens. Done on test data and with the model already trained.
  • Impurity importance. The one from module 2, which comes out of the tree's splits. It is biased towards variables with many distinct values.
  • SHAP. A method that shares each specific prediction out among the variables that pushed it. Short for SHapley Additive exPlanations.
  • Local explanation. Why the model said what it said in one specific case.
  • Global explanation. What the model holds on to in general, adding up many cases.
  • Waterfall plot. The SHAP figure that shows one case, stacking the contributions.
  • Beeswarm plot. The SHAP figure that shows many cases at once, one row per variable.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Measure importance again, better

The importance of module 2 came out of how the tree was built. It serves, but it is biased.

Permutation importance is more direct. It takes the model already trained, wrecks one column by shuffling it, and measures how much it worsens. What it measures is how much the model depends on that column.

Step 2. Read the profile of dependencies

If the importance is spread over many columns, the model combines information. If a single one takes almost all of it, the model is that column with decorations.

Step 3. Put the right rival in front

Three competitors, not two:

  • always predicting the mean, which is the rival of module 2,
  • repeating the last assay, which is persistence,
  • the full model.

Step 4. Subtract, do not compare

The number that matters is not the model's R². It is the difference against the right baseline.

Step 5. Open up one specific prediction

Global importance says what the model depends on, on average. SHAP goes down to the case: why this specific alarm, at this specific hour.

Result

The result, measured

What we expected. That the model would combine memory and process, and that the contribution over persistence would be modest but clear, perhaps 0.05 or 0.10.

What came out.

What it means. The 0.641 of module 5 was real, and almost all of it was inertia. The plant changes slowly, so the value of an hour ago is an excellent prediction on its own.

Nine thousandths is the price of the whole project: the cleaning, the built variables, the boosting and the tuning. None of it buys a model worth deploying.

Why this is not a failure

A well-measured negative result is a result. And this one is measured three times: by permutation, by direct comparison and by SHAP.

What is demonstrated here is that the inertia of the process already contains almost all the predictable information one hour ahead. That is a finding about the plant, not about the model.

What this module leaves open

One way out remains. At one hour, persistence is unbeatable because the process has barely moved. At longer horizons that advantage has to disappear.

If machine learning adds anything, it has to be there. Module 8 repeats the measurement at 1, 3, 6 and 12 hours, and also changes the question: predicting the change instead of the level.

Traps

Watch out

  • A high R² is not merit until the baseline is subtracted. Here 0.641 becomes +0.009.
  • The baseline is chosen before seeing the result. If it is chosen afterwards, the one that flatters the model most gets chosen.
  • Impurity importance and permutation importance do not measure the same thing. The first comes from how the tree was built. The second, from how much the finished model depends on it.
  • Permutation is done on test data. On training it measures memorisation.
  • SHAP explains the model, never the process. If the model learned a correlation inverted by the control loop, SHAP will explain it with perfect composure.
  • A single variable with dominant importance is an alarm. It almost always means leakage, or that the variable is the target in disguise.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The right baseline is the blank of a test. Nobody reports the recovery of a new reagent without running a test without reagent in parallel. If the blank already gives 90% of what the reagent gives, the reagent contributes the remaining 10%, not the 100% the meter shows.

Persistence is the blank test of this project. It scores 0.632 while doing nothing.

Permutation importance is a sensitivity test in the plant. One variable is disturbed and you see how much the result moves. If disconnecting a sensor changes nothing, that sensor was not governing the process.

And the result has a very concrete operating reading. That the grade of an hour ago predicts the grade now means that the circuit has a long residence time compared with the laboratory's frequency. That is real information about the plant, and no model gives it: the baseline does.

Review

Review

The model gives R² 0.641 and persistence 0.632. How do you sum that up in one sentence?

The model adds nine thousandths over repeating the laboratory's last assay.

That is the number to give, not the 0.641. A model's R² is measured against predicting the mean, which here is a rival with no information at all. Persistence does use information the plant already has, so it is the honest rival. All the work of cleaning, built variables and boosting buys +0.009.

Shuffling one column sinks the R² by 0.818, when the whole model is worth 0.641. How can that be?

Because R² can go below zero, and that is where the model falls without that column.

Permutation importance measures the difference between the R² with the column intact and the R² with the column shuffled. If with it the score is 0.641 and without it it falls to −0.177, the drop is 0.818. There is no cap at 1: the drop can exceed the original R² when the wrecked model predicts worse than the mean.

What exactly does SHAP explain, and what does it not?

It explains the model. It does not explain the process.

SHAP shares a specific prediction out among the variables that pushed it, and it does so faithfully: it says where that number came from. What it cannot say is whether that relationship is causal. In this project the amine appears with its sign inverted with respect to the chemistry, because the operator doses more when the silica rises. SHAP will reproduce that inverted relationship without blinking, because it is the one the model learned. Confusing a SHAP explanation with a physical explanation is the most common mistake in using it.

The project spent five modules celebrating improvements. Were they wrong?

They were not wrong, they were wrongly compared. The measurements were correct and still are.

Module 2 measured its 0.04 well, module 4 measured its −0.126 well and module 5 measured its 0.641 well. What was missing was the right rival in the same table. As soon as it appears, the 0.641 breaks down into 0.632 of inertia and 0.009 of model. The practical lesson is that the baseline is defined at the start of the project, not at the end, because choosing it at the end means choosing the one that flatters the work done.

If persistence wins at one hour, where could machine learning contribute?

At longer horizons, and by predicting the change instead of the level.

Persistence wins at one hour because the process has barely moved in that time. The further ahead you look, the more that advantage degrades, and that is where a model with process information could contribute something of its own. The other route is to change the question: instead of predicting how much silica there will be, predict how much it is going to rise or fall. Persistence, by definition, always predicts zero change. Module 8 measures both ideas.
