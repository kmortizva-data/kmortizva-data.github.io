Terms

Glossary

9 terms, in plain words
  • Neuron. A weighted sum of inputs, plus a bias, passed through a function.
  • Weight. The number that multiplies each input. It is what the network learns.
  • Bias. A number added at the end. It shifts the result. In Spanish, sesgo.
  • Activation function. The one that decides whether the neuron switches on. ReLU lets the positive through and turns the negative into zero.
  • Hidden layer. A group of neurons between the input and the output.
  • Epoch. One full pass over all the training data.
  • Drift. The model's performance worsening over time because the process changed.
  • Retraining threshold. The level of error above which training has to be done again.
  • Monitoring. Measuring the model's error in production, continuously.
By hand

Before the theory: a toy example

A tiny network: two inputs, two hidden neurons and one output. All the weights already fixed.

inputs:    x1 = scaled air         x2 = scaled amine
neuron A:  weights 0.5 and −1.0    bias +1.0
neuron B:  weights 1.0 and  0.5    bias −2.0
output:    weights 2.0 and  2.0    bias +0.5

Step 1. An hour with high amine

Inputs: x1 = 2, x2 = 3.

neuron A: 0.5·2 + (−1.0)·3 + 1.0 = 1 − 3 + 1 = −1   ->  ReLU(−1) = 0
neuron B: 1.0·2 + 0.5·3 + (−2.0) = 2 + 1.5 − 2 = 1.5 ->  ReLU(1.5) = 1.5
output:   2.0·0 + 2.0·1.5 + 0.5 = 0 + 3 + 0.5 = 3.5

Neuron A is switched off. Its sum came out negative and ReLU set it to zero, so it takes no part in the answer.

Step 2. An hour with high air and little amine

Inputs: x1 = 4, x2 = 0.

neuron A: 0.5·4 + (−1.0)·0 + 1.0 = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3   ->  ReLU(3) = 3
neuron B: 1.0·4 + 0.5·0 + (−2.0) = 4 + 0 − 2 = 2   ->  ReLU(2) = 2
output:   2.0·3 + 2.0·2 + 0.5 = 6 + 4 + 0.5 = 10.5

Now both neurons are switched on and the answer is 10.5.

Step 3. What just happened

The whole network is weighted sums and a cut at zero. There is nothing else.

What gives it power is step 1: depending on the input, some neurons switch on and others do not. With many neurons, the network keeps carving up the space and fitting a different behaviour in each piece.

And the weights are not set by hand. They are learned with the gradient descent of module 3, the same one that took w from 0 to 1, then 1.5, then 1.75.

The threshold is 0.661, 30% above the 0.509 of training. September crosses it clearly, and that is where an operations team would have to retrain.

March is above it too, and that is the interesting part. March is part of the training set and even so the model predicts it badly. Module 9 gave the explanation: March is 100% state 1, and that state disappears in May. The model, trained on a mixture, describes neither regime well.

The GPU

The GPU multiplies matrices 32 times faster, from 541 to 17 milliseconds. The R² of the model trained on it is 0.190.

It speeds up, it does not improve. That is the practical lesson: the GPU buys time, not quality. And for 4,091 rows of tabular data, it does not even buy time that is needed.

Declared discrepancy: the original note for this experiment reported 34 times and 580 milliseconds. Measured again, it comes out at 541 milliseconds and 32 times. The new measurement is reported.

The recommendation

Do not deploy machine learning to predict the SiO₂ of the concentrate with these data.

Held up by four measurements:

  1. The contribution over repeating the last assay is +0.009 at one hour.
  2. From five hours on nobody beats predicting the mean.
  3. The verdict withstood four attacks: retraining, moving window, per-regime models and reframing the problem as a warning.
  4. The cause is identified: the plant changed operating regime in May, and there is no stable relationship to learn.

What it would take to try again

  • A log of setpoints and operating changes. That is what the file lacks, and without it the May change is invisible to the model.
  • More frequent laboratory assays, or an online analyser. With the target measured every few minutes, persistence would stop being so strong.
  • At least a year of data, to have several campaign cycles instead of a single change.
  • An experiment with excitation, moving setpoints on purpose. It is the only way to separate cause from correlation in a controlled plant, and it is what module 14 is about.

Traps

Watch out

  • A neural network does not win for being modern. Here it comes last among the ones that use memory, with 0.165 against the boosting's 0.641.
  • Networks need a lot of data. 4,091 rows is very little for 25 inputs and two layers.
  • The GPU speeds up, it does not improve. Same model, same result, 32 times less time.
  • The retraining threshold is declared at deployment, not when the alarm goes off.
  • A training month with high error is a signal, not noise. March has one, and it points to the change of regime.
  • Drift is measured in production, not assumed. Without monitoring nobody finds out that the model stopped being useful.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

The neural network is an empirical plant model with many parameters. It serves when there are thousands of recorded campaigns. With six months of a single circuit, fitting hundreds of weights is like fitting a six-constant model to three experimental points.

Drift is the decalibration of an online analyser. It does not fail all at once: it keeps drifting while nobody looks, which is why daily verification standards exist. The threshold of 0.661 is exactly that, a verification standard.

And the final recommendation is a test report with a negative result, which in metallurgy is delivered just like a positive one. If a test campaign shows that a new reagent does not improve recovery, that report saves the investment. Here it saves the deployment of a system that would have given worse warnings than looking at the last assay.

Review

Review

The neural network comes last. Does that mean networks are no use for processes?

No. It means they are no use for this dataset, and for a concrete reason: size.

There are 4,091 usable hours and 25 input variables. A two-layer network with 32 and 16 neurons has hundreds of weights to fit with those few thousand examples. Tree models need less data for the same job, which is why they keep winning on tabular data of this size. With years of history, or with sequence data treated as a sequence, the comparison could be different. Module 11 checks it.

March's error is 0.691 and March is in the training set. How is that explained?

With the finding of module 9. March is 100% operating state 1, and that state disappears in May.

The model was trained on March to July, that is, on a mixture of two very different regimes. The result is an average model that describes the central months reasonably well, June with 0.437, and the extremes badly. A high error inside the training set itself is not noise: it is the signal that the data are not homogeneous and that a single model cannot cover them.

How is the retraining threshold chosen?

With the training error plus a margin declared in advance, not by watching when it starts to fail.

Here the training RMSE is 0.509 and the chosen margin is 30%, so the threshold lands at 0.661. September crosses it with 0.763. The important part of the procedure is the order: the threshold is set at deployment, when nobody yet knows what is going to happen. Setting it after seeing the curve is choosing the number that justifies the decision already taken.

The GPU speeds things up 32 times. Is it worth it for this project?

No, and knowing that is also a result.

The GPU multiplies large matrices 32 times faster, from 541 to 17 milliseconds, and with it the model's R² is 0.190 instead of 0.165. The difference comes from the model, not from the hardware: training faster does not change what there is to learn. The GPU is justified when training is the bottleneck, which is the case with images, text or long sequences. With 4,091 tabular rows, the bottleneck is the information available.

Summarise the whole project in two minutes, as in an interview.

The brief was to predict the percentage of silica in the concentrate of an iron flotation plant, in real time, from process sensors. Six months of real data, 737,453 rows.

With honest temporal evaluation, the ceiling of every tabular model was R² 0.04. Building variables with the process's memory lifted it to 0.64, which looked like a success. The decisive step was measuring the right baseline: repeating the last laboratory assay already gave 0.632, so all of machine learning contributed nine thousandths. From five hours of lead time on, no method beats predicting the mean.

The verdict was attacked four ways, including retraining daily and reframing the problem as an out-of-specification warning, and none moved it. Unsupervised learning explained why: the plant changed operating regime in May and there is no stable relationship to learn.

The deliverable is a reasoned recommendation not to deploy, plus the list of what it would take for a second attempt to make sense. Defending a well-measured negative result is a different skill from obtaining a good one, and in a plant it saves more money.
