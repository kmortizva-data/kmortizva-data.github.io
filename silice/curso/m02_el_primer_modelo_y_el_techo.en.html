Context

What this module solves

Here the first model of the project is trained, and we learn how to grade it. A decision tree, which is the easiest model there is to read: a chain of yes-or-no questions.

The problem that haunts all of machine learning also shows up. If you let the tree grow, it memorises. It gets 100% of what it has already seen and is useless for anything new.

And out comes the result that marks the rest of the project. With honest temporal evaluation, the ceiling is at R² 0.04. It does not move by changing algorithm.

Step 1. The error before asking anything

Without a model, the best bet is the mean of them all.

mean = (5 + 4 + 6 + 1 + 0 + 2) / 6 = 18 / 6 = 3

The error is measured by adding up the square of each miss.

error = (5-3)² + (4-3)² + (6-3)² + (1-3)² + (0-3)² + (2-3)²
      =   4    +   1    +   9    +   4    +   9    +   1
      = 28

28. That is the starting point.

Step 2. Try a split

The tree tries splitting the batches into two groups. Split at air below 295.

left  (280, 285, 290) -> 5, 4, 6   mean 5   error = 0 + 1 + 1 = 2
right (300, 305, 310) -> 1, 0, 2   mean 1   error = 0 + 1 + 1 = 2
error after = 2 + 2 = 4

The gain is what has been cleaned up.

gain = 28 - 4 = 24

Step 3. Try another split, to compare

Split at air below 305.

left  (280, 285, 290, 300) -> 5, 4, 6, 1   mean 4   error = 1 + 0 + 4 + 9 = 14
right (305, 310)           -> 0, 2         mean 1   error = 1 + 1 = 2
error after = 14 + 2 = 16
gain = 28 - 16 = 12

24 against 12. The first split cleans up twice as much, so the tree keeps that one. That is all a tree does as it grows: it tries every possible split, computes the gain of each one and picks the largest. Then it repeats inside each piece.

Step 4. How a prediction is graded

Another example, smaller. Five batches that all came out at 2% SiO₂, and a model that predicted this:

Actual22222
Predicted12224
Miss−1000+2

Two ways of summing up those five misses:

MAE  = (1 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 2) / 5 = 3 / 5 = 0.6
MSE  = (1 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 4) / 5 = 5 / 5 = 1.0
RMSE = root of 1.0 = 1.0

MAE 0.6 and RMSE 1.0 on the same misses. The difference is the batch that missed by 2. The MAE counts it once. The RMSE squares it and gives it far more weight.

Step 5. What just happened

Both pieces of the module are in place. The tree picks its splits by lowering the squared error. And the RMSE punishes large misses more than small ones.

A practical rule follows. If the RMSE is much larger than the MAE, there are large misses hiding. In the real file the difference will be 1.114 against 0.845.

Terms

Glossary

13 terms, in plain words
  • Decision tree. A model made of chained yes-or-no questions. Each answer leads to another question, until a number comes out.
  • Root node. The first question, at the very top.
  • Leaf. An end of the tree. No more questions are asked there and the answer is given.
  • Depth. How many chained questions there are at most from the root to a leaf.
  • Hyperparameter. A knob you set before training and the model does not learn. The maximum depth is one.
  • MAE. Mean absolute error. The typical miss, in the units of the data.
  • RMSE. Root mean squared error. Like the MAE, but punishing large misses more.
  • R². What fraction of the target's variation the model explains. Zero means it adds nothing over predicting the mean. It can come out negative.
  • Baseline. The minimum rival that has to be beaten. Here, always predicting the mean.
  • Overfitting. The model learning the particulars of your specific data instead of the general rule. You recognise it because it scores far better on training than on test.
  • Validation curve. The training and test errors drawn against a knob. It shows where the sweet spot is.
  • Random forest. Many trees trained on different resamples. The result is averaged across all of them.
  • Feature importance. How much each column contributed to the model's splits. It is not a proof of cause.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Apply the decisions of module 1

Before training, the data have to be left as module 1 said. The 1,171 duplicate rows are removed. The gap and the frozen stretch are kept. The two feed columns are kept. And % Iron Concentrate, which was a leak, is excluded.

That leaves 21 input columns.

Step 2. Split by time, not at random

The cut goes on 1 August. Training from March to July, test in August and September.

This is not a detail. A random partition puts adjacent hours on both sides, and adjacent hours resemble each other. The model would pass by copying from its neighbour.

Step 3. Train a short tree and read it

Depth 3 on purpose. A tree of depth 3 has eight leaves at most, so it can be drawn and read aloud.

Reading it matters as much as training it. If the tree's first question makes no metallurgical sense, something is wrong with the data.

Step 4. Grade it against a baseline

A lone R² says nothing. It has to be compared with the minimum rival, which is always predicting the training mean.

Step 5. Look for the sweet spot of the depth

The same tree is trained with increasing depths. The training error and the test error are drawn on the same chart. There the overfitting is seen, instead of assumed.

Step 6. Test whether many trees fix what one could not

A random forest trains a hundred trees on different resamples and averages. If the problem were the variance of a single tree, the forest would notice.

Result

The result, measured

What we expected. That the short tree would be weak and that the forest of a hundred trees would clearly improve on it. That is what happens in almost every tutorial.

What came out. The three models tie.

What it means. When three models of very different complexity land in the same place, the limit is not in the model. It is in the problem. The ceiling of this project is R² 0.04, and it does not rise by changing algorithm.

The R² of 0.9 seen in tutorials with this same dataset come from splitting at random. That is leakage by temporal neighbourhood, and module 1 already explained why.

What this module leaves open

The baseline here is always predicting the mean. It is the weakest rival there is, because it ignores that the laboratory already measured the previous hour.

Beating that baseline by 2% does not say much. The question of what it should really be compared against stays open. Module 7 answers it with a number. And that number changes the reading of everything in between.

Traps

Watch out

  • Three models that tie are not three failures. They are a measurement of the problem. Changing algorithm when this happens is a waste of time.
  • The training R² is no use for deciding anything. At depth 30 it is almost 1 and the model is useless. Only the test one counts.
  • The depth is chosen by looking at data, not by default. Here the sweet spot is 2, which is far less than anyone would have set by eye.
  • Feature importance depends on the model. A deep forest shares out the importance differently. Here the pruned one was used, which is the one that generalises.
  • Importance is not cause. It says what the model held on to, not what drives the process.
  • A tree only gives as many distinct values as it has leaves. With depth 3 that is eight, for a target that is continuous.
  • Beware the weak baseline. Predicting the mean is easy to beat and proves almost nothing.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

A decision tree is a plant decision tree, of the kind already hanging in the control room. If the pH drops below 9.5, raise the lime. If the feed grade falls, adjust the dosing. Chained questions that end in an action.

Overfitting has its exact equivalent in calibration. A calibration curve fitted with a high-degree polynomial passes through every standard. And then it gets a new sample wildly wrong. It fitted the noise of the standards, not the relationship.

And the module's result reads like a laboratory flotation test. If three different reagent schemes give the same recovery, the limit is not in the scheme. It is in the liberation of the mineral. Changing reagent does not fix an insufficient grind.

Review

Review

Three different models give R² around 0.04. What do you conclude?

That the limit is set by the problem, not by the model. A tree of three questions, a tree pruned to two and a forest of a hundred trees have very different capacities. If all three land at 1.11 of RMSE, the difference between them is not what is holding the result back.

The practical conclusion is to stop trying algorithms. What has to be attacked is the information available: new variables, another prediction horizon, or another way of posing the question. That is what the following modules do.

The training error at depth 30 is 0.061 and the test error 1.543. Explain what happened.

The tree memorised. At depth 30 it can ask so many chained questions that it ends up isolating tiny groups of rows, almost one leaf per case.

On the data it has already seen it is almost perfect, which is why the training error drops to 0.061. But what it learned are the particulars of those specific rows, not the general rule. With new hours it fails more than a tree of two questions. That is why the only curve that matters is the test one.

Why is the partition by date and not at random?

Because adjacent hours resemble each other closely. With a random partition, the 14:00 hour can fall in training and the 15:00 hour in test.

The model then does not predict: it recognises an almost identical situation it has already seen. The R² rises to values of 0.9 and means nothing, because in the plant the future is never in the training set. The temporal partition reproduces the real situation, where the model has to speak about months that did not exist when it was trained.

The two feed columns come out with importance 0.000. Was keeping them a mistake?

No, it was the right way to decide it. In module 1 the correlation of those columns with the target was 0.08 and 0.07 in absolute value, so they were suspected of contributing little.

Suspecting is not measuring. Keeping them cost two columns out of 21 and let the model itself judge them. The forest left them in place 15 of 21 with zero importance, which confirms the suspicion with a second, independent method. Throwing them away in advance would have been an unverified decision.

The model improves on the baseline by 2%. Is that a good result?

It cannot be known yet, because that baseline is too weak. Always predicting the mean ignores information the plant already has: the laboratory measured the previous hour.

An honest rival would have to use that information. The right question is not whether the model beats the mean, but whether it beats repeating the last assay. This module leaves it open on purpose and module 7 answers it. The answer changes how the whole project is read.
