Context

What this module solves

Twelve modules giving a number, and all lose to repeating the last assay. That closes one question and opens another.

A plant does not only want a value. It wants to know how far to trust it. "The silica will be 2.4" is no use if the model is wrong half the time. "The silica will be between 1.6 and 3.4, and this interval is right 90 times out of 100" is actionable.

The honest question of the module: is a calibrated interval deployable, even if the point prediction is not?

By hand

Before the theory: a toy example

Step 1. The problem with a single number

A model says 2.4. The operator asks how far it can be off and there is no answer.

You can compute the deviation of the training errors and multiply it by 1.645, which is what corresponds to 90% in a bell curve. That assumes the errors follow a bell curve. It is almost never true.

Step 2. Learn the quantile, with the pinball loss

There is a way for the model to learn the 90th percentile directly: penalise the errors asymmetrically.

Five batches that came out at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. We look for a number that leaves 90% below it.

loss = weight · (actual − predicted)        when the actual is ABOVE
     = (1 − weight) · (predicted − actual)  when it is BELOW

With weight 0.9, falling short costs nine times more than overshooting. We try three values:

PredictionLeft aboveLeft belowTotal lossMean
31 + 2 = 3, times 0.92 + 1 = 3, times 0.12.7 + 0.3 = 3.00.60
41, times 0.93 + 2 + 1 = 6, times 0.10.9 + 0.6 = 1.50.30
5none4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10, times 0.10 + 1.0 = 1.00.20

The minimum is at 5, which is the high percentile of these five numbers. With weight 0.5 the minimum would fall at 3, the median. The asymmetric weight is what turns a loss into a percentile finder.

Step 3. Conforming, which is something else

Quantile regression learns a percentile, but nobody guarantees it gets the 90% right. Conformal prediction attacks the problem from the other side.

A block of data the model never saw is set aside, its errors are measured there, and they are sorted. Twenty calibration errors, already sorted:

0.1  0.1  0.2  0.2  0.3  0.3  0.4  0.4  0.5  0.5
0.6  0.6  0.7  0.7  0.8  0.8  0.9  0.9  1.5  2.0

To cover 90% you need the error that leaves 90% below it. Out of 20 values, that is the 18th.

the 18th sorted error = 0.9
interval = prediction ± 0.9

Step 4. Check that it covers

Of the 20 calibration errors, 18 are less than or equal to 0.9. That is exactly 90%.

coverage = 18 / 20 = 0.90

And here is the important part: nothing has been assumed about the shape of the errors. No bell curve, no symmetry. They have only been sorted and counted.

Step 5. What just happened

The last two errors were 1.5 and 2.0, far above the rest. A bell curve fitted to those 20 numbers would give a deviation inflated by those two, and an interval of the wrong width.

The conformal method is unmoved: it counts positions. That is why its guarantee holds with any model and with any error distribution, which is what makes it genuinely useful.

Terms

Glossary

11 terms, in plain words
  • Point prediction. A single number as the answer.
  • Prediction interval. A range that should contain the real value with a declared probability.
  • Coverage. The fraction of times the interval really contains the real value.
  • Nominal level. The coverage that was requested. Here, 90%.
  • Falling short. Covering less than promised. Also called undercoverage. It is the serious failure.
  • Pinball loss. The asymmetric loss that makes a model learn a percentile.
  • Quantile regression. Training with that loss to predict a percentile instead of the mean.
  • Conformal prediction. Building the interval from the errors measured on a calibration block.
  • Calibration set. Data the model did not see when training and which are not the test data either.
  • Residual. The model's error in one specific case.
  • Distribution-free. The guarantee assuming no particular shape of the errors.
Method

Step by step

Step 1. Split in three, not in two

Training, calibration and test. The calibration comes from the last quarter of the training months, not from a random piece, so that its hours come after the training ones.

Step 2. Build the naive interval, to have something to compare against

The deviation of the training residuals times 1.645. It is what gets done out of habit and how much it fails has to be measured.

Step 3. Train two quantile models

One for the 5th percentile and another for the 95th. Between the two lies the central 90%.

Step 4. Conform

Measure the model's absolute errors on the calibration set, sort them, and take the one that leaves 90% below. That is the radius of the interval.

Step 5. Measure the real coverage

Count how many test hours fall inside. It is the only check that counts.

Step 6. Look at the width too

An interval that covers 100% by being the size of the whole range is no use. Coverage and width are reported together.

Code

The code, in parts

Step 1. The pinball loss, to understand what it optimises

def pinball(prediccion, valores, cuantil):
    diferencia = valores - prediccion
    return np.mean(np.maximum(cuantil * diferencia, (cuantil - 1) * diferencia))

Result

The result, measured

What we expected. That the three methods would hover around the requested 90%, with small differences in width.

What came out. Two of the three fall short, and by quite a bit.

What it means. Falling short is the serious failure of an interval, and it is the one the two most common methods commit.

An interval that promises 90% and covers 84.8% lies to whoever uses it. If an operator sets a safety margin believing he is wrong one time in ten, and in reality he is wrong one in seven, the decision is badly calibrated.

Quantile regression ends up even worse, with 83.1%, and that is surprising because its very objective was to learn the percentiles. Learning them on the training set does not guarantee getting them right on future data, above all when the plant changed regime.

The first thing in the project that delivers what it promises

Conformal prediction delivers the 92.0% it promised to cover at 90%.

It is an important result after twelve modules of negative results. And it is neither chance nor luck: the guarantee is mathematical and assumes nothing about the shape of the errors. It only asks that the calibration resemble the test.

It is the first time in the whole project that machine learning wins a clean comparison against persistence. Not in the point prediction, but in the width of the interval at valid coverage.

Honesty about the width

The target runs from 0.60 to 5.53 in the test period, a range of 4.93. The conformal interval measures 2.434, which is half of that range.

An interval like that says little. "Between 1.2 and 3.6" covers everything from excellent concentrate to out of specification. It delivers what it promises and its operating value is limited.

Both things are true at once, and both have to be said.

What this module leaves open

Thirteen modules measuring what can be predicted, and the result is always the same: very little. It remains to explain why the process data do not inform, beyond noting that they do not.

The answer has a name and it is not from machine learning. The plant operates in closed loop, and a control loop erases precisely the variation a model would learn from. Module 14 deals with it.

Traps

Watch out

  • Falling short is the serious failure. An interval that covers less than it promises is worse than giving no interval.
  • 1.645 sigma assumes a bell curve. A model's errors on plant data almost never follow one.
  • Learning a quantile does not guarantee covering it. Here quantile regression ends at 83.1%.
  • The calibration cannot be the test set. If it were, the guarantee would evaporate.
  • The calibration is taken from the end of the training set, not at random. In time series, a random piece is a leak.
  • Coverage and width are reported together. Covering 100% with the whole range is not an achievement.
  • The conformal guarantee assumes exchangeability. If the plant changes regime between calibration and use, it degrades.
Analogy

Metallurgical bridge

A prediction interval is the declared error of a laboratory assay. A report that says "62.4% Fe" and nothing more is an incomplete report. One that says "62.4 ± 0.3" lets you decide whether the difference from the previous batch is real or measurement noise.

Conformal prediction is the certified reference material. Instead of assuming the analyser behaves like a bell curve, it is given known standards, its real errors are written down and the declared uncertainty comes from there. Nothing is assumed: it is measured on cases the instrument did not use to calibrate itself.

And the conclusion about the width has its exact equivalent. An assay with an uncertainty of half the variable's range is honest and is no use for controlling the process. Knowing that your method has that precision is valuable, because it stops you taking decisions it cannot support.

Review

Review

Why can an interval be useful even if the point prediction is bad?

Because it answers a different, actionable question: how far to trust it.

A bad point prediction forces you to ignore the model. An interval with guaranteed coverage lets you decide with the right margin, even if it is wide. If the system says the silica will be between 1.2 and 3.6 with 90% confidence, the operator knows he cannot rule out going out of specification and acts accordingly. The value is in the guarantee, not in the precision.

The normal interval covers 84.8% when it asked for 90%. What went wrong?

The assumption that the errors follow a bell curve.

The method multiplies the deviation of the residuals by 1.645 because that is the 90% factor in a normal distribution. This model's errors are not normal: they have a tail, because in the months of another operating regime the model fails far more. A deviation computed on a mixture of small errors and large errors describes neither well, and the interval comes out too narrow.

What exactly does conformal prediction guarantee, and what does it ask in return?

It guarantees that the coverage will be at least the one requested, assuming nothing about the shape of the errors.

It achieves it by measuring the model's errors on a calibration block it never saw, and using the empirical percentile of those errors as the radius of the interval. What it asks in return is exchangeability: that the calibration data and the data in use come from the same population. It is a real condition and here it is met only halfway, because the plant changed regime. Even so it delivers 92.0%.

Quantile regression learns the percentiles and covers less than the naive method. How?

Because it learns the percentiles of the training set, not those of the future.

The model minimises the pinball loss over the months from March to July, so its percentiles describe those months well. In August and September the plant operates in another regime, with different errors, and those percentiles fall short. The conformal method sidesteps the problem because it does not learn the percentile inside the model, but measures it afterwards, on data set aside on purpose.

The conformal interval measures 2.434 on a range of 4.93. Is that a good result or not?

Both, and they have to be said together.

It is good as a method. It delivers the promised coverage, which none of the others do, and its interval is 9% narrower than persistence's. That is the first clean comparison machine learning wins in the whole project. It is bad as a product: an interval of half the variable's range runs from excellent concentrate to out of specification, so it does not guide an operating decision. A correct method on uninformative data gives a correct and uninformative result.
